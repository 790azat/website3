Hardwood Flooring Installation: Solid vs. Engineered Wood Options

Hardwood flooring can add durability, warmth, and resale appeal to a home, but choosing the right product is only the first decision. Homeowners also need to consider subfloor conditions, moisture, installation method, room location, plank dimensions, finishing requirements, and long-term maintenance.

The two primary hardwood categories are solid hardwood and engineered hardwood. Both use real wood as the visible surface, but their construction is different.

Solid hardwood is milled from a single piece of wood. Engineered hardwood uses a real-wood wear layer over a multi-layer wood-based core designed for greater dimensional stability.

Neither is automatically the right choice for every home. The best option depends on the room, subfloor, climate, installation conditions, and how long you expect the flooring to remain in the property.

Solid vs. Engineered Hardwood at a Glance

Feature Solid Hardwood Engineered Hardwood
Construction Single piece of wood Real wood veneer/wear layer over layered core
Typical thickness Often 3/4 inch Commonly thinner, varies by product
Moisture stability More susceptible to expansion/contraction Generally more dimensionally stable
Refinishing Multiple times possible depending on thickness Depends on wear-layer thickness
Installation Nail/staple, sometimes glue Nail/staple, glue, or floating depending on product
Below-grade installation Generally not recommended Often suitable with an appropriate product
Lifespan Potentially very long Potentially very long
Maintenance Moderate Moderate
Appearance Authentic solid-wood construction Real wood surface with more construction flexibility

Manufacturer specifications vary, so homeowners should always check the installation requirements for the specific flooring selected.

What Is Solid Hardwood Flooring?

Solid hardwood flooring is manufactured from a single piece of wood.

Common species include:

  • Oak
  • Maple
  • Hickory
  • Ash
  • Walnut
  • Cherry

Because the entire plank is wood, solid flooring can often be sanded and refinished multiple times over its service life.

This is one of its biggest advantages.

A properly maintained solid hardwood floor can remain in service for decades and can potentially be refinished several times when the surface becomes worn.

Advantages of Solid Hardwood

Solid hardwood appeals to homeowners who prioritize longevity and traditional construction.

Benefits include:

  • Long service life
  • Multiple refinishing opportunities
  • Extensive species and stain choices
  • Traditional appearance
  • Potentially strong resale appeal
  • Ability to repair or replace individual boards

The thickness of the flooring is important when considering future refinishing. A thick floor generally provides more material that can potentially be sanded over its lifetime.

Limitations of Solid Hardwood

The major consideration is dimensional movement.

Wood naturally responds to changes in moisture content. Solid hardwood can expand when humidity increases and contract when conditions become drier.

If installation conditions are poor or the flooring is installed before the home's environment has stabilized, homeowners may encounter:

  • Gaps
  • Cupping
  • Crowning
  • Buckling
  • Squeaking

The National Wood Flooring Association emphasizes moisture testing and acclimation procedures because moisture differences between flooring, subfloor, and the surrounding environment can contribute to flooring problems.

What Is Engineered Hardwood?

Engineered hardwood has a genuine wood surface layer attached to a constructed core.

The core is generally made from multiple layers of wood or wood-based material arranged to improve dimensional stability.

The top layer is real wood, so engineered flooring can provide the appearance of solid hardwood while offering greater flexibility in certain installation environments.

Advantages of Engineered Hardwood

Engineered hardwood can be particularly useful where moisture and dimensional stability are concerns.

Potential benefits include:

  • Better dimensional stability
  • Wider range of installation environments
  • Suitable products for some below-grade applications
  • Multiple installation methods
  • Wide-plank availability
  • Real wood surface
  • Less seasonal movement than many solid-wood products

The National Wood Flooring Association notes that engineered wood flooring can be installed in areas where solid hardwood may not be appropriate, depending on the product and installation conditions.

Engineered Hardwood Is Not Waterproof

A common misconception is that engineered wood can be treated like waterproof flooring.

It cannot.

Engineered hardwood is still a wood flooring product, and prolonged exposure to water can damage the finish, wear layer, or core.

Do not install engineered hardwood in a location simply because its construction is more moisture-stable.

Bathrooms, laundry rooms, basements, and other moisture-prone spaces require careful product selection and strict adherence to manufacturer installation requirements.

How Much Does Hardwood Flooring Installation Cost?

Hardwood installation costs vary significantly based on species, flooring construction, room size, subfloor preparation, labor, removal of existing flooring, and installation method.

Current national estimates generally put professionally installed hardwood flooring at approximately $6 to $12 per square foot, although premium species, complex layouts, extensive preparation, and high-end products can push the cost considerably higher.

A basic project budget should account for:

  • Flooring
  • Installation labor
  • Subfloor preparation
  • Moisture testing
  • Old-floor removal
  • Disposal
  • Underlayment, if applicable
  • Adhesive or fasteners
  • Trim and transitions
  • Staining or finishing
  • Furniture moving
  • Repairs to damaged subfloor

The cheapest flooring quote may not include all of these items.

Installation Method: Nail-Down

Traditional solid hardwood is commonly installed by nailing or stapling planks to a suitable wood subfloor.

This method provides a mechanically secured floor and is widely used for traditional hardwood installations.

The subfloor must be appropriate, sufficiently flat, and within the manufacturer's moisture requirements.

Glue-Down Installation

Some engineered hardwood products can be glued directly to an appropriate subfloor.

This can be useful over concrete when the specific product and adhesive system are approved for that application.

The concrete must be properly prepared, and moisture testing is particularly important.

Do not assume that any engineered hardwood can simply be glued to any concrete floor.

Floating Hardwood Floors

Certain engineered hardwood products can be installed as floating floors, where the flooring is not mechanically fastened directly to the subfloor.

Instead, the planks connect to one another through an approved locking or tongue-and-groove system.

A floating installation can reduce installation time, but the subfloor still needs to meet the manufacturer's requirements for flatness, cleanliness, moisture, and stability.

Moisture Testing Before Installation

Moisture is one of the most important factors in hardwood flooring performance.

Before installation, the installer should evaluate:

  • Flooring moisture content
  • Subfloor moisture
  • Room humidity
  • Temperature
  • Potential moisture sources

The NWFA emphasizes that wood flooring should be evaluated in relation to the moisture conditions of the building before installation.

Potential moisture problems include:

  • Concrete slabs with excessive moisture
  • Plumbing leaks
  • Foundation moisture
  • Poor exterior drainage
  • Crawl-space humidity
  • Recent drywall or concrete work
  • Inadequate HVAC conditioning

Installing hardwood over an unresolved moisture problem can create expensive flooring failures.

Should Hardwood Be Acclimated?

Whether flooring needs traditional acclimation depends on the specific product and manufacturer instructions.

The old assumption that every wood floor must simply be left inside the room for a fixed number of days is not universally correct.

Instead, installers should follow the flooring manufacturer's requirements and evaluate moisture conditions before installation.

The objective is to make sure the flooring and installation environment are appropriate for one another—not merely to satisfy an arbitrary number of days.

Solid vs. Engineered: Which Is Better for Basements?

For below-grade installations, engineered hardwood is generally more versatile.

Solid hardwood is commonly unsuitable for direct installation in many basement environments because of the greater moisture and humidity challenges associated with below-grade construction.

Engineered products specifically approved for below-grade installation can offer more options.

However, an engineered product does not eliminate the need for moisture control.

Before installing any wood flooring in a basement, address:

  • Foundation moisture
  • Concrete slab moisture
  • Drainage
  • Humidity
  • Plumbing leaks
  • HVAC conditions

If the basement has recurring moisture problems, solving the building-envelope issue should come before installing expensive hardwood.

What About Wide-Plank Flooring?

Wide planks can create a distinctive high-end appearance, but wider boards can also be more sensitive to dimensional movement.

Engineered hardwood can be attractive for homeowners who want wide-plank floors while seeking greater dimensional stability.

For solid hardwood, installation conditions and species selection become particularly important.

The installer should follow the flooring manufacturer's requirements for board width, fastening, expansion, and environmental conditions.

Choosing Wood Species

The species affects both appearance and durability.

Oak

Oak is one of the most widely used hardwood flooring species and offers a broad range of grain patterns and finishes.

Maple

Maple has a relatively subtle grain and is often chosen for clean, contemporary interiors.

Hickory

Hickory offers distinctive grain variation and is known for its hardness.

Walnut

Walnut provides a darker, richer appearance but is softer than some commonly used flooring species.

The best species is not necessarily the hardest. Homeowners should balance hardness, appearance, price, availability, and the style of the property.

Factory-Finished vs. Site-Finished Flooring

Homeowners also need to decide where the finish will be applied.

Factory-Finished

Factory-finished flooring arrives with its finish already applied.

Advantages can include:

  • Faster installation
  • Consistent factory coating
  • Less finishing work inside the home
  • Minimal drying time after installation

Site-Finished

Unfinished flooring is installed first and then sanded and finished at the property.

Advantages can include:

  • Greater customization
  • More control over stain color
  • A uniform finished surface
  • Ability to blend flooring with existing wood

The tradeoff is additional labor, dust, finishing time, and disruption.

Maintenance After Installation

Both solid and engineered hardwood benefit from routine maintenance.

Homeowners should:

  • Clean spills promptly
  • Use manufacturer-approved cleaning products
  • Avoid excessive water
  • Protect floors from furniture scratches
  • Use appropriate mats near exterior doors
  • Maintain reasonable indoor humidity
  • Keep pet nails trimmed
  • Follow the manufacturer's maintenance schedule

Avoid soaking hardwood flooring during cleaning. Excess water can penetrate joints and contribute to swelling or finish damage.

Common Hardwood Installation Mistakes

Several problems can turn an otherwise good flooring project into an expensive repair.

Installing Over an Unprepared Subfloor

Uneven or damaged subfloors can create movement, squeaks, and visible irregularities.

Ignoring Moisture

Moisture problems can cause serious flooring failures even when the installation itself appears professional.

Using the Wrong Installation Method

A flooring product designed for nail-down installation should not automatically be glued or floated.

Skipping Expansion Requirements

Wood needs room to respond to changes in environmental conditions. Expansion requirements should follow the manufacturer's specifications.

Choosing Based Only on Appearance

A beautiful flooring product may not be appropriate for the room's moisture conditions or subfloor.

Solid vs. Engineered Hardwood Decision Guide

If Your Priority Is... Consider
Maximum refinishing potential Solid hardwood
Traditional construction Solid hardwood
Historic appearance Solid hardwood
Below-grade installation Engineered hardwood
Greater dimensional stability Engineered hardwood
Wide-plank options Engineered hardwood or appropriate solid product
Concrete subfloor Often engineered, depending on product
Minimal maintenance Either, depending on finish
Long-term restoration potential Solid or thick-wear-layer engineered

These are general guidelines. The specific product's installation documentation should take precedence.

Questions to Ask a Flooring Contractor

Before signing a hardwood installation contract, ask:

  1. What type of subfloor do I have?
  2. Has the subfloor been moisture tested?
  3. What installation method will you use?
  4. Does the manufacturer approve that method?
  5. What expansion requirements apply?
  6. Will old flooring be removed?
  7. Is subfloor repair included?
  8. Are transitions and trim included?
  9. Who handles furniture moving?
  10. What happens if moisture levels are outside the manufacturer's limits?
  11. What warranty covers installation?
  12. What maintenance products does the manufacturer recommend?

A written scope of work should identify the flooring product, grade, species, finish, installation method, preparation work, and any allowances for unexpected subfloor repairs.

The Bottom Line

Both solid and engineered hardwood can provide a durable, attractive flooring surface when properly selected and installed.

Solid hardwood offers traditional construction and substantial refinishing potential, making it attractive for homeowners planning to keep a property for many years. Engineered hardwood provides a real wood surface with greater dimensional stability and can offer more flexibility over concrete and in certain below-grade applications.

The most important decision, however, is not simply choosing between two frame-like categories of wood flooring. It is matching the flooring construction to the room, subfloor, moisture conditions, installation method, and long-term maintenance plan.

Before installation, have the subfloor evaluated, verify moisture conditions, follow the flooring manufacturer's requirements, and make sure the contractor's quote includes preparation—not just the visible flooring material.

A properly installed hardwood floor can last for decades. The foundation for that lifespan is established before the first plank is fastened.
