Home Insulation Upgrades: Spray Foam vs. Fiberglass Batts

Insulation upgrades can improve indoor comfort, reduce drafts, and lower the amount of energy a home needs for heating and cooling. But choosing an insulation material is not simply a question of which product has the highest R-value.

Two common options are spray polyurethane foam and fiberglass batts. Spray foam can combine insulation with air sealing and can achieve high R-values in relatively limited space. Fiberglass batts are generally less expensive, widely available, and practical for accessible wall, floor, and ceiling cavities.

The better choice depends on the part of the house being insulated, available space, moisture conditions, air leakage, installation quality, and budget.

What Is R-Value?

R-value measures an insulation material's resistance to heat flow. In general, a higher R-value indicates greater resistance to heat transfer. However, the target R-value depends on the home's location and the specific part of the building envelope being insulated.

ENERGY STAR's current guidance, for example, recommends substantially different insulation levels for attics depending on climate zone. For existing wood-framed homes, recommended attic upgrades can range from approximately R-30 to R-60 or more depending on location and the amount of insulation already present.

That means homeowners should determine the appropriate target before comparing products.

Fiberglass Batts: Affordable and Familiar

Fiberglass batts are pre-cut or roll-form insulation products designed to fit between framing members.

ENERGY STAR identifies fiberglass batts and rolls as particularly suitable for unfinished walls, floors, ceilings, and other accessible cavities. The installation skill level is generally considered basic compared with sprayed foam systems.

Advantages of Fiberglass

Fiberglass batts are popular because they offer several practical benefits:

  • Lower material and installation costs
  • Easy availability
  • Straightforward installation in open stud bays
  • No chemical spraying process
  • Easy removal or replacement
  • Compatibility with many conventional wood-framed assemblies
  • Good performance when correctly sized and installed

The material itself is relatively inexpensive. Angi's July 2026 estimates put fiberglass insulation at approximately $0.30 to $1.50 per square foot, although the final installed cost depends on the project and labor.

Fiberglass can be particularly attractive when walls or ceilings are already open during remodeling.

The Main Weakness of Fiberglass: Air Leakage

Insulation and air sealing are related but not identical.

Fiberglass can slow heat transfer through a properly insulated cavity, but it does not automatically create a continuous air barrier around the house.

The Department of Energy recommends sealing cracks and penetrations around areas such as walls, ceilings, windows, doors, ducts, pipes, and other building-envelope interfaces.

This distinction matters because air can move around or through gaps in an otherwise insulated assembly.

A well-installed fiberglass system should therefore be combined with appropriate air sealing.

For example, before insulating an attic, the DOE recommends sealing penetrations between the conditioned house and attic. This can include openings around wiring, plumbing, ducts, recessed lights, and other penetrations.

Spray Foam: Insulation and Air Sealing Together

Spray polyurethane foam is applied as a liquid that expands and cures within the building assembly.

Its biggest advantage is that it can provide both thermal insulation and air sealing when properly designed and installed.

The DOE's Building Science Education guidance notes that spray foam can be used around difficult areas such as rim joists, floors above unconditioned spaces, and attics to insulate and air-seal simultaneously.

This can make spray foam particularly useful around irregular surfaces, penetrations, and areas where achieving a continuous air barrier with batts would be difficult.

Open-Cell vs. Closed-Cell Foam

There are two major types of spray polyurethane foam.

Open-cell foam is less dense and generally has a lower R-value per inch. DOE guidance places typical open-cell SPF around R-3.5 to R-3.8 per inch.

Closed-cell foam is denser and provides substantially more thermal resistance per inch. DOE guidance places closed-cell spray foam around R-7 to R-9 per inch, depending on the product. It is also more resistant to moisture and functions as a vapor retarder when installed appropriately.

The higher R-value per inch can be important when wall or roof cavities have limited depth.

How Much Does Spray Foam Cost?

Spray foam generally costs more than fiberglass.

Angi's September 2026 data puts professional spray foam installation at approximately $1,455 to $4,337, with an average of about $2,888. Whole-house projects can reach approximately $8,000 depending on the size and configuration of the property.

The type of foam also affects the price:

Insulation Approx. Cost
Fiberglass 0.30–1.50/sq. ft.
Open-cell spray foam 0.60–1.60 per board foot installed
Closed-cell spray foam 1.30–3.10 per board foot installed

Angi's 2026 estimates show that closed-cell foam generally costs more because it uses a denser material and provides greater resistance to moisture and heat flow per inch.

A direct price comparison can therefore be misleading. Spray foam is priced differently from fiberglass, and the amount of material needed to reach a particular performance target can vary.

Where Fiberglass Batts Make Sense

Fiberglass is often practical when the framing cavities are open, regular, and easy to access.

Good applications can include:

  • Unfinished exterior walls
  • Basement walls where appropriate
  • Floors
  • Ceilings
  • Attic assemblies designed for batt insulation
  • New construction
  • Major remodeling projects where drywall has already been removed

ENERGY STAR specifically lists batt insulation as appropriate for unfinished walls, floors, and ceilings.

It can also make financial sense when a homeowner has a large area to insulate and the existing assembly does not require the specialized air-sealing characteristics of spray foam.

Where Spray Foam Makes More Sense

Spray foam can be advantageous when:

  • Space is limited
  • Air leakage is a major problem
  • Framing is irregular
  • There are difficult-to-reach gaps
  • Rim joists need both insulation and air sealing
  • A roof assembly requires high R-value within limited depth
  • Moisture resistance is an important design consideration

DOE guidance notes that spray foam has a high R-value per inch and can be particularly useful at difficult attic perimeter areas.

However, this does not mean spray foam is automatically the best insulation for an entire house.

Installation Quality Matters More Than the Product Name

Poor installation can undermine either material.

Fiberglass batts can perform poorly if they are:

  • Compressed
  • Cut too short
  • Installed with gaps
  • Installed around obstacles without proper fitting
  • Compressed behind wiring or plumbing
  • Missing from portions of the cavity

DOE guidance emphasizes achieving sufficient R-value uniformly across an attic and avoiding gaps, voids, compression, and misalignment with air barriers.

Spray foam has its own installation risks. Incorrect mixing, application thickness, temperature, humidity, or curing conditions can affect the finished product.

EPA guidance recommends following manufacturer instructions, controlling the work area, using appropriate protective equipment, and ensuring adequate ventilation during professional spray-foam installation.

Homeowners should therefore be cautious about choosing a contractor solely because they offer the lowest spray-foam price.

Moisture and Ventilation Need to Be Considered

Insulation should be part of a complete building-envelope strategy.

Closed-cell spray foam has strong moisture-resistance characteristics, but insulation should not be used to conceal an existing roof leak, plumbing leak, or other moisture problem.

The DOE recommends considering insulation, air barriers, and vapor control together with the home's climate and assembly design.

Attics also need appropriate ventilation when the assembly is designed as a vented attic. DOE guidance emphasizes maintaining appropriate airflow paths from soffits toward ridge or other attic vents when insulating attic floors.

Can You Combine Fiberglass and Spray Foam?

Yes. An insulation system does not necessarily need to use one material everywhere.

DOE building guidance includes high-performance assemblies that combine spray foam with batt or other insulation materials.

For example, a contractor might use spray foam to air-seal difficult penetrations or rim joists while using fiberglass in larger, accessible cavities.

This hybrid approach can provide targeted air sealing without paying the cost of spraying every square foot of the building envelope.

Which Insulation Should You Choose?

Factor Spray Foam Fiberglass Batts
Initial cost Higher Lower
Air sealing Excellent when properly installed Requires separate air sealing

The right choice often depends more on the assembly than on the insulation material itself.

Final Considerations

Spray foam and fiberglass batts can both be effective insulation systems when they are correctly matched to the building assembly.

Fiberglass is usually the more budget-friendly option and can work extremely well in accessible, properly sealed cavities. Spray foam costs more but can provide higher R-value per inch and combine insulation with air sealing, making it particularly useful in difficult areas and assemblies with limited space.

Before choosing a material, determine the existing insulation level, identify air leaks and moisture problems, establish the appropriate R-value for the home's climate zone, and decide whether the project involves an attic, wall, crawlspace, basement, or roof assembly.

For many homes, the most cost-effective strategy is not simply choosing the most expensive insulation. It is air sealing first, reaching the appropriate R-value, and installing the insulation continuously and correctly. ENERGY STAR and DOE guidance both emphasize the importance of combining insulation with effective air sealing for a better-performing building envelope.
