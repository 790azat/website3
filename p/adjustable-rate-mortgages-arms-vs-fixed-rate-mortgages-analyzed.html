Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs) vs. Fixed-Rate Mortgages Analyzed

Choosing between an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) and a fixed-rate mortgage affects how predictable your housing costs will be, how your interest rate can change, and how much you may pay over the life of the loan.

A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the interest rate unchanged for the mortgage term, while an adjustable-rate mortgage can change after an initial fixed period according to the loan's index, margin, adjustment schedule, and rate caps.

ARMs often begin with a lower interest rate than comparable fixed-rate mortgages, which can reduce the initial principal-and-interest payment. However, the payment can increase after the initial period if the ARM's rate adjusts upward.

The right way to compare the two is to look beyond the starting rate and examine the complete loan structure, including future payment scenarios, adjustment limits, closing costs, and how long you expect to keep the mortgage.

How a Fixed-Rate Mortgage Works

A fixed-rate mortgage has an interest rate established when the loan is originated that does not change during the loan term.

For example, assume a borrower takes out a $400,000 30-year mortgage at a fixed 6.5% interest rate.

The monthly principal-and-interest payment would remain approximately $2,528 throughout the 30-year term, assuming the borrower does not refinance, pay off the loan early, or otherwise modify it.

The total mortgage payment can still change if property taxes, homeowners insurance, or mortgage insurance change. The fixed-rate feature applies to the mortgage's principal-and-interest payment, not necessarily every component of the homeowner's monthly housing expense.

How an Adjustable-Rate Mortgage Works

An ARM generally has two stages.

During the initial period, the interest rate is fixed.

After that period ends, the rate adjusts according to the terms of the mortgage.

Common ARM formats include:

  • 3/1 ARM
  • 5/1 ARM
  • 5/6 ARM
  • 7/1 ARM
  • 10/1 ARM

For a 5/1 ARM, the "5" generally means the initial interest rate is fixed for five years. The "1" indicates that the rate can then adjust annually. A 5/6 ARM generally has a five-year initial period followed by adjustments every six months.

The specific adjustment schedule should always be confirmed in the loan documents.

What Determines an ARM's Interest Rate?

Once the initial fixed period ends, an ARM's rate is generally determined using an index plus a margin, subject to the loan's adjustment caps.

The index reflects broader market interest rates.

The margin is a percentage set by the lender and specified in the loan agreement.

In simplified form:

Index + Margin = Fully Indexed Interest Rate

For example, suppose an ARM has:

Index: 4.0% Margin: 2.25%

The fully indexed rate would be:

4.0% + 2.25% = 6.25%

The actual rate can be constrained by the ARM's adjustment caps and other provisions.

The margin generally remains fixed after closing, while the index can change as market conditions change.

What Are ARM Rate Caps?

Rate caps limit how much an ARM's interest rate can change.

There are generally three types.

Initial Adjustment Cap

This limits the amount the rate can change during the first adjustment after the initial fixed period.

Subsequent Adjustment Cap

This limits the change permitted during later adjustment periods.

Lifetime Cap

This limits how far the interest rate can move above or below the initial rate over the life of the mortgage.

For example, an ARM described as having a 2/2/5 cap structure could have:

  • Maximum 2 percentage-point change at the first adjustment
  • Maximum 2 percentage-point change at subsequent adjustments
  • Maximum 5 percentage-point increase over the initial rate during the life of the loan

Actual loan terms vary, so the cap structure should be confirmed in the Loan Estimate and mortgage documents.

Fixed-Rate vs. ARM: Core Differences

Feature Fixed-Rate Mortgage Adjustable-Rate Mortgage
Initial rate Fixed Usually fixed initially
Rate after initial period Does not change Can change
Principal-and-interest payment Generally stable Can increase or decrease
Rate risk Lower Higher
Initial payment May be higher Often lower
Long-term predictability High Lower
Exposure to future rates Limited Significant after adjustment
Refinancing Optional May be considered if circumstances change

A fixed-rate mortgage provides greater payment predictability. An ARM generally trades some long-term certainty for the possibility of a lower initial rate.

Comparing the Initial Monthly Payment

Consider a hypothetical $400,000 mortgage.

Suppose a fixed-rate mortgage carries a 6.5% rate while a 5/1 ARM starts at 5.75%.

The approximate principal-and-interest payments would be:

30-year fixed at 6.5%: $2,528/month

30-year mortgage at 5.75%: $2,334/month

The ARM would initially reduce the principal-and-interest payment by approximately $194 per month.

That difference can be meaningful, particularly for a buyer with a large loan balance.

However, the comparison changes once the ARM begins adjusting.

If the ARM eventually resets to 7.5%, the payment on the remaining balance could become substantially higher.

The initial payment therefore represents only one stage of the loan.

What Happens If ARM Rates Fall?

An ARM can move downward as well as upward, depending on the loan's index, margin, caps, and other provisions.

If the relevant index declines, the fully indexed rate could decline at the next adjustment.

However, the payment does not necessarily fall by the same amount immediately in every ARM structure.

Some loans have rate floors, and adjustment caps can limit how quickly the rate changes. The loan documents determine how the adjustment works.

What Happens If ARM Rates Rise?

If the index increases, an ARM's rate can rise when the loan adjusts.

For example, a borrower might start with a 5.75% rate during the initial period and later face a 7.0% or 7.5% rate, subject to the loan's adjustment limits.

That can substantially increase the principal-and-interest payment.

This is the central difference between the two mortgage structures:

A fixed-rate borrower generally transfers interest-rate risk to the lender. An ARM borrower retains more exposure to future interest-rate movements.

Who Might Consider a Fixed-Rate Mortgage?

A fixed-rate mortgage can fit borrowers who prioritize predictable principal-and-interest payments.

It may be particularly relevant when:

  • You expect to keep the home for many years.
  • Your budget has limited room for payment increases.
  • You prefer predictable long-term housing costs.
  • You do not want to depend on refinancing.
  • You are purchasing during a period when you want to lock in a known rate.

The major advantage is predictability.

If market mortgage rates rise substantially after closing, the borrower's fixed rate does not change.

If rates later fall, the borrower can potentially refinance, subject to qualification, closing costs, and market conditions.

Who Might Consider an ARM?

An ARM can appeal to borrowers whose plans make the initial fixed period particularly relevant.

For example, someone expecting to move before the first adjustment might place more value on the lower initial rate.

ARMs can also appeal to borrowers who have enough financial capacity to handle potential payment increases and who understand the loan's adjustment rules.

The CFPB notes that an ARM may make sense for someone who expects to move within the initial fixed period, but warns borrowers not to assume that selling or refinancing will necessarily be possible before the rate changes.

The key issue is whether the borrower can afford the mortgage if the original plan changes.

The Importance of Your Expected Holding Period

How long you expect to own the property can significantly affect the comparison.

Suppose you expect to sell in four years.

A 5/1 ARM would still be within its initial fixed-rate period when you sell, assuming the transaction occurs as planned.

In that situation, the future adjustment may never affect you.

But suppose you remain in the property for 15 years.

The ARM could undergo numerous adjustments, making future rates and payments much less predictable.

This is why comparing an ARM and fixed-rate mortgage requires more than comparing today's rates.

You need to consider the period during which you expect to actually hold the mortgage.

Refinancing an ARM Into a Fixed Mortgage

Some borrowers take an ARM with the intention of refinancing into a fixed-rate mortgage before the initial period ends.

That can be a viable strategy in certain circumstances, but refinancing is not guaranteed.

Future conditions can include:

  • Higher mortgage rates
  • Lower property value
  • Changes in income
  • Higher debt
  • Different credit profile
  • Tighter lending standards
  • Insufficient home equity

The CFPB specifically advises borrowers not to assume that they will be able to refinance or sell before an ARM's rate changes.

A borrower considering an ARM should therefore be able to manage the loan under a higher-rate scenario rather than relying entirely on a future refinance.

ARM Payment Shock

The term payment shock describes a significant increase in the required mortgage payment.

Consider a simplified example:

Starting loan: $500,000 Initial ARM rate: 5.5% Initial principal-and-interest payment: approximately $2,839

If the rate later rises to 7.5%, the payment could increase substantially depending on the remaining balance and remaining amortization period.

Even if the interest rate increase appears relatively small in percentage-point terms, the dollar effect can be significant on a large mortgage.

The CFPB recommends asking the lender how high the interest rate and monthly payment could go under the loan's adjustment rules.

Comparing Total Interest Costs

The starting rate does not determine the ultimate cost of an ARM.

For example, an ARM could have a lower rate during the first five years but become more expensive if rates rise significantly afterward.

Conversely, if rates remain stable or decline, an ARM could produce lower interest costs during some periods.

A fixed-rate mortgage provides a known interest-rate structure, making long-term cost projections easier.

An ARM requires scenario analysis.

Consider calculating:

  • Payment at the initial rate
  • Payment after the first adjustment
  • Payment at the maximum permitted rate
  • Total interest over the expected holding period
  • Closing costs
  • Potential refinance costs

What to Compare on the Loan Estimate

When comparing mortgage offers, review more than the advertised interest rate.

For a fixed-rate mortgage, examine:

  • Interest rate
  • APR
  • Monthly principal and interest
  • Points
  • Origination charges
  • Closing costs
  • Loan term

For an ARM, additionally examine:

  • Initial rate
  • Initial fixed period
  • Adjustment frequency
  • Index
  • Margin
  • Initial adjustment cap
  • Subsequent adjustment cap
  • Lifetime cap
  • Rate floor, if applicable
  • Maximum possible payment
  • Prepayment provisions

The CFPB recommends reviewing these ARM terms carefully because different loans can have identical initial rates but substantially different adjustment structures.

Fixed-Rate Mortgage vs. ARM for a Long-Term Home

A borrower planning to remain in a home for decades may place greater importance on payment predictability.

With a fixed-rate mortgage, the principal-and-interest payment is generally unchanged for the life of the loan.

With an ARM, the borrower accepts the possibility of future rate adjustments.

That does not automatically make one structure financially preferable in every situation. The comparison depends on the initial rate, future rate environment, loan terms, fees, expected holding period, and borrower's ability to absorb payment changes.

Fixed-Rate Mortgage vs. ARM for a Shorter-Term Plan

If you expect to sell the home before an ARM's first adjustment, the initial fixed period can be particularly relevant.

For example, a borrower planning to move within four years might compare a 5/1 ARM with a 30-year fixed mortgage by looking at the expected cost during those four years.

However, the plan should account for the possibility that the move is delayed.

If the borrower ultimately stays for eight or ten years, the ARM may enter its adjustment period.

Questions to Ask Before Choosing an ARM

Before selecting an adjustable-rate mortgage, ask the lender:

  1. What is the initial interest rate?
  2. How long is the initial fixed period?
  3. What index is used?
  4. What is the margin?
  5. How often can the rate adjust?
  6. What is the first adjustment cap?
  7. What is the subsequent adjustment cap?
  8. What is the lifetime cap?
  9. Is there a rate floor?
  10. What is the highest possible monthly payment?
  11. Can the payment change at a different time than the interest rate?
  12. Can the loan balance ever increase?
  13. Is there a prepayment penalty?
  14. What would the payment be at several higher interest rates?

These questions can reveal risks that are not obvious from the initial advertised rate.

How to Compare the Two Mortgage Types

A practical comparison should include three scenarios.

Scenario 1: Rates Stay Similar

Calculate the projected ARM payment if rates remain around the current level after the initial fixed period.

Scenario 2: Rates Rise

Calculate the payment using the maximum or a substantially higher rate allowed under the loan's terms.

Scenario 3: You Sell Early

Calculate your estimated total mortgage cost through the date you expect to sell.

This approach gives you a more complete view than comparing the initial monthly payments.

Final Considerations

Fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages solve different financing needs.

A fixed-rate mortgage provides a stable interest rate and predictable principal-and-interest payment structure. An ARM generally starts with a fixed period and then adjusts according to an index and margin, subject to contractual caps.

The initial ARM rate can be lower than the rate on a comparable fixed mortgage, but that advantage comes with future interest-rate and payment risk. The CFPB recommends understanding the adjustment frequency, index, margin, caps, maximum payment, and affordability of the loan if rates rise.

For borrowers comparing the two, the key questions are:

How long will I keep the mortgage?

How much could my ARM payment increase?

Could I afford the maximum payment without refinancing or selling?

What are the total costs of each loan over my expected holding period?

A mortgage comparison based on those questions provides a more complete picture than simply choosing the loan with the lower initial interest rate.
