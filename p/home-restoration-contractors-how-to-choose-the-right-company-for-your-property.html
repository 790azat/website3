Home Restoration Contractors: How to Choose the Right Company for Your Property

Home restoration is different from ordinary remodeling. Restoration contractors are often called in after a fire, flood, major storm, burst pipe, mold problem, or other event has damaged a property. The work may involve emergency cleanup, structural repairs, drying, demolition, rebuilding, and coordination with insurance companies.

Because restoration projects can begin under stressful circumstances, homeowners may feel pressure to hire the first company available. That can create expensive problems later. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends researching contractors, comparing written bids, checking credentials, and understanding how insurance and mortgage funds will be distributed before work begins.

What Does a Home Restoration Contractor Do?

A restoration contractor can coordinate the process of returning a damaged property to a safe and usable condition.

Depending on the type of damage, services may include:

  • Emergency water extraction
  • Structural drying
  • Mold remediation
  • Smoke and fire damage cleanup
  • Storm and wind damage repairs
  • Roof and siding restoration
  • Drywall and insulation replacement
  • Flooring replacement
  • Electrical repairs
  • Plumbing repairs
  • HVAC repairs
  • Structural reconstruction
  • Interior remodeling after demolition
  • Debris removal
  • Final repairs and finishing

Not every company performs every service. A water-damage specialist may handle drying and remediation but subcontract reconstruction. A general contractor may coordinate the rebuilding while specialized companies handle mold, electrical, plumbing, or structural engineering.

Before hiring, determine exactly which parts of the restoration the company will perform.

Restoration vs. Remodeling

The distinction is important.

Remodeling generally involves planned improvements, such as replacing a kitchen, adding a bathroom, or changing a floor plan.

Restoration usually responds to existing damage and focuses first on making the property safe and stopping additional deterioration.

For example, after a major pipe leak, the sequence might be:

  1. Stop the water source.
  2. Assess the damage.
  3. Remove standing water.
  4. Dry affected materials.
  5. Remove materials that cannot be salvaged.
  6. Address mold or contamination where necessary.
  7. Repair structural components.
  8. Replace drywall, flooring, and finishes.
  9. Complete final inspections and repairs.

The order matters. Painting over wet or mold-contaminated materials, for example, does not solve the underlying problem. The EPA advises addressing moisture problems and cleaning/remediating mold before painting or sealing affected surfaces.

Determine What Kind of Restoration Specialist You Need

Different types of damage require different expertise.

Damage Type Contractor or Specialist to Consider
Water damage Water restoration contractor
Mold Mold remediation specialist
Fire and smoke Fire restoration company
Storm damage General/restoration contractor
Roof damage Roofing contractor
Structural damage General contractor + structural professional when required
Electrical damage Licensed electrician
Plumbing damage Licensed plumber
HVAC damage Qualified HVAC contractor

For substantial projects, one company may coordinate multiple trades.

For mold remediation, for example, the EPA recommends looking for contractors with relevant experience and checking references. Professional restoration organizations such as the IICRC also maintain directories of certified firms and technicians.

Check Licenses, Insurance, and Experience

Do not assume that a company advertising "restoration services" has every qualification required for the work.

Ask for:

  • Contractor license or registration
  • General liability insurance
  • Workers' compensation coverage
  • Relevant trade licenses
  • Restoration certifications where appropriate
  • References from comparable projects
  • Business address and contact information

Licensing requirements vary by state and locality. FEMA recommends verifying that contractors are properly licensed for the proposed work and checking whether permits are required before repairing or rebuilding a disaster-damaged property.

The FTC also recommends requesting identification, licenses, insurance documentation, and references before paying for restoration services.

If subcontractors will perform electrical, plumbing, roofing, or structural work, ask who they are and whether the required licensing and insurance apply to them as well.

Ask About Restoration-Specific Experience

A contractor who normally builds additions may not be the right choice for a heavily water-damaged home.

Ask:

  • How many similar restoration projects have you completed?
  • Have you handled insurance restoration before?
  • Which portions of the work will you perform directly?
  • Which trades will be subcontracted?
  • How do you document concealed damage?
  • How do you determine whether materials can be salvaged?
  • How will moisture be measured and documented?
  • Who manages permits?
  • Who will supervise the project?

EPA guidance specifically recommends checking the qualifications, training, and experience of professionals handling water damage and mold-related work.

Document the Damage Before Work Begins

If it is safe to do so, photograph and video the property before demolition or cleanup.

Document:

  • Each damaged room
  • Ceilings and walls
  • Flooring
  • Appliances
  • Cabinets
  • Exterior surfaces
  • Structural damage
  • Personal property
  • Water lines or sources of damage
  • Smoke or fire damage

Keep copies of photographs, estimates, invoices, inspection reports, and correspondence.

The CFPB recommends photographing or recording property damage before and after repairs because documentation can be important when dealing with an insurance claim.

Do not put yourself in danger simply to obtain photographs. If the building may be structurally unsafe or contaminated, wait for qualified professionals.

Contact Your Insurance Company Before Signing

If the restoration is related to an insured loss, contact the insurer before authorizing major work whenever circumstances allow.

Ask:

  • What damage is covered?
  • What deductible applies?
  • Has an adjuster inspected the property?
  • What documentation is required?
  • Are emergency mitigation expenses covered?
  • Can I select my own contractor?
  • How will payments be issued?
  • Does my mortgage servicer need to endorse insurance checks?

The CFPB notes that homeowners should contact their insurance company and mortgage servicer before hiring contractors to understand how and when insurance funds will be distributed.

Insurance coverage also depends on the policy. For example, standard homeowners insurance generally does not cover flood damage, which may require separate flood insurance.

Get a Detailed Restoration Estimate

A restoration estimate should explain more than the total price.

Look for:

Estimate Category What to Verify
Emergency work Water removal, stabilization, temporary protection
Demolition Materials being removed
Drying Equipment and monitoring
Structural repairs Framing, subfloor, roofing, etc.
Electrical Repairs or replacement
Plumbing Repairs and replacement
HVAC Inspection and repairs
Insulation Removal and replacement
Finishes Drywall, flooring, paint, trim
Debris Removal and disposal
Permits Who obtains and pays for them
Labor Included labor and subcontractors
Materials Specifications and allowances
Change orders Process for additional damage

Hidden damage can become apparent after walls, ceilings, or floors are opened. Your contract should explain how unexpected conditions will be documented and priced.

Understand Insurance Payments

Insurance restoration can involve more complicated payment arrangements than an ordinary renovation.

Depending on the mortgage and insurance arrangements, an insurance settlement check may involve both the homeowner and mortgage servicer. The servicer may release funds in stages as restoration progresses.

Before starting, understand:

  • Total approved claim amount
  • Deductible
  • Contractor estimate
  • Insurance-approved scope
  • Any gap between the estimate and expected repair cost
  • Payment schedule
  • Mortgage-servicer requirements
  • Out-of-pocket expenses

Do not assume that the insurance settlement automatically covers every improvement you want to make.

If you upgrade from the damaged material to a substantially more expensive product, the difference may be your responsibility unless the policy or insurer provides otherwise.

Be Careful With Assignment and Payment Agreements

Read every document before signing, particularly documents involving insurance proceeds.

Be cautious if a contractor:

  • Demands full payment before work begins
  • Pressures you to sign immediately
  • Refuses to provide a detailed contract
  • Won't provide insurance documentation
  • Arrives unsolicited immediately after a disaster
  • Offers an unusually large discount for immediate payment
  • Asks you to exaggerate damage on an insurance claim
  • Tells you that you must use a particular lender

The CFPB specifically warns homeowners about contractors who demand upfront payments, pressure consumers after disasters, or attempt to steer homeowners toward particular lenders.

The FTC similarly recommends avoiding cash payments and obtaining a written contract before restoration work begins.

Ask About Permits and Building Codes

Restoration is not always a simple replacement of what was there before.

Major damage may trigger requirements involving:

  • Building permits
  • Electrical permits
  • Plumbing permits
  • Structural engineering
  • Updated building-code requirements
  • Flood-resistant construction
  • Energy requirements
  • Fire-safety requirements

Ask the contractor who will obtain each required permit and who will arrange inspections.

FEMA specifically advises homeowners to consult local code officials when repairing or rebuilding disaster-damaged structures because local requirements can affect how and where reconstruction is performed.

Watch for Mold and Moisture Problems

Water damage deserves particular attention because the visible damage may not tell the entire story.

Wet drywall, insulation, flooring, framing, and other materials can retain moisture after standing water has been removed.

The EPA recommends professional assistance for larger mold problems or situations involving significant water damage, and advises homeowners to select professionals with relevant experience.

Ask the restoration contractor:

  • What moisture readings were recorded?
  • Which materials were removed?
  • Which materials were retained?
  • How was drying verified?
  • Was affected insulation replaced?
  • How was potential mold growth evaluated?
  • What documentation will be provided?

Final Restoration Contractor Checklist

Before hiring a restoration company, confirm:

  • Damage has been documented
  • Insurance company has been contacted
  • Contractor license has been verified
  • Insurance coverage has been verified
  • Restoration experience has been checked
  • References have been contacted
  • Subcontractors have been identified
  • Written estimates have been obtained
  • Scope of work is detailed
  • Materials are specified
  • Permit responsibilities are clear
  • Payment schedule is documented
  • Change-order procedures are documented
  • Insurance payment arrangements are understood
  • Warranties are provided in writing
  • Final inspection and cleanup are included

Final Considerations

Choosing a home restoration contractor requires more than comparing estimates. The right company needs to understand the type of damage, know how to stabilize the property, coordinate specialized trades, comply with applicable building requirements, and document the work properly.

For insurance-related restoration, homeowners should also keep the insurance company, mortgage servicer, contractor, and their own records aligned throughout the project. The CFPB recommends maintaining written records, receipts, contracts, warranties, payment documentation, and project progress information during disaster recovery.

Most importantly, don't let the urgency of restoring your home eliminate basic contractor due diligence. A few hours spent checking licenses, insurance, references, estimates, and contracts can help prevent months of problems after the restoration work has already begun.
