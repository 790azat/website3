ADU (Accessory Dwelling Unit) Construction Costs and Permit Requirements

An accessory dwelling unit (ADU) can turn unused space, a garage, or part of a residential lot into a separate living area. Homeowners use ADUs for family members, long-term rentals, guest accommodations, or additional living space.

But an ADU is more complicated than simply adding a small building to a backyard. Construction costs can vary dramatically depending on whether the unit is converted from existing space or built from the ground up. Local zoning, building codes, utility connections, site conditions, and permit fees can also have a major effect on the final budget.

In 2026, one national cost estimate puts the average ADU construction price around $180,000, with a broad range of approximately $40,000 to $360,000. Permit costs alone can range from roughly $1,350 to $9,000 depending on location and project characteristics.

What Is an ADU?

An accessory dwelling unit is a separate residential living space located on the same property as a primary home.

An ADU can generally be:

  • Detached: A separate backyard or accessory structure.
  • Attached: Connected to the primary residence.
  • Interior: Created inside an existing basement, attic, or other space.
  • Garage conversion: An existing garage converted into habitable living space.
  • Addition: A new portion added to the primary house.

The exact definition and permitted configurations depend on local law.

For example, Portland requires a building permit for both new ADUs and conversions of garages, attics, or basements into habitable space, along with applicable electrical, plumbing, and mechanical permits.

ADU Construction Cost Breakdown

The biggest cost difference usually comes from the type of project.

ADU Type General Cost Consideration
Basement conversion Potentially lower if utilities and structure already exist
Garage conversion Existing shell can reduce construction work
Attached ADU May share some systems with the primary house
Detached ADU Requires more site and exterior construction
Custom high-end ADU Significantly higher material and labor costs

A conversion may look inexpensive initially, but existing spaces often need substantial upgrades before they qualify as legal living areas.

Common issues include ceiling height, emergency egress, insulation, ventilation, stairs, plumbing, and electrical capacity. Portland specifically identifies these as potential challenges when converting existing space to an ADU.

Major Expenses to Include in the Budget

Design and Engineering

Depending on the project, you may need:

  • Architectural drawings
  • Structural engineering
  • Site plans
  • Surveying
  • Energy calculations
  • Civil or drainage plans
  • Specialty reports

A simple conversion can require less design work than a detached custom structure.

Site Preparation

Detached ADUs can require:

  • Excavation
  • Grading
  • Foundation work
  • Drainage
  • Tree removal
  • Utility trenching
  • Driveway or access modifications

A difficult lot can therefore cost considerably more than a straightforward backyard site.

Foundation and Framing

A new detached ADU generally requires a new foundation and complete structural framing.

The foundation type may depend on soil, climate, grading, flood exposure, local code requirements, and the building design.

Plumbing and Electrical

ADUs generally need functioning residential utility systems.

Budget for:

  • Water supply
  • Sewer or septic connection
  • Drainage
  • Electrical service
  • Electrical panel capacity
  • Lighting
  • Outlets
  • Plumbing fixtures
  • Water heating

Some jurisdictions may require additional utility infrastructure or fees. Seattle, for example, notes that ADUs can involve sewer treatment capacity charges and may require electrical service changes or new services.

Kitchen and Bathroom

Because an ADU is intended to function as a separate living space, kitchen and bathroom construction can become a significant portion of the budget.

Costs can include:

  • Cabinets
  • Countertops
  • Appliances
  • Plumbing fixtures
  • Shower or tub
  • Toilet
  • Vanity
  • Tile
  • Exhaust ventilation
  • Water-resistant materials

Choosing standard-size cabinets and readily available fixtures can make the project easier to budget than a fully custom interior.

HVAC

An ADU needs an appropriate heating and cooling system.

Possible approaches include:

  • Ductless mini-splits
  • Central HVAC
  • Heat pumps
  • Electric resistance heating
  • Systems connected to the primary home

The best solution depends on the climate, size, insulation, available electrical capacity, and local requirements.

Don't Forget Soft Costs

Homeowners sometimes focus on construction labor and materials while overlooking project costs outside the physical building.

Include a separate allowance for:

  • Architecture
  • Engineering
  • Surveys
  • Permit fees
  • Plan review
  • Utility fees
  • Impact or system-development charges
  • Inspections
  • Contractor overhead
  • Waste disposal
  • Temporary site protection

Portland, for example, states that ADU projects can involve building permit fees, water service fees, and system development charges, which can be substantial depending on the project.

What Permits Does an ADU Need?

There is no single nationwide ADU permit.

The exact requirements are determined by the property's jurisdiction and the project's scope.

Common approvals can include:

  1. Zoning or land-use approval
  2. Building permit
  3. Electrical permit
  4. Plumbing permit
  5. Mechanical/HVAC permit
  6. Sewer or septic approval
  7. Utility approvals
  8. Inspections
  9. Final occupancy approval

Some jurisdictions combine several approvals into one application, while others handle them separately.

The distinction between zoning and building codes is important. Zoning determines whether and where an ADU can be built; building codes establish how the structure must be constructed.

Oregon's building authority explicitly advises homeowners to address local land-use requirements first because zoning determines whether construction is permitted before building-code compliance is considered.

Check Zoning Before Paying for Plans

Before hiring an architect or contractor, determine whether your property can legally accommodate an ADU.

Ask the local planning department about:

  • Whether ADUs are permitted
  • Maximum ADU size
  • Setbacks
  • Height restrictions
  • Lot coverage
  • Parking requirements
  • Owner-occupancy rules
  • Short-term rental restrictions
  • Number of ADUs allowed
  • Historic-district requirements
  • Floodplain or environmental restrictions
  • Utility requirements

State laws can also change local rules.

For example, Washington currently requires many cities and counties planning under the state's Growth Management Act to allow two ADUs on qualifying residential lots, subject to the statutory framework and applicable local rules.

Massachusetts has also adopted statewide ADU requirements that limit the ability of local zoning rules to conflict with state ADU law.

These examples illustrate why homeowners should not assume that an ADU rule from another city applies to their property.

Prepare the Permit Application

A typical application may require:

  • Property information
  • Site plan
  • Existing floor plan
  • Proposed floor plan
  • Building elevations
  • Structural drawings
  • Electrical plans
  • Plumbing plans
  • Mechanical plans
  • Energy documentation
  • Construction details
  • Contractor information

Portland, for example, requires ADU projects to have a site plan and may require detailed floor and structural plans depending on the project.

Using a pre-approved ADU design, where available, can simplify the process. Seattle maintains pre-approved detached ADU designs intended to make permitting more predictable.

How Long Does ADU Permitting Take?

There is no universal permit timeline.

The process can involve:

Property research → preliminary design → zoning review → architectural/engineering plans → permit application → plan review → revisions → permit issuance → construction → inspections → final approval.

A straightforward project using standardized plans may move faster than a custom ADU on a difficult property.

California, for example, has statewide processing requirements under its ADU framework, including review of application completeness within 30 days and approval or denial within 60 days for qualifying applications.

Other jurisdictions have different procedures.

Build a Contingency Into the Budget

An ADU budget should include money for unexpected conditions.

Potential surprises include:

  • Poor soil
  • Drainage problems
  • Existing foundation deficiencies
  • Underground utilities
  • Electrical service limitations
  • Sewer connection complications
  • Required fire upgrades
  • Unexpected structural work
  • Material price changes
  • Permit revisions

A contingency reserve can prevent a relatively small construction problem from forcing the homeowner to stop work or take on additional debt.

Questions to Ask an ADU Contractor

Before signing a construction contract, ask:

  • Is your license current?
  • Do you carry appropriate insurance?
  • Who obtains the permits?
  • Who pays permit and utility fees?
  • Are architectural and engineering services included?
  • Are site preparation costs included?
  • What assumptions were made about utilities?
  • What is excluded from the estimate?
  • How are change orders handled?
  • What payment schedule is proposed?
  • What warranty is provided?
  • Who coordinates inspections?
  • What happens if the project encounters unexpected site conditions?

Get the answers in writing.

ADU Construction Checklist

Before breaking ground, confirm:

  • ADUs are permitted on the property.
  • Maximum size has been verified.
  • Setbacks and height limits are understood.
  • Utility requirements have been checked.
  • Sewer or septic capacity has been confirmed.
  • Site conditions have been evaluated.
  • Plans are complete.
  • Contractor bids are comparable.
  • Permit responsibilities are documented.
  • Permit and impact fees are included in the budget.
  • Construction contingency is reserved.
  • Financing has been arranged if necessary.
  • Required inspections are understood.
  • Final occupancy requirements are confirmed.

The Bottom Line

Building an ADU can be a substantial home improvement project, and the construction price is only part of the financial equation.

A detached unit may require site preparation, foundation work, utility connections, exterior construction, and extensive permitting. A garage or basement conversion may reduce some costs but can still require major upgrades to meet residential building standards.

The safest way to establish a realistic ADU budget is to verify zoning first, identify utility and site constraints, obtain detailed plans, request comparable contractor estimates, and include permit fees and contingency funds before construction begins.

Because ADU regulations vary by state, county, and city, homeowners should confirm the current requirements with the local planning and building departments rather than relying on a generic national checklist. Local authorities ultimately determine which zoning, building, trade, utility, and inspection requirements apply to a particular property.
