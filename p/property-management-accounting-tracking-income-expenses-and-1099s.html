Property Management Accounting: Tracking Income, Expenses, and 1099s

Property management accounting becomes increasingly important as a rental portfolio grows. Tracking rent payments in a bank account may be sufficient for a single property, but multiple properties create a much more complicated financial picture.

Landlords and property managers may need to track rental income, security deposits, repairs, utilities, insurance, property taxes, management fees, contractor payments, owner distributions, and capital improvements. They also need reliable records for tax reporting and, in some cases, information returns such as Forms 1099.

The objective is to create an accounting system that clearly shows where money came from, where it was spent, which property generated the transaction, and how each transaction should be treated for financial and tax purposes.

Separate Property Finances

One of the first steps toward organized property management accounting is separating rental activity from personal finances.

For larger portfolios, it can also be useful to organize transactions by:

  • Property
  • Unit
  • Legal entity
  • Owner
  • Bank account
  • Expense category

Separate accounts make reconciliation easier and create a clearer audit trail.

The IRS emphasizes maintaining records that document rental income and expenses, including receipts, bills, canceled checks, and other supporting documentation.

Create a Consistent Chart of Accounts

A chart of accounts establishes the categories used to classify transactions.

Typical rental-income categories can include:

  • Base rent
  • Parking income
  • Storage income
  • Pet rent
  • Application fees
  • Late fees
  • Utility reimbursements
  • Other tenant charges

Expense categories may include:

  • Repairs and maintenance
  • Property management
  • Insurance
  • Property taxes
  • Utilities
  • Landscaping
  • Cleaning
  • Pest control
  • Advertising
  • Legal and professional services
  • Accounting
  • Mortgage interest
  • Supplies

A consistent chart of accounts makes it easier to compare properties and prepare financial reports.

Track Income by Property

Every rental payment should be associated with the correct property and tenant.

For example, if a management company collects $50,000 in rent across ten properties, recording only the total deposit into a bank account does not provide enough information for useful property-level reporting.

The accounting system should identify:

Property → Tenant → Rent charge → Payment → Deposit

This makes it easier to determine occupancy revenue, outstanding balances, delinquency, and property-level performance.

The IRS generally requires rental income to be included in gross income, and cash-basis taxpayers generally report rental income when it is actually or constructively received.

Handle Security Deposits Separately

Security deposits require careful treatment because they are not automatically rental income.

The IRS states that a security deposit generally is not included in income when the landlord may be required to return it to the tenant at the end of the lease. If the landlord later keeps part or all of the deposit under the terms of the agreement, the tax treatment can change depending on the circumstances.

From an accounting perspective, refundable security deposits should therefore be tracked separately from rental revenue.

The system should record:

  • Amount received
  • Tenant
  • Property
  • Date received
  • Deposit account, where required
  • Amount returned
  • Permitted deductions
  • Date of settlement

State and local laws can also establish specific requirements for handling and returning security deposits.

Distinguish Repairs From Improvements

Not every property expense should be treated identically.

Ordinary repairs and maintenance may be treated differently from capital improvements for accounting and tax purposes.

Examples of ordinary operating expenses can include routine plumbing repairs, minor electrical work, cleaning, landscaping, and maintenance supplies.

A major roof replacement, building renovation, or substantial system upgrade may instead require capitalization and depreciation under applicable tax rules.

The IRS explains that improvements generally are not deducted in full in the year they are incurred; their cost is generally recovered through depreciation under the applicable rules.

This distinction is important because incorrectly categorizing a major improvement as an ordinary repair can distort both financial reporting and tax calculations.

Track Property-Level Expenses

Property managers should assign expenses to the property that actually incurred them whenever possible.

For example:

Property A: $1,200 HVAC repair

Property B: $850 plumbing repair

Property C: $500 landscaping

Recording all three simply as "maintenance" without property-level attribution makes it difficult to determine which properties require unusually high maintenance spending.

Property-level reporting can also help owners evaluate whether recurring repair costs justify equipment replacement or other capital investments.

Reconcile Bank Accounts Regularly

Bank reconciliation compares the accounting records with actual bank activity.

A monthly reconciliation should identify:

  • Deposits
  • Rent payments
  • Contractor payments
  • Electronic transfers
  • Bank fees
  • Owner distributions
  • Mortgage payments
  • Outstanding checks
  • Unrecorded transactions

Reconciliation helps identify duplicate transactions, missing entries, incorrect amounts, and unauthorized payments.

For property managers handling funds for multiple owners, regular reconciliation becomes particularly important because money may move between operating accounts, security-deposit accounts, owner accounts, and property-specific accounts.

Track Accounts Payable

Contractor and vendor invoices should not be tracked solely through email.

A structured accounts-payable process can record:

  • Vendor
  • Property
  • Invoice number
  • Invoice date
  • Service description
  • Amount
  • Approval status
  • Payment date
  • Payment method
  • Applicable 1099 classification

This information makes year-end reporting significantly easier.

It also allows managers to identify unpaid invoices and avoid paying the same invoice twice.

Collect Vendor Tax Information Early

Property managers who regularly hire independent contractors should collect the appropriate tax information before payments accumulate.

For U.S. information reporting, Form W-9 is commonly used to obtain a contractor's taxpayer identification number and certification.

The information should be stored securely because it can contain sensitive taxpayer information.

When onboarding a plumber, electrician, landscaper, cleaner, handyman, or other service provider, establish the vendor record before the first payment whenever practical.

Understanding Form 1099-NEC

Form 1099-NEC is generally used to report qualifying nonemployee compensation for services performed in a trade or business.

For payments made during 2026, the federal reporting threshold for qualifying nonemployee compensation is $2,000, compared with $600 for payments made before 2026. The IRS states that the new $2,000 threshold applies to payments made in 2026, with future thresholds subject to inflation adjustments.

This can apply to qualifying payments to independent contractors such as certain maintenance professionals, accountants, attorneys, or other service providers.

The classification depends on the nature of the payment and the recipient, so landlords should not determine reporting requirements solely from the vendor's job title.

Understanding Form 1099-MISC

Form 1099-MISC is used for several categories of payments that are different from nonemployee compensation.

For 2026, the IRS instructions generally identify a $2,000 threshold for rents reported in Box 1, along with specific rules for other categories.

There is an important property-management exception involving rent paid to real estate agents or property managers. The IRS explains that rent paid to a real estate agent or property manager generally does not have to be reported by the property owner on Form 1099-MISC; the agent or property manager may have its own reporting obligation for rent passed through to the property owner.

Because information-reporting rules can depend on the payment structure and recipient's tax status, the property's accountant or tax professional should review unusual arrangements.

Do Not Assume Every Contractor Requires a 1099

Not every payment to a vendor automatically produces a Form 1099 requirement.

The IRS identifies exceptions, including certain payments to corporations and payments for merchandise rather than services. There are also specific rules for attorneys and other categories of recipients.

For this reason, a property-management accounting system should record vendor tax classifications rather than simply generating a 1099 whenever a vendor exceeds a dollar threshold.

The goal is to apply the applicable IRS rules to the specific payment.

Track 1099s Throughout the Year

Waiting until January to determine which vendors require 1099 reporting can create unnecessary administrative work.

A better workflow is:

  1. Create a vendor record.
  2. Collect the appropriate tax information.
  3. Record the vendor's entity and payment classification.
  4. Track qualifying payments throughout the year.
  5. Review threshold totals periodically.
  6. Reconcile payments with the general ledger.
  7. Generate applicable information returns.
  8. Deliver recipient copies and file with the IRS according to applicable deadlines.

The IRS requires Form 1099-NEC to be filed with the IRS and furnished to the recipient by January 31 for reportable payments.

Electronic filing may be required depending on the total number of information returns filed, so property managers should review the current IRS filing rules before year-end.

Use Software for Portfolio-Level Reporting

Property-management accounting software can connect tenant ledgers, bank transactions, invoices, work orders, vendors, and owner statements.

Useful reports include:

  • Rent roll
  • Income statement
  • General ledger
  • Accounts receivable
  • Accounts payable
  • Property-level profit and loss
  • Owner statement
  • Vendor payment report
  • Maintenance expense report
  • 1099 payment report
  • Budget-versus-actual report

The larger the portfolio, the more valuable standardized reporting becomes.

Keep an Audit Trail

Every significant transaction should have supporting documentation.

For example, a $3,500 plumbing expense should ideally connect:

Vendor → Invoice → Work order → Property → Payment → Accounting entry

This creates a traceable record that can be reviewed by the property manager, owner, accountant, or tax professional.

The IRS recommends maintaining records that substantiate rental income and expenses and notes that documentation may be required if a return is audited.

Review Financials Monthly

Monthly accounting reviews can identify problems before they become year-end surprises.

Review:

  • Rent collected
  • Delinquent balances
  • Operating expenses
  • Contractor payments
  • Bank reconciliations
  • Security-deposit balances
  • Owner distributions
  • Property-level profitability
  • Unusual transactions

Compare actual results with the property's budget.

A sudden increase in plumbing, HVAC, or turnover costs may indicate a building issue that deserves attention before expenses continue increasing.

Final Considerations

Property management accounting should provide more than a record of money entering and leaving a bank account. A properly structured system should show how each transaction relates to a particular property, tenant, owner, vendor, and accounting category.

Income should be tracked consistently, expenses should be classified accurately, security deposits should be kept distinct from rental revenue, and major improvements should be evaluated separately from ordinary repairs.

For 2026, landlords and property managers also need to account for the IRS's updated information-reporting thresholds: qualifying nonemployee compensation and several Form 1099-MISC categories generally use a $2,000 threshold for payments made during 2026, replacing the $600 threshold used for earlier payments.

Because tax treatment can depend on the property owner's structure, accounting method, vendor classification, and specific transaction, this information should not replace advice from a qualified tax professional.

A consistent accounting workflow, combined with monthly reconciliation and year-round vendor tracking, can make property finances easier to manage and significantly simplify tax preparation as a rental portfolio expands.
