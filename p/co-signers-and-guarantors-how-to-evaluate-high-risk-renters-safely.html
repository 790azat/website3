Co-Signers and Guarantors: How to Evaluate High-Risk Renters Safely

A rental applicant who does not meet a property's standard income or credit criteria does not necessarily have to be rejected. In some situations, a qualified co-signer or guarantor can provide an additional layer of financial protection for the landlord.

But accepting a guarantor is not the same as eliminating risk.

The landlord still needs to evaluate the primary applicant, verify the guarantor's financial capacity, document the arrangement properly, and apply screening standards consistently. When consumer reports are involved, additional requirements under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) can also apply. The Federal Trade Commission specifically identifies requiring a co-signer as a potential adverse action when it is based on information in a consumer report.

A structured process can help landlords protect rental income without turning guarantor arrangements into an inconsistent or unnecessarily complicated screening system.

What Is the Difference Between a Co-Signer and a Guarantor?

The terms are sometimes used interchangeably, but the legal effect depends on the lease and applicable state law.

A co-signer generally signs the rental agreement and may have contractual responsibility for the tenant's obligations.

A guarantor typically agrees to satisfy specified obligations if the primary tenant fails to do so.

The actual rights and obligations should come from the written agreement rather than the label.

Before accepting either arrangement, landlords should understand:

  • Who is legally responsible for unpaid rent
  • Whether liability includes property damage
  • Whether liability continues through lease renewals
  • Whether the guarantor's obligation has a dollar limit
  • Whether the guarantee covers fees or other charges
  • When the landlord can pursue the guarantor
  • Whether state law imposes additional requirements

A standardized lease or guaranty prepared or reviewed by a qualified local attorney can reduce ambiguity.

When Should a Landlord Consider a Guarantor?

A guarantor can be useful when an applicant falls short of a predefined qualification but otherwise meets the property's screening requirements.

Examples might include:

  • Limited credit history
  • A first-time renter
  • Recent relocation
  • Variable or newly established income
  • Student or recent graduate
  • Self-employment with difficult-to-document income
  • Income that falls below the property's standard
  • A previous financial event that requires additional underwriting

The key is to establish the property's criteria before reviewing individual applicants.

Instead of deciding case by case whether someone "feels risky," landlords can define objective conditions under which a guarantor may be accepted.

For example:

Applicants who do not independently satisfy the standard income requirement may qualify with an approved guarantor who meets the property's separate income, credit, and verification requirements.

That creates a repeatable process.

Evaluate the Primary Applicant Separately

A strong guarantor should not automatically erase every concern about the primary applicant.

The landlord should still evaluate the applicant using the same relevant criteria applied to comparable applicants.

Review factors such as:

  • Verified income
  • Employment or income source
  • Credit history
  • Rental history
  • Previous landlord references
  • Outstanding rental obligations
  • Application completeness
  • Ability to meet ongoing lease obligations

The purpose is to understand the entire tenancy risk rather than simply finding someone else who can pay the rent.

Tenant screening reports may contain credit, rental, eviction, or criminal-history information and are generally subject to the FCRA when used for housing decisions.

Screen the Guarantor Too

A guarantor is taking on a financial obligation, so the landlord should not accept the person solely because the applicant says they are financially secure.

The guarantor's screening process can include:

Income verification

Determine whether the guarantor has sufficient income to support the obligation if necessary.

Depending on the property's policy, documentation might include:

  • Pay statements
  • Tax documents
  • Bank statements where appropriate
  • Employment verification
  • Business-income documentation for self-employed guarantors

Credit history

A credit report can provide information about the guarantor's existing financial obligations and payment history.

However, landlords should use the same predetermined criteria consistently rather than selecting arbitrary thresholds after seeing an applicant's report.

Existing obligations

A guarantor with substantial existing debt or multiple guarantees may have less practical capacity than income alone suggests.

The objective is not to collect every possible piece of personal information. It is to establish whether the guarantor can realistically satisfy the obligation if the tenant defaults.

Verify That the Guarantor Is Actually Who They Claim to Be

Identity verification is an important part of the process.

A landlord should confirm that:

  • The guarantor's identity matches the application
  • Contact information is consistent
  • Income documents appear legitimate
  • Employment information can be independently verified
  • The person understands what they are agreeing to

Be particularly cautious about documents that appear altered, inconsistent, or incomplete.

If a third-party screening company is used, the landlord should understand what information the service actually verifies rather than assuming that a screening score guarantees accuracy.

Recent FTC enforcement activity illustrates why accuracy matters. In July 2026, the FTC announced a $2.25 million settlement involving RentGrow over allegations concerning the accuracy and handling of tenant-screening reports.

Don't Treat a Credit Score as the Entire Decision

Credit scores can be useful, but they are only one component of rental underwriting.

A landlord could consider a broader picture:

Factor Primary Applicant Guarantor
Identity verification Yes Yes
Income verification Yes Yes
Credit history Yes Yes
Rental history Yes As appropriate
Employment/income stability Yes Yes
Existing obligations Yes Yes
References As appropriate As appropriate
Lease liability Tenant Guarantor obligation

This approach helps distinguish between someone with limited credit history and someone with a documented pattern of serious payment problems.

Establish Clear Guarantor Requirements

A written policy might specify requirements such as:

  • Minimum verified income
  • Minimum credit standard
  • Acceptable income sources
  • Required documentation
  • Residency requirements, if legally permissible
  • Required identification
  • Maximum guarantee amount
  • Required lease or guaranty signatures

The exact thresholds should reflect the property's market and applicable law.

More importantly, the same policy should be available to all similarly situated applicants.

HUD's 2024 rental-screening guidance emphasizes that housing providers remain responsible for complying with the Fair Housing Act even when screening is outsourced to third-party companies. It also cautions against imprecise or overly broad screening criteria.

Apply the Policy Consistently

Consistency is one of the most important protections for a landlord.

Suppose the written policy says an applicant can qualify with a guarantor who meets a specific income and credit standard.

One applicant should not be required to provide a guarantor with substantially greater qualifications simply because the property manager has a subjective concern about that applicant.

The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination in housing based on protected characteristics including race, color, national origin, religion, sex, disability, and familial status.

State and local laws may provide additional protections.

Landlords should therefore avoid making screening decisions based on personal impressions, stereotypes, protected characteristics, or information unrelated to the applicant's ability to satisfy the lease.

Understand FCRA Requirements

This is particularly important when a landlord uses a tenant-screening company, credit bureau, background-check service, or other consumer-reporting agency.

The FTC states that landlords must have a permissible purpose before obtaining a consumer report and must certify that the report will be used only for housing purposes.

More importantly, if information from a consumer report contributes to an adverse action, the landlord generally must provide an adverse-action notice.

The FTC specifically lists these examples:

  • Rejecting an application
  • Requiring a co-signer
  • Requiring a larger deposit
  • Charging higher rent

The notice must include information about the consumer-reporting company and the applicant's rights to dispute inaccurate information and obtain a free copy of the report within the applicable 60-day period.

This means a landlord should not assume that "we approved them with a guarantor" eliminates FCRA obligations.

If the consumer report influenced the decision to require the guarantor, an adverse-action notice may be required.

Screen the Guarantor's Consumer Report Properly

If a landlord obtains a credit or tenant-screening report on a proposed guarantor, that report should be handled as a consumer report when covered by the FCRA.

That means landlords should:

  1. Have a permissible purpose.
  2. Use the information only for the permitted housing purpose.
  3. Follow applicable disclosure and authorization procedures.
  4. Make decisions using established criteria.
  5. Provide required adverse-action notices when applicable.
  6. Securely dispose of consumer reports when they are no longer needed.

The FTC specifically states that consumer reports and information gathered from them must be securely disposed of after use.

Make the Guaranty Legally Clear

A guarantor arrangement should not depend on a casual promise such as:

"My parents will pay if anything happens."

The written agreement should clearly establish the guarantor's obligations.

Depending on the jurisdiction and agreement, relevant provisions may address:

  • Rent
  • Late charges
  • Property damage
  • Lease violations
  • Legal expenses
  • Renewal periods
  • Extensions
  • Maximum liability
  • Notice requirements
  • Termination of the guaranty

Because guaranty law varies by state, landlords should use locally appropriate documents rather than copying a generic form from the internet.

Watch for Red Flags in Documentation

A guarantor arrangement deserves additional verification when documentation contains inconsistencies.

Potential warning signs include:

  • Employer information that cannot be verified
  • Income documents with inconsistent figures
  • Different addresses across documents
  • Unexplained gaps in employment information
  • Unusual alterations to financial documents
  • Refusal to provide required verification
  • Information that conflicts with the application

A red flag is not automatically proof of misconduct.

The appropriate response is generally to verify the information through the landlord's established process rather than immediately making an accusation.

Use Property Management Software to Standardize the Process

For landlords managing multiple properties, a property-management platform can help organize guarantor screening.

Useful features can include:

  • Digital applications
  • Document collection
  • Screening integrations
  • Income verification
  • Application status tracking
  • Electronic signatures
  • Lease management
  • Automated reminders
  • Secure document storage

Technology should support the landlord's screening policy rather than create the policy itself.

HUD's screening guidance notes that automated screening does not remove Fair Housing Act responsibilities.

Landlords should understand what criteria an automated system uses and retain meaningful oversight of the final decision.

A Practical Guarantor Evaluation Workflow

A simple process can look like this:

1. Receive application → 2. Apply standard screening criteria → 3. Determine whether guarantor qualification is permitted → 4. Obtain required authorization → 5. Screen guarantor → 6. Verify income and identity → 7. Evaluate guarantor against written criteria → 8. Document decision → 9. Issue required notices → 10. Execute lease and guaranty → 11. Secure records.

This reduces inconsistent decisions and makes the process easier to audit later.

Final Considerations

A co-signer or guarantor can provide meaningful protection when an applicant does not independently satisfy a property's standard qualification criteria. But the arrangement works only when the additional party is financially capable, properly verified, and legally obligated.

The safest approach is to treat guarantor screening as an extension of the property's normal underwriting process—not as an informal exception.

Use written qualification standards, verify both parties, evaluate financial capacity rather than subjective impressions, handle consumer reports according to the FCRA, and apply the same rules consistently.

That gives landlords a clearer way to manage rental risk while maintaining a screening process that is structured, documented, and easier to defend.
