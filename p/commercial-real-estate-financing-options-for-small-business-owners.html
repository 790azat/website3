Commercial Real Estate Financing Options for Small Business Owners

Buying commercial real estate can be a major step for a small business. Owning the building where your company operates can provide greater control over the property, eliminate the uncertainty of renewing a commercial lease, and potentially build equity over time.

But commercial property financing works differently from a traditional residential mortgage. Lenders typically evaluate the business, the property, the borrower's financial strength, projected cash flow, and the proposed use of the building.

For small business owners, financing options can include SBA 7(a) loans, SBA 504 loans, conventional commercial real estate loans, commercial bridge loans, and other specialized financing. The appropriate structure depends on whether you are purchasing an owner-occupied property, constructing a facility, refinancing existing debt, or acquiring an investment property.

What Is Commercial Real Estate Financing?

Commercial real estate financing is a loan used to purchase, construct, renovate, refinance, or otherwise finance property used for business purposes.

Examples include:

  • Office buildings
  • Retail stores
  • Restaurants
  • Medical offices
  • Warehouses
  • Manufacturing facilities
  • Service businesses
  • Mixed-use properties
  • Industrial buildings

A major distinction is whether the property will be owner-occupied or primarily held as an investment.

A business purchasing a building where it will operate is evaluated differently from an investor purchasing an apartment building or commercial property primarily for rental income.

For SBA financing, the distinction is particularly important because SBA programs are designed for eligible operating businesses rather than speculative real estate investment. The SBA 504 program, for example, cannot be used for speculation or investment in rental real estate.

SBA 7(a) Loans

The SBA 7(a) program is one of the most flexible government-backed financing options available to qualifying small businesses.

A 7(a) loan can be used to acquire, refinance, or improve real estate and buildings. It can also finance working capital, equipment, furniture, fixtures, supplies, and certain other business needs. This flexibility can be useful when a commercial property purchase needs to be combined with other business investments.

The maximum 7(a) loan amount is currently $5 million.

For loans above $150,000, the SBA generally guarantees up to 75% of the loan, subject to the program's rules. Real estate financing can have a maturity of up to 25 years, including applicable extensions.

A 7(a) loan may therefore be useful when a business wants to purchase a building while also financing renovations, equipment, or other eligible business expenses.

Example

Suppose a small manufacturing company wants to purchase a $1.8 million building and spend another $300,000 on eligible improvements and equipment.

A 7(a) structure could potentially combine multiple eligible uses into one financing package, subject to the lender's underwriting and SBA requirements.

The business would still need to demonstrate creditworthiness and a reasonable ability to repay the debt.

SBA 504 Loans

The SBA 504 program is specifically designed for major fixed assets.

It can finance the purchase or construction of buildings and land as well as certain long-term equipment. The program provides long-term, fixed-rate financing through a structure involving a participating lender and an SBA Certified Development Company (CDC).

The current maximum 504 loan amount is $5.5 million. Real estate projects can have 25-year maturity terms.

A typical 504 structure involves:

  • A third-party lender providing at least 50% of the project financing
  • A CDC providing up to 40%
  • The borrower contributing at least 10%

Certain projects can require a larger borrower contribution depending on the circumstances.

This structure can make 504 financing particularly relevant for businesses purchasing or constructing substantial owner-occupied facilities.

SBA 7(a) vs. SBA 504

Although both programs can finance commercial real estate, their structures differ.

Feature SBA 7(a) SBA 504
Maximum program loan $5 million $5.5 million
Real estate use Purchase, refinance, improvements Purchase, construction, renovation
Working capital Permitted Generally not permitted
Equipment Permitted Certain long-term equipment
Real estate maturity Up to 25 years Up to 25 years
Rate structure Negotiated, subject to SBA limits Fixed-rate CDC portion
Delivery SBA participating lender Third-party lender + CDC
Investment rental real estate Not designed for speculative use Not permitted

The SBA states that 7(a) loans can finance multiple purposes, while 504 loans are intended for major fixed assets and cannot be used for working capital or speculative/investment rental real estate.

Conventional Commercial Real Estate Loans

Businesses that do not need SBA financing can also approach banks and credit unions for conventional commercial real estate loans.

These loans are generally secured by the commercial property and may also involve guarantees or additional collateral depending on the lender and borrower.

A conventional lender may examine:

  • Business revenue
  • Historical profitability
  • Debt service coverage
  • Business and personal credit
  • Property value
  • Property condition
  • Existing leases
  • Borrower liquidity
  • Down payment
  • Experience operating the business
  • Overall repayment capacity

Terms vary significantly among financial institutions.

Some conventional loans have shorter amortization periods or balloon structures, while others may provide longer amortization. Interest rates can also be fixed, variable, or structured with an initial fixed period.

Because commercial loans can vary considerably, comparing the full repayment structure is important rather than focusing only on the initial interest rate.

Commercial Mortgage Loans

A commercial mortgage is essentially the business-property equivalent of a mortgage secured by real estate.

The property itself provides collateral for the loan, but the lender may also evaluate the financial strength of the business.

For an owner-occupied property, the lender may focus heavily on whether the company's operating cash flow can support the new debt.

For income-producing properties, lenders may also analyze the property's rental income and operating expenses.

The resulting loan amount can therefore depend on both property value and cash-flow capacity.

Commercial Bridge Loans

A bridge loan provides short-term financing while a business waits for a longer-term financing solution or another transaction to occur.

For example, a company might need to purchase a new facility before its existing property is sold.

Bridge financing can provide speed and flexibility, but it typically comes with a shorter repayment period than permanent commercial real estate financing.

A borrower should have a clearly defined exit strategy, such as:

  • Refinancing into permanent financing
  • Selling another property
  • Completing a property sale
  • Receiving long-term financing after renovations
  • Stabilizing a newly acquired property

The shorter maturity means the borrower needs to understand what happens if the expected refinancing or sale takes longer than anticipated.

Construction Financing

Businesses purchasing land and constructing a new facility may need construction financing rather than a standard commercial mortgage.

Construction loans can fund expenses such as:

  • Land acquisition
  • Site preparation
  • Building construction
  • Contractor payments
  • Materials
  • Engineering
  • Permits
  • Certain professional fees

Funds are typically released according to a construction draw schedule rather than being provided entirely at closing.

Once construction is complete, the financing may be refinanced into permanent commercial real estate debt or converted under the terms of the original financing structure.

SBA 504 financing can also support the purchase or construction of qualifying buildings and land for eligible small businesses.

How Much Down Payment Is Required?

There is no single commercial real estate down payment requirement.

The amount depends on the financing program, property, borrower, lender, occupancy, credit profile, and project risk.

For SBA 504 projects, the standard structure can involve a minimum 10% borrower contribution, with the third-party lender and CDC providing the remaining financing under the program's structure. Some projects can require more borrower equity.

Conventional commercial loans can have different equity requirements determined by the individual lender.

A larger down payment can reduce the amount borrowed, but business owners should avoid putting so much cash into the property that the company lacks sufficient working capital or reserves.

What Lenders Look For

Commercial real estate underwriting usually goes beyond the borrower's personal credit score.

Expect lenders to examine the business's financial performance and the property's economics.

Common documentation can include:

  • Business tax returns
  • Personal tax returns
  • Profit-and-loss statements
  • Balance sheets
  • Bank statements
  • Business debt schedule
  • Personal financial statement
  • Purchase contract
  • Property appraisal
  • Environmental reports when applicable
  • Existing leases
  • Property operating statements
  • Business projections
  • Details about the proposed use of the property

SBA lenders also evaluate whether the business is eligible, creditworthy, and capable of repaying the loan.

Debt Service and Business Cash Flow

One of the most important questions is whether the business can comfortably service the proposed debt.

Imagine a company currently paying $12,000 per month in rent. It is considering purchasing a building with an estimated monthly debt payment of $15,000.

The business needs to evaluate more than the $3,000 difference.

Ownership can introduce additional expenses such as:

  • Property taxes
  • Commercial property insurance
  • Repairs
  • Maintenance
  • Utilities
  • Landscaping
  • Property management
  • Capital expenditures

The company should therefore compare the total cost of occupancy, not simply rent versus mortgage payment.

Owner-Occupied vs. Investment Property

The intended use of the property can dramatically affect financing options.

An owner-occupied building is used by the business itself. Examples include a dental practice purchasing its clinic, a manufacturer purchasing a factory, or a contractor purchasing a warehouse.

An investment property is primarily acquired to generate rental income.

SBA programs are focused on eligible operating businesses, and the 504 program specifically excludes speculative or investment rental real estate.

Businesses purchasing investment properties may therefore need conventional commercial financing or specialized commercial real estate investment loans.

A 2026 SBA Financing Change to Know

SBA policy changed in July 2026 regarding the cumulative availability of 7(a) and 504 financing.

Effective July 4, 2026, eligible borrowers can combine 7(a) and 504 loans for up to $10 million in cumulative SBA-backed financing, subject to program requirements. Previously, the cumulative limit was $5 million.

This does not mean every small business can automatically borrow $10 million. Each individual program has its own requirements, and the business must satisfy SBA eligibility and lender underwriting standards.

For a larger commercial property project, however, the change can be relevant when determining how an SBA financing package could be structured.

How to Compare Commercial Real Estate Loans

When evaluating financing proposals, compare more than the advertised interest rate.

Look at:

  • Loan amount
  • Interest rate
  • Fixed or variable rate
  • APR when available
  • Amortization period
  • Maturity date
  • Monthly payment
  • Down payment
  • Origination fees
  • SBA guarantee or other program fees
  • Appraisal costs
  • Environmental review costs
  • Prepayment penalties
  • Balloon payment requirements
  • Collateral requirements
  • Personal guarantees
  • Working-capital requirements

A loan with a slightly lower rate can still have a higher overall cost if it includes significant fees or a shorter maturity.

Steps to Finance a Commercial Property

A practical process can look like this:

  1. Determine the property's intended use.
  2. Estimate the total project cost, including renovations and transaction expenses.
  3. Calculate the amount of cash the business can contribute.
  4. Prepare business and personal financial documentation.
  5. Estimate the property's operating costs.
  6. Calculate the business's ability to service the proposed debt.
  7. Compare conventional, 7(a), and 504 financing where applicable.
  8. Request proposals from multiple lenders.
  9. Review the complete loan structure, not just the interest rate.
  10. Confirm the financing before committing to the property purchase.

For SBA financing, the SBA's Lender Match tool can connect eligible businesses with participating lenders. The SBA recommends discussing rates, credit requirements, cash-flow requirements, prepayment provisions, and other loan terms directly with lenders.

Final Considerations

Commercial real estate financing can help small businesses move from leasing space to owning their operating property, expand into a larger facility, or finance construction and improvements.

The major financing paths include SBA 7(a), SBA 504, conventional commercial mortgages, construction loans, and bridge financing. Each has different requirements, costs, repayment structures, and permitted uses.

For an eligible owner-occupied business, SBA financing can provide an important alternative to conventional commercial lending. A 7(a) loan offers flexibility for projects involving real estate and other business expenses, while a 504 loan is structured around major fixed assets and long-term financing.

Before choosing a loan, calculate the property's full cost of ownership, preserve adequate business liquidity, and compare the interest rate, fees, maturity, collateral requirements, and repayment structure. The goal is not simply to finance the building, but to structure the debt so the business can continue operating comfortably after the purchase.
