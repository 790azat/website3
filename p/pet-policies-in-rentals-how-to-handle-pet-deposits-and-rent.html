Pet Policies in Rentals: How to Handle Pet Deposits and Rent

Allowing pets can expand the pool of potential renters, but it also creates additional considerations for property managers. Animals can contribute to wear, odors, scratched surfaces, landscaping damage, and other maintenance issues. A clearly written pet policy can establish expectations while giving landlords a consistent framework for handling deposits, recurring pet rent, property damage, and tenant responsibilities.

The important distinction is that ordinary pets and assistance animals are not treated the same under federal fair-housing law. A landlord's standard pet policy generally applies to pets, while an assistance animal may qualify for a reasonable accommodation and cannot simply be treated as a pet.

State and local laws can also regulate pet deposits, fees, and security deposits, so landlords should verify the rules applicable to each property.

Start With a Written Pet Policy

A pet policy should be part of the leasing process rather than an informal agreement made after a tenant moves in.

The policy can address:

  • Whether pets are permitted
  • Permitted types of animals
  • Number of pets
  • Size or weight restrictions where legally permitted
  • Pet deposits
  • Pet fees
  • Monthly pet rent
  • Cleaning requirements
  • Damage responsibility
  • Noise and nuisance rules
  • Waste disposal
  • Leash requirements
  • Vaccination or licensing requirements where applicable
  • Rules for common areas

The policy should be applied consistently to similarly situated tenants.

If a property has different rules for different buildings or unit types, those differences should be documented clearly rather than communicated informally.

Pet Deposit vs. Pet Fee vs. Pet Rent

These terms describe different financial arrangements.

Pet Deposit

A pet deposit is generally refundable, subject to applicable law and the condition of the property at move-out.

It is typically intended to provide financial protection against damage attributable to the animal.

State law can regulate the amount, handling, and return of deposits. In some jurisdictions, a separate pet deposit may be subject to the same statutory security-deposit limits as the standard deposit.

Pet Fee

A pet fee is generally a one-time charge that is not intended to be returned.

Whether a landlord can charge one, and how much can be charged, depends on applicable law.

Landlords should not assume that simply calling a payment a "fee" makes it exempt from security-deposit regulations. Some states regulate the total amount that can be collected from a tenant regardless of the label attached to the charge.

Monthly Pet Rent

Pet rent is a recurring charge added to the monthly rental payment.

It can provide an ongoing source of revenue associated with allowing pets, but state and local laws may regulate the amount or whether such charges are permitted.

The lease should clearly state the amount and when it becomes due.

How Much Should a Pet Deposit Be?

There is no nationwide pet-deposit amount that applies to every private rental.

Instead, landlords should first determine the legal maximum applicable to the property.

State laws can establish limits on security deposits and may determine whether pet deposits count toward those limits. Nolo's current state-law analysis notes that some jurisdictions allow separate pet deposits while others impose restrictions that affect how much can be collected in total.

After determining the legal limit, consider the property's actual risk profile.

A large single-family rental with expensive hardwood floors and extensive landscaping may present different potential costs from a small apartment with durable flooring.

The amount should be based on a documented policy rather than arbitrary decisions between tenants.

Should Landlords Charge Pet Rent?

Pet rent can be useful when a landlord wants to account for ongoing costs associated with allowing animals.

For example, a property may experience additional:

  • Carpet cleaning
  • Common-area cleaning
  • Landscaping
  • Pest-control requirements
  • Routine wear
  • Administrative work

However, monthly pet rent should not be treated as a substitute for compliance with security-deposit rules or damage requirements.

The landlord should also calculate whether the additional monthly revenue actually corresponds to the property's operating costs and local market conditions.

Document the Pet Before Move-In

Documentation is particularly important when a landlord may later need to determine whether damage occurred during the tenancy.

Before move-in, record:

  • Type of animal
  • Breed or description, where appropriate
  • Approximate age
  • Number of animals
  • Existing property condition
  • Flooring condition
  • Doors and trim
  • Walls
  • Yard or landscaping
  • Furniture, if the property is furnished

Use dated photographs or video in addition to written inspection notes.

A detailed move-in record can help distinguish pre-existing conditions from damage discovered after the tenant leaves. It can also support consistent security-deposit accounting where permitted by law.

Define the Tenant's Pet Responsibilities

A pet addendum should explain what the tenant is responsible for.

Potential provisions include:

  • Keeping the animal under control
  • Cleaning animal waste
  • Preventing damage
  • Maintaining required licenses
  • Following applicable animal-control laws
  • Preventing excessive noise
  • Keeping pets from damaging landscaping
  • Reporting property damage
  • Following building or community rules

The agreement can also establish procedures for unauthorized animals.

For example, if a tenant brings an unapproved pet into the property, the lease should explain the process for correcting the violation rather than relying on an informal response.

Address Pet Damage Separately From Normal Wear

A pet deposit should not automatically become a source of additional income when a tenant moves out.

The landlord should determine whether a particular condition represents ordinary wear, tenant-caused damage, or routine property maintenance.

Examples of potentially pet-related damage could include:

  • Deep scratches in doors
  • Chewed trim
  • Significant carpet staining
  • Damaged flooring
  • Extensive yard damage
  • Persistent odors requiring remediation

By contrast, ordinary aging of a property should not automatically be attributed to the animal.

Documentation from the move-in inspection is therefore particularly valuable.

Assistance Animals Are Different From Pets

This is one of the most important compliance issues in a rental pet policy.

HUD explains that an assistance animal is not a pet. A person with a disability may request an assistance animal as a reasonable accommodation to a housing provider's pet restrictions.

Where the Fair Housing Act's requirements are satisfied, a housing provider may need to allow the animal even when the property has a no-pet policy.

A reasonable accommodation may also involve waiving a pet deposit, fee, or other pet-related rule. HUD specifically identifies waiver of a pet deposit or fee as an example of a possible reasonable accommodation.

Therefore, landlords should not automatically charge pet rent or a pet deposit for an assistance animal.

Do Not Apply Pet Rules Automatically to Assistance Animals

HUD's guidance also distinguishes assistance animals from ordinary pets when evaluating restrictions.

A housing provider generally cannot impose ordinary pet restrictions simply because the animal is an assistance animal. However, the accommodation framework does not mean every animal or every request must automatically be accepted under every circumstance.

HUD identifies circumstances involving factors such as an undue financial or administrative burden, fundamental alteration, direct threat, or significant property damage that can be relevant to reasonable-accommodation analysis.

Because these situations can be legally sensitive, landlords should use an established accommodation procedure rather than making an immediate decision based on the animal's breed, appearance, or the tenant's disability.

What About Damage Caused by an Assistance Animal?

Not charging a pet fee does not necessarily mean the tenant is exempt from responsibility for actual property damage.

HUD's assistance-animal guidance explains that a housing provider may charge for damage caused by an assistance animal when the provider normally charges tenants for comparable damage, including through the standard security deposit.

The distinction is important:

Pet charge: A fee or deposit imposed simply because an animal is present.

Damage charge: A legitimate charge for actual tenant-caused property damage under applicable law.

Landlords should document the damage and follow the same standards used for other tenants.

Create a Consistent Approval Process

A scalable rental operation should have a written procedure for reviewing pets.

For example:

  1. Tenant identifies the animal.
  2. Management reviews the property's pet policy.
  3. Required documentation is collected.
  4. Applicable state and local rules are checked.
  5. Pet approval or applicable accommodation procedures are completed.
  6. The lease or pet addendum is executed.
  7. Any legally permitted deposit or fee is collected.
  8. The animal is added to the property's records.

Consistency is important because arbitrary decisions can create both operational and legal problems.

Include the Policy in the Lease

Do not rely on verbal agreements.

The lease or pet addendum should clearly identify:

  • Approved animal
  • Number of animals
  • Pet-related charges
  • Payment schedule
  • Tenant responsibilities
  • Property rules
  • Damage provisions
  • Unauthorized-animal procedures
  • Applicable accommodation language

HUD itself advises renters to clarify pet costs and have pet rules included in the lease or pet agreement.

Review State and Local Requirements

Pet policies are not governed solely by federal law.

State and local rules can affect:

  • Security-deposit limits
  • Pet-deposit limits
  • Nonrefundable fees
  • Monthly pet rent
  • Deposit handling
  • Refund deadlines
  • Rent-control requirements
  • Housing-code requirements
  • Assistance-animal procedures

For example, current state-law guidance identifies different approaches to pet deposits and pet rent, including jurisdictions that impose specific limits on recurring pet charges.

A policy that is appropriate for one property may therefore need to be modified for another jurisdiction.

Final Considerations

A well-designed pet policy protects the property's physical condition while giving tenants clear expectations about animals and related costs.

For ordinary pets, landlords can establish reasonable rules and, where permitted, use pet deposits, fees, or monthly pet rent. Those charges should be structured according to applicable state and local requirements rather than simply copied from another property's lease.

Assistance animals require a separate legal analysis. Under the Fair Housing Act, they are not considered pets, and qualifying reasonable accommodations can require landlords to waive pet restrictions and related charges.

The most practical approach is to maintain a written, consistently applied pet policy, document property condition before and after each tenancy, distinguish actual damage from ordinary wear, and review the policy whenever applicable laws change.
