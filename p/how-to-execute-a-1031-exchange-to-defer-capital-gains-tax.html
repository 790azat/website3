How to Execute a 1031 Exchange to Defer Capital Gains Tax

A 1031 exchange can allow a real estate investor or business owner to defer recognition of capital gain when selling qualifying real property and acquiring other qualifying real property. The rules come from Section 1031 of the Internal Revenue Code and are particularly relevant when an investor wants to move equity from one investment property into another without triggering the full federal tax on the gain at the time of the sale.

A 1031 exchange is not simply a matter of selling one property and using the proceeds to purchase another. The transaction must meet specific requirements involving the type of property, how the proceeds are handled, identification deadlines, and the timing of the replacement purchase.

Because a failed exchange can result in unexpected tax liability, investors should generally involve a qualified intermediary and tax professional before the relinquished property closes.

What Is a 1031 Exchange?

Section 1031 generally allows qualifying real property held for investment or productive use in a trade or business to be exchanged for other real property held for investment or productive business use without immediately recognizing the entire gain.

The tax is generally deferred rather than permanently eliminated. The tax basis of the old property generally carries into the replacement property, subject to adjustments. This means the deferred gain can affect the taxable gain recognized when the replacement property is eventually sold.

For example, suppose an investor owns a rental property with an adjusted tax basis of $300,000 and sells it for $600,000.

The transaction produces a substantial realized gain. If the investor completes a qualifying 1031 exchange, the gain may generally be deferred rather than immediately recognized.

The replacement property effectively carries forward the tax consequences of the previous investment.

What Property Qualifies?

Since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, Section 1031 applies to real property, rather than personal or intangible property. The property being sold and the replacement property generally must both be held for investment or for productive use in a trade or business.

Examples of potentially qualifying real estate can include:

  • Rental houses
  • Apartment buildings
  • Commercial buildings
  • Office properties
  • Industrial properties
  • Retail properties
  • Investment land
  • Certain other qualifying real property

A personal residence generally does not qualify simply because it is real estate. Property held primarily for sale, such as inventory held by a real estate dealer, is also generally excluded from Section 1031 treatment.

What Does "Like-Kind" Mean?

Like-kind does not mean that the replacement property has to be identical to the property you sold.

For real estate, the definition is relatively broad. Qualifying investment real property can generally be exchanged for other qualifying investment real property even when the properties have different characteristics or uses.

For example, an investor may potentially exchange:

Rental house → Apartment building

or:

Commercial property → Investment land

The important issue is generally whether the properties qualify as real property held for the required business or investment purposes, rather than whether they are the same type of building.

The 45-Day Identification Rule

One of the most important deadlines in a 1031 exchange is the 45-day identification period.

After transferring the property being sold, the investor generally has 45 days to identify potential replacement property in writing. The identification must clearly describe the replacement property and be delivered to the appropriate person involved in the exchange.

For real estate, the identification can generally use a legal description, street address, or other sufficiently distinguishable description.

The deadline is strict, so investors should begin evaluating replacement properties before selling the original property.

How Many Properties Can You Identify?

The IRS generally allows investors to identify:

  • Up to three replacement properties, regardless of their value; or
  • Any number of properties whose combined fair market value does not exceed 200% of the value of the relinquished property, subject to the applicable rules.

A third identification method, sometimes called the 95% rule, can apply when the investor identifies more properties than permitted under the standard identification rules but ultimately acquires property meeting the applicable value requirement.

Because these rules are technical, investors should have the identification prepared and reviewed before the deadline.

The 180-Day Exchange Deadline

Identifying a replacement property is only the first deadline.

The investor generally must receive the replacement property by the earlier of:

  • 180 days after transferring the relinquished property, or
  • The due date, including extensions, of the investor's federal tax return for the year of the transfer.

This means the full 180 days is not always available.

For example, if the transaction occurs late in the tax year, the investor's tax-return due date can become the earlier deadline unless an extension provides additional time.

Why You Need a Qualified Intermediary

In a standard deferred exchange, the investor generally should not receive the sale proceeds directly.

A qualified intermediary (QI) can facilitate the transaction by holding the proceeds and using them toward the acquisition of qualifying replacement property. The IRS recognizes qualified intermediaries as one of the safe-harbor arrangements used to prevent the taxpayer from having actual or constructive receipt of the exchange proceeds.

The QI should generally be involved before the sale of the relinquished property closes.

Waiting until after receiving the proceeds can create serious problems because the transaction may be treated as a sale rather than a qualifying deferred exchange.

Step-by-Step 1031 Exchange Process

A typical deferred exchange can be organized into the following steps.

1. Determine whether the property qualifies

Confirm that the property being sold is real property held for investment or productive business use.

Also determine whether the intended replacement property satisfies the same general requirements.

2. Calculate the potential gain

Work with a tax professional to determine the property's adjusted basis, selling expenses, depreciation history, debt, and estimated gain.

This helps establish how much tax could otherwise become due.

3. Select a qualified intermediary

Choose the QI before the relinquished property is transferred.

The intermediary will establish the exchange structure and explain how the sale proceeds will be handled.

4. Sell the relinquished property

The sale documents should be structured so that the exchange is properly documented and the proceeds are transferred to the qualified intermediary rather than directly to you.

5. Identify replacement property

Within 45 days, provide the required written identification.

Consider identifying backup properties when appropriate because a transaction can fail if your first choice becomes unavailable.

6. Complete the replacement purchase

Acquire qualifying replacement property within the applicable 180-day exchange period.

7. Report the exchange

A qualifying exchange must still be reported to the IRS even when no gain is recognized.

Form 8824, Like-Kind Exchanges, is used to report the exchange.

What Is "Boot" in a 1031 Exchange?

A common misconception is that every dollar of gain automatically disappears when a 1031 exchange is completed.

If the investor receives money or other non-like-kind property as part of the transaction, the gain may be recognized to the extent of that non-like-kind property, commonly referred to as boot.

For example, an investor sells qualifying property for $800,000 and acquires qualifying replacement property worth $700,000 while receiving $100,000 in cash.

The cash can create taxable gain to the extent provided under the applicable rules.

The calculation can become more complicated when mortgages and other liabilities are involved.

Debt and the 1031 Exchange

Mortgage debt deserves special attention.

Suppose an investor sells a property for $1 million and has a $400,000 mortgage secured by the property. The replacement property may also involve financing.

A reduction in liabilities, combined with cash received, can potentially contribute to recognized gain unless appropriately offset under the tax rules.

The IRS specifically treats certain liabilities assumed by another party or associated with the transferred property as part of the consideration for purposes of calculating recognized gain, subject to applicable offsets.

Because debt replacement calculations can become complicated quickly, investors should have the proposed transaction reviewed before closing.

Does the Replacement Property Have to Cost More?

There is no simple rule that says the replacement property must always cost more than the property sold.

An investor can acquire a replacement property of lesser value, but receiving cash or other non-like-kind property can result in recognized gain to the extent of the applicable amount.

Investors seeking full deferral generally need to structure the transaction carefully rather than simply comparing purchase prices.

The amount reinvested, liabilities, cash received, transaction expenses, and adjusted basis can all affect the final tax calculation.

What Happens to Your Tax Basis?

A 1031 exchange generally carries the tax basis of the relinquished property into the replacement property, with adjustments for items such as additional costs, liabilities, recognized gain, and money or property received.

This is why a 1031 exchange is generally described as tax deferral, not tax elimination.

Consider a simplified example:

  • Original adjusted basis: $300,000
  • Sale value: $600,000
  • Realized gain: $300,000
  • Replacement property: $600,000

If the exchange qualifies for full nonrecognition, the $300,000 gain generally is not immediately recognized. Instead, the replacement property's tax basis reflects the deferred tax consequences.

When that replacement property is eventually sold in a taxable transaction, the deferred gain can become relevant.

What About Depreciation?

Investors who own depreciable rental or commercial property should pay particular attention to depreciation.

Depreciation affects the property's adjusted tax basis and therefore can affect the gain recognized on a later disposition.

A 1031 exchange can defer gain, but it does not mean that all previous depreciation-related tax consequences simply disappear.

The tax treatment can depend on the property's history, basis, exchange structure, and subsequent transactions.

A tax professional should calculate the potential depreciation-related consequences before the sale.

Can You Exchange Land for a Building?

Potentially, yes.

Because Section 1031 focuses on qualifying real property rather than requiring identical property types, investment land can potentially be exchanged for another qualifying investment property, such as a commercial building.

Similarly, a rental property can potentially be exchanged for investment land.

The critical requirements are the property's qualifying status and the investor's compliance with the applicable exchange rules.

Common 1031 Exchange Mistakes

Several errors can cause an exchange to fail or create unexpected tax liability.

Receiving the sale proceeds directly

The investor should generally establish the exchange structure before closing and avoid actual or constructive receipt of the proceeds.

Missing the 45-day deadline

A replacement property must generally be identified within 45 days.

Missing the 180-day deadline

The replacement property must generally be received by the earlier applicable deadline.

Buying property that does not qualify

Personal-use property and property held primarily for sale generally do not qualify.

Assuming equal value automatically means full deferral

Cash, non-like-kind property, debt changes, and other transaction details can affect recognized gain.

Waiting until after the sale to arrange the exchange

The exchange structure should be established before the relinquished property is transferred.

How to Plan a 1031 Exchange

Ideally, planning begins before listing the property for sale.

Start by determining:

  • Adjusted tax basis
  • Estimated sale price
  • Selling expenses
  • Existing mortgage balance
  • Depreciation history
  • Estimated gain
  • Desired replacement property
  • Available financing
  • Required cash reserves
  • Potential transaction costs

Then coordinate the real estate agent, lender, qualified intermediary, attorney, and tax professional so that the transaction is structured consistently from the beginning.

This is especially important when the replacement property requires financing, renovations, or other complicated closing arrangements.

Final Considerations

A 1031 exchange can be a valuable strategy for investors who want to move from one qualifying real estate investment to another while deferring recognition of taxable gain.

The process is highly deadline-driven. The replacement property generally must be identified within 45 days and received within the applicable 180-day period, while the proceeds should generally remain within the exchange structure rather than being received directly by the investor.

The transaction also needs to involve qualifying real property held for investment or productive business use. Receiving cash or other non-like-kind property can result in partial gain recognition, meaning a 1031 exchange does not automatically make every transaction completely tax-free.

Before selling, calculate the potential gain, establish the exchange with a qualified intermediary, identify replacement properties in advance, and have the transaction reviewed by a qualified tax professional. Proper planning is particularly important when mortgages, depreciation, multiple properties, or significant cash proceeds are involved.
