Your credit score can influence both your ability to qualify for a mortgage and the interest rate a lender offers. In general, borrowers with stronger credit profiles tend to receive more favorable mortgage pricing, while borrowers with lower scores may face higher rates, additional requirements, or fewer loan options. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/))

Mortgage pricing is not based on credit score alone. Lenders can also consider the loan-to-value ratio (LTV), loan type, property, debt-to-income ratio (DTI), loan term, occupancy, assets, and other characteristics.

Understanding how credit scores fit into mortgage pricing can help you estimate your potential rate tier and identify steps that may improve your financing options before applying.

## What Is a Mortgage Credit Score?
A credit score is a numerical representation derived from information in your credit reports. Different scoring models can produce different scores for the same borrower.

Mortgage lenders may use specialized credit-scoring models and information from multiple credit-reporting companies. The CFPB notes that most mortgage lenders have historically looked at scores from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion when evaluating mortgage applications, although the scoring models used in mortgage lending are evolving. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/))

Fannie Mae's 2026 Selling Guide also reflects changes in approved credit-score models, including the addition of VantageScore 4.0 and FICO Score 10T for certain deliveries and implementations. ([singlefamily.fanniemae.com](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/selling-policy-communications))

This means the score you see through a consumer credit-card application or personal-finance website may not necessarily be the exact score a mortgage lender uses.

## How Credit Scores Affect Mortgage Pricing
Mortgage lenders use risk-based pricing, meaning loan terms can vary based on characteristics associated with repayment risk.

The CFPB states that higher credit scores generally make borrowers eligible for lower interest rates, although credit score is only one component of the lender's decision. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/))

For conventional loans sold to Fannie Mae, credit score can also affect **loan-level price adjustments (LLPAs)**. These adjustments are part of the pricing framework lenders use when determining the cost of eligible loans. The applicable adjustment can depend on credit score and other characteristics, including LTV. ([singlefamily.fanniemae.com](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/document/pdf/selling-guide-march-6-2024))

As a result, two borrowers applying for otherwise similar mortgages can receive different pricing.

## Typical Credit Score Tiers
There is no universal set of mortgage interest-rate tiers used by every lender.

However, the CFPB provides general guidance on how credit ranges can affect mortgage pricing. Its consumer guidance states that borrowers with scores in the **mid- to high-700s or above** generally receive the lowest rates, while borrowers in the **680–740** range typically pay somewhat higher rates. Borrowers in the **620–680** range generally face higher rates and fewer choices, while scores below 620 can make qualification more difficult depending on the loan type and lender. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/prepare/get-your-money-situation-in-order/))

These ranges are useful for understanding the general relationship, but they should not be treated as fixed industry rate tiers.

A lender could price a 739 score differently from another lender, and the difference between 739 and 740 does not automatically produce a specific interest-rate change.

## Why the Same Credit Score Can Produce Different Rates
Credit score is only one part of mortgage pricing.

Consider two borrowers who both have a 740 credit score. Their mortgage offers can still differ because one borrower may have:

  - A larger down payment
  - Lower DTI
  - A smaller loan amount
  - A different property type
  - A primary residence instead of an investment property
  - A shorter loan term
  - A different loan program

Loan-to-value is particularly important in conventional mortgage pricing. Fannie Mae's pricing matrix incorporates both credit score and LTV when determining applicable loan-level price adjustments. ([singlefamily.fanniemae.com](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/9391/display?mibextid=Zxz2cZ))

Therefore, improving your credit score is only one potential way to improve your mortgage pricing.

## Credit Score and Down Payment Work Together
Your credit score and down payment can interact in mortgage pricing.

A larger down payment generally reduces the LTV ratio.

For example:

**$400,000 home with $20,000 down**

Loan amount: $380,000
LTV: 95%

Compared with:

**$400,000 home with $80,000 down**

Loan amount: $320,000
LTV: 80%

Both borrowers could have the same credit score, but their loan characteristics are different.

Conventional pricing matrices can apply different adjustments based on the combination of credit score and LTV. ([singlefamily.fanniemae.com](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/9391/display?mibextid=Zxz2cZ))

This is why a borrower should not evaluate mortgage pricing based on credit score alone.

## Credit Score and Mortgage Insurance
Credit can also interact with mortgage insurance requirements and pricing when the borrower has a higher LTV.

For example, a conventional borrower making a relatively small down payment may need private mortgage insurance (PMI). The cost of that insurance can vary based on borrower and loan characteristics, including credit-related factors.

The result is that credit can affect the overall cost of borrowing through more than the mortgage interest rate itself.

When comparing offers, look at the interest rate, mortgage insurance, lender fees, points, and APR rather than focusing on one number.

## How Much Can a Credit Score Change Your Mortgage Cost?
Even a relatively small difference in mortgage pricing can affect the total interest paid over a long loan term.

The CFPB provides an example showing how changing the credit score from 625 to 700 can produce different ranges of mortgage offers and significantly different potential interest costs under its hypothetical assumptions. The CFPB emphasizes that actual offers depend on the borrower's circumstances and market conditions. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/explore-rates/))

Consider an illustrative example:

A borrower takes a **$320,000 30-year fixed mortgage**.

At 6.50%, the principal-and-interest payment is approximately **$2,023 per month**.

At 7.00%, it is approximately **$2,129 per month**.

The difference is about **$106 per month**, or roughly $1,272 per year.

This example is purely mathematical and does not represent current mortgage offers or a specific credit-score tier. Actual rates depend on market conditions and the complete loan profile.

## Does a 20-Point Credit Increase Matter?
It can, but there is no universal answer.

Mortgage pricing is not necessarily adjusted for every single point. Pricing systems often use ranges, and moving from one pricing range to another may have a larger effect than a small increase that does not cross a relevant threshold.

For example, improving a score from 680 to 681 may not materially change an offer, while moving into a different pricing category could potentially affect pricing.

The applicable thresholds vary by lender and loan program.

This is one reason borrowers should not assume that there is a specific "magic number" that automatically produces a lower mortgage rate.

## How to Improve Your Credit Before Applying
If you are planning to apply for a mortgage, several credit-management practices can help protect or improve your profile.

### Pay Bills on Time
Payment history is an important component of credit scoring models. Missed or late payments can negatively affect your credit profile.

### Reduce Revolving Balances
Credit utilization—the amount of revolving credit you are using relative to available credit—can affect credit scores. Reducing balances can potentially improve a score, although the effect varies by scoring model and individual credit profile.

### Avoid Unnecessary New Credit
Opening new credit accounts can affect your credit profile and generate hard inquiries.

The CFPB recommends avoiding unnecessary new credit applications when preparing for a mortgage. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-happens-when-a-mortgage-lender-checks-my-credit-en-2005/))

### Check Your Credit Reports
Review your credit reports for inaccurate information before applying.

An incorrect late payment, collection account, or other error could negatively affect your score and potentially increase the cost of borrowing.

The CFPB recommends checking credit reports and disputing inaccurate information. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/does-my-credit-score-affect-my-ability-to-get-a-mortgage-loan-or-the-mortgage-rate-i-pay-en-319/))

## Don't Close Old Accounts Without Considering the Effect
Closing an old credit account can sometimes affect your credit profile.

For example, eliminating an available credit line could increase your overall utilization ratio if you continue carrying similar balances on other accounts.

Before closing accounts shortly before a mortgage application, consider discussing the potential effect with a qualified financial professional or reviewing your credit profile carefully.

The objective is not simply to have fewer accounts but to maintain a stable credit profile while preparing for the mortgage.

## Mortgage Rate Shopping Usually Doesn't Require Avoiding All Credit Inquiries
Shopping for a mortgage does involve credit inquiries, but consumers generally should not avoid comparing lenders simply because they are concerned about multiple mortgage inquiries.

The CFPB states that multiple mortgage credit checks made within a **45-day window** are generally recorded as a single inquiry for scoring purposes. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-exactly-happens-when-a-mortgage-lender-checks-my-credit-en-2005/))

This gives borrowers an opportunity to compare mortgage offers without treating every lender inquiry as a separate scoring event.

However, applying for unrelated credit cards, auto loans, or other new credit during the mortgage process can create additional inquiries and change your debt profile.

## Credit Score vs. APR
When comparing mortgage offers, do not look only at the advertised interest rate.

The **interest rate** represents the cost of borrowing expressed as a percentage.

The **annual percentage rate (APR)** incorporates the interest rate along with certain points, fees, and other loan charges, providing a broader measure of borrowing cost. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-the-difference-between-a-mortgage-interest-rate-and-an-apr-en-135/))

For example, one lender might offer:

**6.50% interest rate + higher upfront fees**

while another offers:

**6.625% interest rate + lower upfront fees**

The second option could potentially have a different overall cost depending on the loan structure and how long you expect to keep the mortgage.

Compare Loan Estimates rather than relying on an advertised rate.

## Can Government Loans Work Differently?
Credit-score requirements and pricing can differ substantially between conventional and government-backed mortgages.

FHA, VA, and USDA programs have their own eligibility and underwriting structures. The CFPB notes that government programs can provide options for some borrowers who may have difficulty qualifying for conventional financing, particularly those with lower credit scores or smaller down payments. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/prepare/get-your-money-situation-in-order/))

However, a lower credit-score requirement does not automatically mean a government-backed mortgage will have a lower total cost.

Mortgage insurance or guarantee fees, upfront costs, funding fees, and other program-specific expenses should be included in the comparison.

## What If Your Credit Score Changes Before Closing?
Mortgage lenders generally verify financial information throughout the loan process.

A significant change in credit profile, new debt, missed payment, or other financial event can affect underwriting or pricing.

For this reason, avoid making major financial changes during the mortgage process without discussing them with your lender.

A new credit card or auto loan may change both your credit profile and DTI, potentially affecting the application.

## How to Compare Mortgage Offers by Credit Tier
Instead of asking only, "What rate can I get with my credit score?" ask lenders for a complete Loan Estimate based on the same assumptions.

Compare:

1.  Interest rate
2.  APR
3.  Points
4.  Lender credits
5.  Origination charges
6.  Mortgage insurance
7.  Monthly principal and interest
8.  Total monthly payment
9.  Cash required to close
10. Five-year cost of borrowing

Using the same loan amount, down payment, property type, term, and points or credits makes comparisons more meaningful.

The CFPB recommends comparing loan offers and understanding how points and lender credits affect the interest rate and upfront costs. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-should-i-use-lender-credits-and-points-also-called-discount-points-en-136/))

## Final Considerations
Credit score can play an important role in determining mortgage eligibility and pricing, but there is no single nationwide set of interest-rate tiers that applies to every borrower.

Generally, higher credit scores are associated with more favorable mortgage pricing, while lower scores can result in higher rates, additional requirements, or fewer loan options. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/owning-a-home/prepare/get-your-money-situation-in-order/))

For conventional mortgages, credit score can interact with factors such as LTV when determining loan-level pricing adjustments. Other characteristics—including loan type, property type, DTI, loan term, occupancy, and lender pricing—can also affect the final offer. ([singlefamily.fanniemae.com](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/9391/display?mibextid=Zxz2cZ))

Before applying, review your credit reports, avoid unnecessary new debt, and compare offers from multiple lenders. When evaluating the final options, look beyond the interest rate and compare APR, points, lender credits, mortgage insurance, closing costs, and the expected total cost of the loan.
