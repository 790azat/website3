Buying a home often means dealing with two different types of protection: **homeowners insurance** and a **home warranty**. They may sound similar, but they cover different risks.

Homeowners insurance is designed primarily to protect against covered damage to the home, belongings, and liability. A home warranty—more accurately described in many cases as a **service contract**—is designed to help pay for repairs or replacement of specified household systems and appliances when they break down.

The distinction matters because having homeowners insurance does not necessarily mean your aging air conditioner, refrigerator, or plumbing system will be repaired when it fails. Likewise, a home warranty generally does not replace insurance coverage for events such as fires, theft, or covered storm damage.

## What Does Homeowners Insurance Cover?
Homeowners insurance is an insurance policy that protects against specified risks covered by the policy. According to the National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), typical homeowners policies can include coverage for the dwelling, other structures, personal property, loss of use, personal liability, and medical payments.

Common examples include:

  - Fire damage to the house
  - Certain wind or hail damage
  - Theft of covered personal belongings
  - Damage to covered structures from specified perils
  - Additional living expenses after a covered loss makes the home temporarily uninhabitable
  - Certain liability claims if someone is injured and the homeowner is legally responsible

Coverage depends on the specific policy, limits, deductibles, and exclusions.

For example, if a covered fire damages your kitchen, homeowners insurance may pay for covered repairs after the deductible, subject to the policy's terms and limits.

However, if your 15-year-old refrigerator simply stops working because of mechanical failure, homeowners insurance generally isn't intended to function as an appliance repair plan.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau also notes that mortgage lenders generally require homeowners insurance because the property securing the mortgage needs to be protected.

## What Is a Home Warranty?
A home warranty is commonly a **service contract** covering specified appliances or home systems for a defined period.

The FTC explains that home warranties can help cover repairs or replacements for items such as appliances and air-conditioning systems, but they cost extra and their coverage varies by contract.

Depending on the plan, covered items may include:

  - HVAC systems
  - Electrical systems
  - Plumbing systems
  - Water heaters
  - Refrigerators
  - Ovens and ranges
  - Dishwashers
  - Washing machines
  - Dryers
  - Garage door openers

The exact list can differ substantially between providers and plans.

A home warranty generally addresses **breakdowns**, rather than damage caused by a covered catastrophe.

For example, suppose your water heater stops working because of an internal mechanical failure. If the water heater is covered under your service contract and the failure meets the contract's conditions, the warranty company may arrange or pay for an eligible repair or replacement, subject to applicable service fees, limits, and exclusions.

The NAIC specifically describes home warranties as covering mechanical breakdowns of individual parts of a home and notes that they generally do not cover the home's structure.

## Home Warranty vs. Homeowners Insurance
|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| \*\*Feature\*\* | \*\*Homeowners Insurance\*\* | \*\*Home Warranty\*\* |
| Primary purpose | Protect against covered property losses and liability | Help with covered system/appliance breakdowns |
| House structure | Yes, for covered perils | Generally no |
| Personal belongings | Typically yes, for covered losses | Generally no |
| Fire damage | Typically covered subject to policy | Generally not |
| Theft | Typically covered subject to policy | Generally not |
| HVAC breakdown | Usually not due to ordinary mechanical failure | May be covered |
| Appliance breakdown | Usually not ordinary wear/failure | May be covered |
| Liability protection | Yes | No |
| Mortgage requirement | Generally required by lenders | Not generally required |
| Deductible/service fee | Insurance deductible | Often a service fee per repair visit |
| Contract type | Insurance policy | Service contract |

The biggest distinction is **what causes the loss**.

Insurance generally responds to covered risks or “perils,” while a home warranty is generally concerned with specified mechanical failures and service needs.

## Does Homeowners Insurance Cover Normal Wear and Tear?
Generally, no.

Normal deterioration and mechanical wear are fundamentally different from sudden damage caused by an insured peril.

Consider an older roof. If the roof gradually deteriorates because of age, homeowners insurance generally isn't a substitute for routine maintenance or replacement due to wear and tear.

But if a covered storm causes sudden damage, the resulting claim may be treated differently depending on the policy.

This is why homeowners should not assume that every expensive home repair belongs under their insurance policy.

The NAIC notes that homeowners policies pay for damage caused by covered perils and that exclusions and policy terms determine what is actually covered.

## Does a Home Warranty Cover Everything That Breaks?
No.

This is one of the most important points to understand before buying a home warranty.

A service contract covers only the items, failures, conditions, and circumstances specified in its terms.

The FTC recommends examining the contract carefully because service contracts may cover only part of a product, contain exclusions, impose service fees, or provide less protection than consumers expect.

For example, a warranty could cover an HVAC system but exclude:

  - Certain components
  - Pre-existing problems
  - Improper installation
  - Lack of maintenance
  - Code-related upgrades
  - Certain unusual repair expenses
  - Problems outside specified coverage limits

Some contracts may also impose maximum payout amounts or require the warranty company to select the service provider.

Therefore, the phrase "home warranty" should never be treated as equivalent to comprehensive home repair insurance.

## Do You Need Both?
For many homeowners, these products can serve complementary purposes.

Consider a homeowner with a 15-year-old house. Homeowners insurance could protect the property against covered events such as fire, certain storm damage, theft, and liability claims. A separate service contract might provide assistance with specified appliance or system breakdowns.

In that situation, the two products address different risks.

But buying both does not automatically make financial sense.

The FTC recommends comparing the cost of a service contract with the potential benefits and checking for hidden costs such as service fees, deductibles, coverage limits, and exclusions.

A homeowner with newer appliances, substantial emergency savings, and manufacturer warranties may decide that paying for a home warranty provides limited additional value.

Another homeowner with aging HVAC equipment, plumbing, and appliances may place greater value on having a predictable service process.

## What Should You Check Before Buying a Home Warranty?
Before signing a contract, review these items carefully:

### 1. Covered systems and appliances
Make a list of the systems and appliances you actually want protected. Confirm that each one is specifically included.

### 2. Service fees
Find out how much you pay when a technician visits. A low annual price can look different once multiple service calls are added.

### 3. Coverage limits
Check whether the company places dollar limits on repairs or replacements.

### 4. Exclusions
Read what isn't covered. Exclusions can be just as important as the list of covered items.

### 5. Maintenance requirements
Some contracts may impose requirements concerning installation, maintenance, or prior conditions.

### 6. Contractor selection
Determine who chooses the repair professional and what happens if you disagree with the company's assessment.

### 7. Replacement terms
If an appliance or system cannot be repaired, find out how replacement is handled and whether the company determines the replacement model or amount.

The FTC recommends obtaining the service contract's terms and understanding the coverage, costs, limitations, and claims process before purchasing.

## What Should You Check on Your Homeowners Insurance Policy?
Insurance deserves a separate review.

Check your:

  - Dwelling coverage limit
  - Personal property coverage
  - Personal liability limit
  - Deductible
  - Replacement-cost versus actual-cash-value provisions
  - Additional living expense coverage
  - Exclusions
  - Optional endorsements
  - Flood and earthquake coverage needs

Flood damage, for example, is generally not covered by a standard homeowners policy and may require separate flood insurance.

You should also periodically review your dwelling coverage because rebuilding costs can change over time.

## Which One Protects the Home's Value?
Neither product should be viewed simply as a guarantee of the home's market value.

Homeowners insurance is primarily designed to provide protection against covered property losses and liability—not to guarantee that the home's resale value will remain intact.

A home warranty is even narrower. It is primarily a service arrangement for specified systems and appliances.

The two products therefore solve different financial problems:

**Homeowners insurance:** "What happens if my home suffers a covered loss?"

**Home warranty:** "What happens if a covered home system or appliance breaks down?"

## Final Considerations
Homeowners insurance and home warranties are not substitutes.

**Homeowners insurance** protects against covered property losses, personal liability, and other risks defined by the policy. It is also commonly required by mortgage lenders.

**A home warranty** is generally a service contract that can help with specified repairs or replacements for covered appliances and home systems. It is optional and its value depends heavily on the contract's price, exclusions, service fees, limits, and claims process.

Before buying either type of protection, read the actual contract rather than relying solely on marketing language. The FTC emphasizes that service contracts can contain significant limitations, while the NAIC recommends understanding the specific coverage and exclusions within an insurance policy.

For homeowners, the practical approach is to think about the risks separately: **insurance for covered losses and liability, and a home warranty/service contract for specified mechanical breakdowns.** Whether the additional cost of a warranty makes sense depends on the age of the home and its systems, available savings, existing manufacturer coverage, and the specific contract being offered.
