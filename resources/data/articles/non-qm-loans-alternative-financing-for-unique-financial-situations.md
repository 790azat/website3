Not every borrower fits the standard mortgage underwriting model. A business owner may have substantial income but significant tax deductions. A real estate investor may have strong rental cash flow but an unusual income profile. Another borrower may have substantial assets but limited traditional employment income.

**Non-QM loans**, or non-qualified mortgages, are designed for situations where a mortgage does not meet the requirements for a Qualified Mortgage (QM) but the lender still determines that the borrower has the ability to repay.

These loans can provide alternative financing when conventional, FHA, VA, or other traditional mortgage options do not fit. However, the flexibility can come with higher interest rates, larger down payments, additional reserves, or other costs and restrictions. The CFPB notes that non-QM mortgages can be riskier or more expensive than qualified mortgages and are generally used when a loan has characteristics that make it ineligible to be a QM.

## What Is a Non-QM Mortgage?
A non-QM mortgage is a residential mortgage that does not satisfy the requirements for Qualified Mortgage status.

That does **not** mean the lender can ignore the borrower's ability to repay.

Under the federal Ability-to-Repay rule, most mortgage lenders must make a reasonable, good-faith determination that a borrower can repay the loan. Lenders generally consider income, assets, employment, credit history, and monthly expenses.

The difference is primarily in **how the borrower's financial situation is documented and how the mortgage is structured**.

A conventional mortgage may require extensive tax-return analysis for a self-employed borrower, for example. A non-QM lender may offer an alternative method of documenting income, subject to its own underwriting requirements.

## Why Would Someone Need a Non-QM Loan?
Traditional mortgage underwriting is designed to create consistent documentation and risk standards. That works well for many salaried employees with straightforward income.

Some borrowers, however, have finances that do not fit neatly into that model.

Potential non-QM borrowers can include:

  - Self-employed business owners
  - Independent contractors
  - Real estate investors
  - Borrowers with substantial assets
  - Foreign-national borrowers who meet applicable lender requirements
  - Borrowers with complex income structures
  - Retirees with significant assets but limited employment income
  - Borrowers with recent financial events that make conventional financing difficult

A borrower should not assume that having an unusual financial situation automatically requires a non-QM loan. Conventional and government-backed programs may accommodate more situations than borrowers realize.

For example, Fannie Mae's current guidelines provide specific methods for evaluating self-employed income, including analysis of business stability, cash flow, tax returns, and the borrower's ability to continue receiving income.

## Bank Statement Loans
One of the most recognizable types of non-QM financing is the **bank statement mortgage**.

Instead of relying primarily on taxable income shown on tax returns, the lender may analyze deposits appearing in personal or business bank accounts over a specified period.

This can be particularly relevant for business owners who generate substantial revenue but report lower taxable income after legitimate business expenses.

For example, suppose a business owner receives significant monthly deposits but reports substantially lower taxable income because of business deductions.

A conventional lender may calculate qualifying income using detailed tax-return and business cash-flow analysis. A bank statement lender may instead use an approved percentage of eligible deposits to estimate qualifying income.

The exact calculation varies by lender.

Not every deposit necessarily qualifies. Transfers between accounts, borrowed funds, refunds, and certain one-time deposits may be excluded.

## Asset-Based Mortgage Qualification
Some non-QM programs are designed around borrowers with substantial assets.

Instead of relying entirely on employment income, the lender may evaluate eligible liquid assets and calculate an income amount based on those assets under its underwriting methodology.

This can be relevant for:

  - Retirees
  - High-net-worth borrowers
  - Investors
  - Individuals living primarily from investments
  - Borrowers with significant savings but limited W-2 income

Asset-based qualification does not mean that every investment account automatically becomes mortgage income. The lender will establish which assets qualify and how they are treated.

Traditional mortgage programs also have certain methods for considering assets and non-employment income, so borrowers should compare those options before moving directly to non-QM financing.

## Debt-Service Coverage Ratio Loans
**DSCR loans** are another common non-QM structure, particularly for real estate investors.

Instead of qualifying primarily based on the borrower's personal employment income, the lender evaluates the cash flow generated by the investment property.

The debt-service coverage ratio generally compares the property's qualifying income with its debt obligations.

A simplified example:

**Property income:** $4,000 per month
**Qualifying property debt payment:** $3,500 per month

**DSCR = $4,000 ÷ $3,500 = 1.14**

A ratio above 1.0 indicates that the property's qualifying income exceeds the debt payment under this simplified calculation.

The lender's actual calculation can include different income, expense, vacancy, and debt assumptions.

DSCR loans can be particularly useful for investors whose personal tax returns do not show enough conventional income to support additional mortgages.

## Interest-Only Non-QM Loans
Some non-QM lenders offer mortgages with an initial **interest-only payment period**.

During that period, the borrower's scheduled payment generally covers interest rather than reducing principal through regular principal-and-interest amortization.

For example, a borrower might have an interest-only period followed by a period in which principal repayment begins.

The initial payment can therefore be lower than the payment on a fully amortizing mortgage, but the borrower needs to understand what happens when the interest-only period ends.

The later payment can increase substantially because the remaining principal must be repaid over the remaining loan term.

Borrowers should therefore compare the initial payment with the **fully amortizing future payment**, rather than evaluating affordability based only on the introductory payment.

## Non-QM Loans for Self-Employed Borrowers
Self-employed borrowers are one of the most common groups associated with alternative mortgage financing.

However, conventional financing should not automatically be ruled out.

Fannie Mae's current guidelines generally require lenders to analyze the stability of self-employment income, the nature and financial strength of the business, and the likelihood that the business can continue generating sufficient income.

Fannie Mae generally looks for a two-year history of prior earnings, although certain borrowers with less than two years of self-employment can qualify when specific requirements are met.

A self-employed borrower should therefore first determine how a conventional lender calculates their actual qualifying income.

If tax deductions significantly reduce qualifying income, a bank statement or another non-QM structure could be worth comparing.

## Non-QM Loans for Real Estate Investors
Real estate investors can have complicated financial profiles.

An investor may own several rental properties, receive substantial rental income, have multiple mortgages, and report depreciation and other expenses on tax returns.

Traditional underwriting can require detailed analysis of those income sources and liabilities.

A non-QM investment loan may instead focus more heavily on the property's cash flow, particularly when using a DSCR structure.

This can make the loan application less dependent on the investor's employment income.

However, investors should compare:

  - Interest rate
  - Down payment
  - Required reserves
  - Property type restrictions
  - Loan-to-value ratio
  - Prepayment penalties
  - Closing costs
  - Minimum DSCR
  - Personal guarantee requirements

A simpler qualification process does not necessarily mean a lower-cost mortgage.

## Credit Requirements
Non-QM does not mean "no credit requirements."

Lenders can establish their own credit standards, and borrowers with lower credit scores may face higher rates, larger down payments, or additional requirements.

Some non-QM lenders may accept credit profiles that fall outside the requirements of particular conventional programs, but eligibility varies considerably.

Before applying, check your credit reports for errors and understand how your score affects the pricing available from each lender.

The CFPB recommends comparing mortgage offers using the Loan Estimate and considering the overall cost rather than focusing on a single loan feature.

## Down Payments and Cash Reserves
Non-QM loans can require more equity than some conventional or government-backed mortgages.

The exact requirement depends on the lender, loan type, property, credit profile, occupancy, and other factors.

For example, an investor seeking a DSCR mortgage may encounter different loan-to-value requirements from a self-employed homeowner applying for a bank statement loan.

Lenders can also require cash reserves after closing.

Reserve requirements are particularly important for borrowers with multiple investment properties or variable income because the lender wants evidence that the borrower can continue making payments if income temporarily falls.

When comparing loans, calculate the **total cash required at closing**, not simply the minimum down payment.

## Interest Rates and Costs
One of the biggest tradeoffs of non-QM financing can be the cost.

Because these loans can involve borrowers or loan structures that fall outside standard Qualified Mortgage guidelines, lenders may price them differently.

Potential costs include:

  - Higher interest rates
  - Origination fees
  - Discount points
  - Larger down payments
  - Reserve requirements
  - Prepayment penalties
  - Appraisal expenses
  - Other lender-specific fees

The CFPB notes that non-QM loans can be more expensive or carry additional risk compared with qualified mortgages.

That does not make every non-QM loan unsuitable. It means the borrower should compare the additional cost against the financing flexibility being provided.

## Non-QM vs. Conventional Mortgage
The most important comparison is often between an alternative loan and a conventional mortgage.

| Feature | Conventional Mortgage | Non-QM Mortgage |
|---|---|---|
| Income documentation | Standardized underwriting methods | More flexible lender-specific methods |
| Self-employed borrowers | Detailed income and tax-return analysis | May offer bank statement or other alternatives |
| Investor financing | Conventional investment-property programs available | DSCR and other specialized options |
| Interest rates | Generally standardized by program and risk | Often priced differently |
| Down payment | Program-specific | Lender- and loan-specific |
| Underwriting | More standardized | More flexible |
| Availability | Broad | More specialized |
| Costs | Vary by borrower | Can be higher |

The table is a general comparison rather than a statement that one category will always have lower costs or easier qualification.

## Non-QM vs. Government-Backed Mortgages
FHA, VA, and USDA mortgages are Qualified Mortgages when they meet the applicable requirements, according to the CFPB.

These programs can be valuable alternatives for borrowers who qualify.

For example:

  - FHA loans can accommodate certain borrowers with lower down payments.
  - VA loans can provide financing for eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and certain surviving spouses.
  - USDA loans can provide financing for eligible properties and borrowers in qualifying rural areas.

A borrower should compare these programs before assuming that a non-QM mortgage is necessary.

## What Documents Will You Need?
The documentation depends heavily on the non-QM program.

A bank statement borrower might need:

  - Personal bank statements
  - Business bank statements
  - Business documentation
  - Identification
  - Credit authorization
  - Asset statements
  - Property information
  - CPA or accountant documentation in some cases

A DSCR borrower may need:

  - Property appraisal
  - Lease information
  - Rental income documentation
  - Property insurance
  - Property tax information
  - Entity documents when applicable
  - Personal identification
  - Asset and reserve documentation

An asset-based borrower may need detailed investment and financial account statements.

Ask the lender for a complete documentation checklist before submitting an application.

## How to Compare Non-QM Offers
When comparing non-QM lenders, look beyond the interest rate.

Review:

1.  **Interest rate**
2.  **APR**
3.  **Loan amount**
4.  **Down payment**
5.  **Monthly payment**
6.  **Origination fees**
7.  **Discount points**
8.  **Prepayment penalty**
9.  **Reserve requirements**
10. **Loan term**
11. **Interest-only period, if applicable**
12. **Rate-adjustment provisions**
13. **Property restrictions**
14. **Refinancing options**

Also ask whether the loan is fixed-rate or adjustable-rate and what payment could result if the rate changes.

The Ability-to-Repay framework requires lenders generally to consider whether the borrower can repay the mortgage rather than relying solely on an introductory payment.

## When a Non-QM Loan May Be Worth Considering
A non-QM mortgage may be worth exploring when a borrower has a legitimate ability to repay but does not fit conventional documentation standards.

Potential situations include:

  - Strong business cash flow but low taxable income
  - Significant investment-property income
  - Large assets with limited employment income
  - Complex income sources
  - Investment properties that qualify through rental cash flow
  - Other financial circumstances that conventional underwriting does not accommodate efficiently

The key is to compare the alternative loan with every conventional or government-backed option for which you qualify.

## Potential Risks to Understand
Non-QM financing can solve a documentation or qualification problem, but borrowers should understand the tradeoffs.

Potential risks include:

### Higher borrowing costs
A higher rate or larger upfront fee can substantially increase the total cost of the mortgage.

### Variable-rate exposure
Some non-QM mortgages may have adjustable rates. A future rate increase can raise the monthly payment.

### Prepayment penalties
Some alternative loans can contain prepayment penalties. Understand when the penalty applies and how it is calculated.

### Large future payments
Interest-only structures can create substantially higher payments once principal amortization begins.

### Less standardized terms
Non-QM products vary considerably between lenders, making careful comparison especially important.

## How to Apply for a Non-QM Mortgage
Start by identifying **why conventional financing does not fit**.

Then gather documentation that demonstrates your ability to repay.

Next, speak with multiple lenders or mortgage brokers that offer the specific type of non-QM financing you need.

Ask each lender:

  - How is qualifying income calculated?
  - What documentation is required?
  - What credit score is required?
  - What down payment is required?
  - How much cash reserve is needed?
  - Is the rate fixed or adjustable?
  - Is there a prepayment penalty?
  - What are the closing costs?
  - How is the loan reported and serviced?
  - What happens if the loan is refinanced or paid off early?

Once you receive offers, compare the total borrowing cost and future payment obligations.

## Final Considerations
Non-QM loans provide an alternative for borrowers whose financial situations do not fit conventional mortgage underwriting.

Bank statement loans can accommodate certain self-employed borrowers, DSCR loans can focus on investment-property cash flow, and asset-based programs can provide another approach for borrowers with substantial financial resources but unconventional income.

However, flexibility often comes with tradeoffs. Non-QM mortgages can have higher rates, additional fees, larger down payments, reserve requirements, or other conditions. The CFPB specifically notes that non-QM loans can be riskier or more expensive than Qualified Mortgages.

Before choosing an alternative mortgage, determine whether you actually need one. A conventional, FHA, VA, or USDA mortgage may still be available depending on your circumstances. If non-QM financing is appropriate, compare multiple lenders and evaluate the **APR, total closing costs, monthly payment, future payment changes, prepayment terms, and total interest expense**.

The goal is not simply to find a lender willing to approve the loan. It is to find financing that accurately reflects your financial situation while keeping the long-term cost and repayment risk manageable.
