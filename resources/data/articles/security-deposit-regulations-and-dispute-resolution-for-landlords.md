Security deposits are one of the most common sources of landlord-tenant disputes.

For landlords, the basic concept seems simple: collect money at the beginning of the tenancy, hold it during the lease, and return the appropriate amount after the tenant moves out. In practice, however, security deposits are governed by state and sometimes local rules covering the amount that can be collected, how funds must be held, permitted deductions, required notices, and deadlines for returning the balance.

Most states impose specific requirements, and the rules vary substantially. Current state comparisons show deposit-return deadlines ranging from roughly 14 to 60 days, with many states using a 30-day period.

For landlords, the safest approach is to treat security deposits as a **regulated liability**, maintain detailed records, and document every deduction.

## What Is a Security Deposit?
A security deposit is money collected from a tenant to protect the landlord against certain obligations under the lease.

Depending on applicable law, permitted uses can include:

  - Unpaid rent
  - Damage beyond ordinary wear and tear
  - Certain unpaid charges
  - Other amounts specifically allowed by the lease and applicable law

A security deposit is generally not the same thing as rental income.

The landlord should maintain records showing the amount received, where it is held when required, deductions made, and the amount ultimately returned.

For properties subject to specific federal housing programs, additional requirements can apply. For example, HUD regulations can impose particular rules concerning refundable deposits and itemized deductions.

## Security Deposit Limits Vary by State
One of the first questions landlords should answer is:

**How much can I legally collect?**

There is no single nationwide limit for ordinary private residential rentals.

Some states cap deposits at one month's rent, while others permit two months or another amount. Some jurisdictions have no general statutory cap.

For example, current state-law references show California generally limiting residential security deposits to one month's rent, while Alaska generally permits up to two months' rent in circumstances covered by its statute. Other states use different formulas or have no general statutory maximum.

Additional rules may apply based on:

  - Property type
  - Lease duration
  - Furnished versus unfurnished housing
  - Tenant age
  - Local ordinances
  - Subsidized housing
  - Whether the landlord owns a small or large number of properties

Landlords should therefore verify the applicable statute before setting a deposit amount.

## Where Should the Deposit Be Held?
Deposit-holding requirements also vary.

Depending on the jurisdiction, a landlord may be required to:

  - Place the deposit in a separate account
  - Hold it in a regulated financial institution
  - Keep it separate from operating funds
  - Pay interest
  - Provide specific disclosures
  - Maintain records of the deposit

The landlord should never assume that a security deposit can simply be treated as ordinary business cash.

For example, New Hampshire legal-aid guidance describes security deposits as money held in trust by the landlord and explains procedures concerning their return and permitted deductions.

Property managers handling deposits for multiple owners may face additional trust-accounting requirements.

## What Can a Landlord Deduct?
A landlord generally cannot use a security deposit as a general-purpose account for any expense associated with a tenant.

Permitted deductions commonly include items such as:

### Unpaid rent
If the tenant leaves owing rent, applicable law may permit the landlord to apply some or all of the deposit toward that balance.

### Damage beyond ordinary wear and tear
A landlord may generally distinguish between ordinary deterioration and tenant-caused damage where the law permits a deduction.

For example:

**Ordinary wear:** Minor carpet wear after several years.

**Potential chargeable damage:** Large burns, extensive stains, or damage caused by negligence.

### Certain unpaid charges
Depending on the lease and local law, deductions may sometimes include other amounts the tenant legally owes.

The important distinction is that deductions should be **legally permitted, reasonable, and supported by documentation**.

## Ordinary Wear and Tear vs. Damage
This distinction is central to many deposit disputes.

Ordinary wear and tear refers to deterioration that naturally occurs through normal use.

Examples might include:

  - Minor wall scuffs
  - Gradual carpet wear
  - Faded paint
  - Normal aging of fixtures

Damage can be substantially different.

Examples could include:

  - Broken doors
  - Large holes in walls
  - Missing fixtures
  - Extensive unauthorized alterations
  - Significant damage caused by negligence

Landlords should avoid charging tenants for routine maintenance or normal aging simply because an item is no longer in new condition.

## Document the Property Before Move-In
One of the strongest ways to reduce disputes is to document the property's condition **before the tenant takes possession**.

Create a move-in inspection record covering:

  - Walls
  - Floors
  - Ceilings
  - Doors
  - Windows
  - Appliances
  - Plumbing fixtures
  - Cabinets
  - Bathrooms
  - Outdoor areas
  - Existing damage

Photographs can provide particularly useful evidence.

For example, if a countertop already has a large scratch at move-in, a dated photograph can establish that the condition existed before the tenancy.

The same process should be repeated at move-out.

This creates a simple comparison:

**Move-in condition → Move-out condition → Documented difference → Repair cost**

## Keep Itemized Records of Deductions
If deductions are permitted, landlords should maintain documentation showing exactly what was charged.

Instead of:

Repairs: $850

use an itemized record such as:

| Deduction | Amount |
|---|---|
| Replace damaged interior door | $280 |
| Repair wall damage | $175 |
| Remove unauthorized fixture | $125 |
| Cleaning beyond ordinary condition | $150 |
| Unpaid rent | $120 |
| **Total deductions** | **$850** |

Keep supporting documentation such as invoices, receipts, photographs, inspection reports, and relevant communications.

Some jurisdictions specifically require an itemized statement when part of a deposit is withheld. State laws differ in both timing and documentation requirements.

## Know the Deposit-Return Deadline
A landlord should have a specific move-out workflow that automatically tracks the statutory deadline.

Current state references illustrate how different the deadlines can be:

  - California: generally 21 days
  - Arizona: generally 14 business days under specified conditions
  - Ohio: generally 30 days
  - Oregon: generally 31 days
  - Alabama: generally 60 days

These are examples, not a substitute for checking the applicable statute and circumstances. Some states calculate the deadline differently depending on whether deductions are being made, whether the tenant supplied a forwarding address, or other conditions.

Missing the deadline can have serious consequences.

Some states impose statutory damages or other penalties when a landlord wrongfully withholds a deposit or fails to follow the required procedure.

## Build a Move-Out Deposit Workflow
A property manager can reduce errors by standardizing the process.

### Step 1: Record the move-out date
Document when the tenancy ends and possession is returned.

### Step 2: Obtain the forwarding address
Some jurisdictions connect the return process to the tenant providing a forwarding address.

### Step 3: Inspect the property
Compare its condition with the move-in documentation.

### Step 4: Calculate permitted deductions
Separate unpaid rent, documented damage, and other legally permitted charges.

### Step 5: Collect supporting documents
Save invoices, photographs, receipts, and inspection reports.

### Step 6: Prepare the itemized statement
Clearly identify each deduction and amount.

### Step 7: Calculate the remaining balance
Return the unused portion according to the applicable legal procedure.

### Step 8: Document delivery
Retain evidence showing when and how the statement and refund were sent.

This workflow creates an audit trail if the tenant later disputes the deductions.

## How Should Landlords Handle a Deposit Dispute?
A dispute does not necessarily require immediate litigation.

Start by reviewing the documentation objectively.

Ask:

1.  What does the lease say?
2.  What does state or local law permit?
3.  Was the condition documented at move-in?
4.  Was the damage documented at move-out?
5.  Is the repair actually necessary?
6.  Is the amount reasonable?
7.  Was the statutory deadline followed?
8.  Was the required itemization provided?

If the landlord discovers an error, correcting it promptly may prevent the dispute from escalating.

If the tenant disputes a deduction, communicate in writing and provide the supporting documentation required by applicable law.

## Consider Mediation Before Court
If the parties cannot resolve the disagreement, mediation may provide an alternative to litigation.

A neutral mediator can help the landlord and tenant negotiate a settlement without requiring a court to decide the entire dispute.

The availability and usefulness of mediation varies by jurisdiction.

For smaller disputes, local housing agencies, courts, legal-aid organizations, or landlord-tenant programs may provide information about available dispute-resolution procedures.

If litigation appears likely, landlords should preserve the entire record rather than relying on memory.

## Property Management Software Can Reduce Errors
For landlords with multiple properties, security-deposit management can become difficult when handled entirely through spreadsheets.

Property-management software can help track:

  - Deposit amounts
  - Tenant balances
  - Move-in dates
  - Move-out dates
  - Inspection records
  - Deduction categories
  - Vendor invoices
  - Refund deadlines
  - Deposit-related communications

The system should not be assumed to automatically make the landlord legally compliant, however.

A software platform can calculate dates or generate forms, but the landlord still needs to configure the workflow according to the laws governing the property.

## A Security Deposit Compliance Checklist
Before collecting or returning a deposit, verify:

  - **Deposit limit:** Is the amount legally permitted?
  - **Holding method:** Does the jurisdiction require a particular account?
  - **Interest:** Is interest required?
  - **Permitted deductions:** Does each deduction have a legal basis?
  - **Move-in documentation:** Is the property's initial condition recorded?
  - **Move-out inspection:** Is the final condition documented?
  - **Itemization:** Does the law require an itemized statement?
  - **Deadline:** When exactly must the refund be sent?
  - **Forwarding address:** Is one required?
  - **Delivery method:** Does the law specify how notice or payment must be delivered?
  - **Records:** Are invoices, photographs, receipts, and communications retained?

## Final Considerations
Security deposits require more than simply collecting money and returning whatever remains after a tenant moves out.

Landlords need to understand the rules governing **deposit limits, holding requirements, permitted deductions, documentation, itemization, and return deadlines** in the jurisdiction where the property is located.

Current state-law comparisons show substantial variation. Some states require returns within roughly two weeks, while others allow considerably longer periods, and the consequences of wrongful withholding also vary.

The strongest defense against a deposit dispute is documentation.

A landlord who can show the property's move-in condition, move-out condition, lease provisions, invoices, photographs, permitted deductions, and timely refund or accounting is in a much stronger position to explain the final deposit calculation.

For larger portfolios, standardized inspections, accounting procedures, and property-management software can make compliance easier to administer. But because security-deposit laws are highly jurisdiction-specific, landlords should verify the current rules for **each property's state and local jurisdiction** and seek legal advice when a significant dispute arises.
