A thorough move-in and move-out inspection creates a documented record of a rental property's condition before and after a tenancy. For landlords and property managers, this documentation can help identify maintenance needs, distinguish ordinary wear from tenant-caused damage, prepare a unit for its next occupant, and support security-deposit accounting where permitted by law.

A good inspection should be more than a quick walk-through. It should consistently document the same areas of the property each time, preferably with written notes and dated photographs.

HUD's published move-in/move-out inspection materials use this same basic approach, documenting individual rooms and features such as floors, walls, ceilings, doors, windows, lighting, electrical outlets, plumbing fixtures, and safety equipment.

Because security-deposit and inspection requirements vary by state and locality, landlords should also verify the rules applicable to each property. More than half of U.S. states impose statutory limits on security deposits, and states can establish different deadlines and procedures for returning them.

## Before the Tenant Moves In
The inspection process should begin before handing over the keys.

Confirm that the property is clean, functional, safe, and ready for occupancy. Any outstanding repairs should be documented and assigned before the tenant takes possession.

Prepare:

  - Move-in inspection form
  - Lease and applicable addenda
  - Key and access-device inventory
  - Utility information
  - Appliance documentation
  - Emergency contact information
  - Dated photographs or video
  - Meter readings, where applicable

If the property is furnished, create a separate inventory of furniture, appliances, fixtures, and other included items.

## Entryway and Exterior
Start the inspection outside the property and work inward.

Check:

  - Exterior doors
  - Locks and deadbolts
  - Door frames
  - Doorbells
  - Exterior lighting
  - Steps and handrails
  - Porches and balconies
  - Walkways
  - Driveways
  - Garage doors
  - Fences and gates
  - Landscaping
  - Exterior walls
  - Roof-visible areas
  - Mailboxes

Photograph existing cracks, stains, dents, damaged landscaping, or other conditions that could later be disputed.

For multifamily properties, document the condition of common areas that are the landlord's responsibility.

## Living Room and Common Areas
Record the condition of:

  - Floors and carpeting
  - Walls
  - Ceilings
  - Doors
  - Trim and baseboards
  - Windows
  - Window coverings
  - Electrical outlets
  - Light fixtures
  - Switches
  - Heating and cooling equipment
  - Built-in shelving
  - Smoke and carbon-monoxide detectors

Look for existing nail holes, stains, scratches, dents, discoloration, or other visible conditions.

Do not simply write "good condition." Specific descriptions are more useful. For example, "two small nail holes above the living-room window" provides a much clearer baseline than "walls good."

## Kitchen Inspection
The kitchen should receive detailed attention because it contains numerous appliances, fixtures, cabinets, and plumbing connections.

Check:

  - Refrigerator
  - Freezer
  - Oven
  - Cooktop
  - Microwave
  - Dishwasher
  - Garbage disposal
  - Cabinets
  - Drawers
  - Countertops
  - Sink
  - Faucets
  - Drainage
  - Flooring
  - Walls
  - Lighting
  - Electrical outlets
  - Exhaust fan

Record the condition and, where appropriate, operation of each appliance.

Photograph existing scratches, dents, stains, chipped surfaces, damaged cabinet doors, or countertop defects.

If appliances are included in the lease, record model or serial information when useful for maintenance and replacement records.

## Bedrooms
Inspect each bedroom separately.

Check:

  - Flooring
  - Walls
  - Ceiling
  - Doors
  - Closets
  - Shelving
  - Windows
  - Window locks
  - Blinds or curtains
  - Electrical outlets
  - Light fixtures
  - Smoke detectors
  - Heating and cooling

Document any existing marks or damage rather than assuming that minor imperfections will be remembered later.

## Bathrooms
Bathrooms deserve particular attention because water-related problems can become expensive if they are not identified early.

Inspect:

  - Toilet
  - Sink
  - Faucets
  - Shower
  - Bathtub
  - Caulking
  - Grout
  - Mirrors
  - Cabinets
  - Exhaust fan
  - Flooring
  - Walls
  - Ceiling
  - Plumbing connections
  - Hot and cold water
  - Drainage

Look for leaks, water staining, mold-like growth, damaged caulking, loose fixtures, and slow drains.

A landlord should address significant water or safety issues before the tenant takes possession rather than treating the move-in inspection as a substitute for required repairs.

HUD's housing guidance identifies functioning plumbing, heating, locks, windows, fire protection, and other property conditions as important elements of housing quality.

## Heating, Cooling, and Major Systems
Test major systems before move-in.

Depending on the property, check:

  - HVAC operation
  - Thermostats
  - Furnace
  - Air conditioning
  - Water heater
  - Plumbing
  - Electrical panels
  - Garbage disposal
  - Laundry equipment
  - Sump pump
  - Garage systems

Record unusual noises, leaks, error codes, or other conditions requiring follow-up.

If a professional inspection or service was recently performed, retain the documentation with the property's maintenance records.

## Safety Equipment
Safety equipment should be checked separately from ordinary property-condition items.

Verify the presence and apparent operation of:

  - Smoke alarms
  - Carbon-monoxide detectors
  - Fire extinguishers where provided or required
  - Exterior lighting
  - Window locks
  - Door locks
  - Handrails
  - Emergency exits

Requirements vary according to state, local building codes, property type, and housing program.

For HUD-assisted properties, physical inspections can include plumbing, electrical systems, heating and cooling, smoke and carbon-monoxide detectors, windows, appliances, and other health and safety elements.

## Document the Move-In Condition
A written checklist is useful, but photographs provide another layer of evidence.

Take clear, dated photographs of:

  - Each room
  - Floors
  - Walls
  - Ceilings
  - Appliances
  - Bathrooms
  - Existing damage
  - Exterior areas
  - Fixtures
  - Furniture, if furnished

For significant defects, take both a wider photograph showing where the item is located and a close-up showing the condition.

Store the photographs with the tenant's property record rather than leaving them on an employee's personal phone.

HUD's inspection materials specifically contemplate recording the condition of individual areas and obtaining landlord and tenant acknowledgment of the inspection.

## Tenant Acknowledgment
Give the tenant an opportunity to review the inspection report.

If the tenant identifies an additional problem after moving in, provide a documented method for reporting it and add the issue to the property's record.

A tenant's acknowledgment should not be treated as eliminating statutory landlord obligations. It is primarily a record of the property's documented condition at the relevant time.

## Move-Out Preparation
The move-out process should begin when the tenant provides legally sufficient notice or otherwise confirms the intended departure.

Review:

  - Lease expiration date
  - Required notice
  - Move-out instructions
  - Cleaning requirements
  - Key-return procedure
  - Utility responsibilities
  - Forwarding address
  - Inspection procedure
  - Security-deposit requirements

The exact deadlines and procedures should follow the lease and applicable state and local law.

## Move-Out Inspection Checklist
Use the same inspection categories used at move-in.

Compare:

**Move-in condition → Move-out condition → Difference → Required action**

Inspect every room, fixture, appliance, and exterior area that was documented previously.

Pay particular attention to:

  - New holes
  - Broken fixtures
  - Damaged flooring
  - Stained carpeting
  - Broken doors
  - Damaged windows
  - Missing appliances or items
  - Excessive trash
  - Unauthorized alterations
  - Water damage
  - Damaged landscaping
  - Missing keys or access devices

HUD's inspection materials specifically recommend comparing conditions and provide a framework for documenting areas where repair costs may be necessary.

## Distinguish Wear and Tear From Damage
One of the most important parts of the move-out process is distinguishing ordinary deterioration from tenant-caused damage.

Examples of ordinary wear can include gradual carpet deterioration, minor paint fading, or normal aging of fixtures.

Damage can include circumstances such as large holes, broken doors, extensive stains, or physical destruction caused by misuse.

HUD's leasing guidance specifically distinguishes ordinary wear from damage and notes that inspection documentation can help establish the condition of the property.

The precise treatment of damage and security-deposit deductions depends on applicable law and the circumstances.

## Final Move-Out Steps
Before releasing the unit for turnover:

  - Collect all keys
  - Collect garage remotes
  - Collect access cards
  - Record final utility or meter information
  - Photograph the empty unit
  - Remove abandoned items according to applicable law
  - Complete the final inspection
  - Document repairs
  - Obtain contractor estimates or invoices when appropriate
  - Update the maintenance record
  - Prepare required security-deposit accounting
  - Schedule cleaning
  - Complete necessary repairs
  - Test major systems
  - Photograph the finished unit

Do not automatically charge the tenant for every repair identified during turnover. Determine whether the condition represents ordinary wear, tenant-caused damage, an existing defect, or routine property maintenance.

## Use a Digital Inspection Workflow
Property management software can make this process easier across a larger portfolio.

A digital inspection system can connect photographs, inspection forms, work orders, tenants, and property records.

A standardized workflow might be:

1.  Generate move-in inspection.
2.  Photograph the property.
3.  Obtain tenant acknowledgment.
4.  Record deficiencies.
5.  Create work orders.
6.  Complete repairs.
7.  Store the final inspection.
8.  Generate the move-out inspection.
9.  Compare the two records.
10. Create turnover work orders.
11. Document repair costs.
12. Prepare the next move-in.

HUD maintains sample move-in/move-out inspection forms and inspection resources that illustrate how these records can be structured.

## Final Considerations
A consistent move-in and move-out process protects both sides of a rental relationship by creating a documented record of the property's condition.

For landlords, detailed inspections can make turnover decisions, maintenance planning, and security-deposit accounting more organized. For tenants, documenting pre-existing conditions can help distinguish those conditions from damage that occurred during the tenancy.

The most effective system uses the **same inspection categories at move-in and move-out**, combines written descriptions with photographs, keeps records centrally, and applies the lease and applicable law consistently.

Before deducting anything from a security deposit, property managers should verify the rules for the property's state and locality. Requirements concerning deposit limits, permitted deductions, inspection procedures, itemization, and return deadlines can vary substantially.

A standardized checklist turns move-in and move-out from informal walk-throughs into a repeatable property-management process that can scale across an entire rental portfolio.
