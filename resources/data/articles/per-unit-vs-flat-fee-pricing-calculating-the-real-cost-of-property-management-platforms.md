Property management software is often marketed with a simple monthly price, but the number on the pricing page rarely tells the entire story.

Some platforms scale their pricing according to the number of units being managed. Others use monthly tiers, minimum portfolio requirements, or a base subscription combined with additional transaction and service fees. As a result, a platform advertised as "$X per unit" may not necessarily be cheaper than one with a higher-looking flat monthly fee.

The right comparison is the **effective cost of managing your portfolio**, including subscription fees, unit-based charges, transaction fees, onboarding, integrations, and paid add-ons.

## What Is Per-Unit Pricing?
With per-unit pricing, your software cost generally increases as the number of units in your portfolio increases.

A simplified calculation is:

**Monthly software cost = Number of units × Per-unit rate**

For example, if software costs $2 per unit per month:

  - 50 units = $100/month
  - 100 units = $200/month
  - 250 units = $500/month
  - 500 units = $1,000/month

This model can be attractive to smaller operators because the software expense can scale with the size of the portfolio.

However, actual property-management platforms frequently use more complicated structures. Some have minimum monthly charges, tiered unit pricing, or additional fees for specific services.

DoorLoop, for example, currently advertises plans beginning at $69 per month for up to 10 units when billed annually, while its pricing structure also includes additional charges for certain services.

## What Is Flat-Fee Pricing?
A flat-fee model charges a fixed subscription amount regardless of small changes in portfolio size, although there may still be limits or tiers.

For example, imagine a hypothetical platform charging:

**$300 per month for up to 200 units**

Whether you manage 75 units or 180 units, the base subscription would remain $300.

That creates a very different cost curve from a strict per-unit model.

At 75 units:

**$300 ÷ 75 = $4 per unit**

At 150 units:

**$300 ÷ 150 = $2 per unit**

At 200 units:

**$300 ÷ 200 = $1.50 per unit**

The same subscription therefore becomes cheaper on a per-unit basis as the portfolio grows.

But buyers should be careful with the term "flat fee." A platform may advertise a monthly subscription while still imposing minimums, unit limits, transaction charges, or paid add-ons.

## The Real Difference: Fixed Costs vs. Variable Costs
The simplest way to understand the two models is to separate **fixed** and **variable** expenses.

### Per-unit pricing
More units generally mean a higher recurring software bill.

**Advantages:**

  - Cost can remain relatively low for small portfolios
  - Expense increases as the business grows
  - Easy to estimate if the pricing is truly per unit

**Potential disadvantages:**

  - Costs can grow substantially with a large portfolio
  - Adding units can increase expenses even when software usage doesn't change
  - High-volume operators may pay significantly more for the same core platform

### Flat or tiered pricing
The subscription remains fixed within a defined range.

**Advantages:**

  - Predictable monthly expense
  - Additional units may have little incremental software cost
  - Can become more economical as the portfolio grows

**Potential disadvantages:**

  - Small landlords may pay for capacity they don't use
  - Plans can have unit ceilings
  - Moving to the next tier can produce a substantial price increase

## Don't Compare the Monthly Subscription Alone
This is where many software comparisons go wrong.

A platform might cost $200 per month while another costs $300, making the first option appear cheaper.

But suppose the $200 platform charges additional fees for:

  - Electronic signatures
  - ACH transactions
  - Bank accounts
  - Tenant screening
  - Inspections
  - Integrations
  - Premium support
  - Payment processing

The second platform might include several of those services in its subscription.

Buildium, for example, currently lists different charges for services such as tenant screening, eSignatures, inspections, and electronic funds transfers depending on the plan. Its advertised plans currently begin at $62 per month for Essential, $192 for Growth, and $400 for Premium.

The relevant number is therefore not simply:

**Monthly subscription**

It is:

**Total monthly software cost = Subscription + usage fees + add-ons + transaction costs + allocated setup costs**

## Calculate Your Effective Cost Per Unit
Even if a platform doesn't use explicit per-unit pricing, calculate your own effective cost per unit.

The formula is:

**Effective cost per unit = Total monthly software cost ÷ Number of units**

Suppose you manage 200 units.

### Platform A
Subscription: $250/month
Additional services: $50/month
Total: $300/month

**$300 ÷ 200 = $1.50 per unit**

### Platform B
Subscription: $400/month
Additional services: $20/month
Total: $420/month

**$420 ÷ 200 = $2.10 per unit**

Platform A is cheaper under this example.

But now imagine your portfolio grows to 500 units.

If Platform A charges $1.50 per unit while Platform B remains at $420 per month:

**Platform A:** $750/month
**Platform B:** $420/month

The cost ranking changes as the portfolio grows.

This is why property managers should calculate pricing at **multiple portfolio sizes**, not just today's unit count.

## Find the Break-Even Point
A break-even calculation can show when one pricing structure becomes cheaper.

Suppose:

**Platform A:** $2 per unit/month
**Platform B:** $400 flat/month

Set the costs equal:

**2 × units = $400**

Therefore:

**units = 200**

At 200 units, both cost $400 per month under this simplified example.

Below 200 units, the per-unit platform costs less.

Above 200 units, the flat-fee platform costs less.

Real-world pricing can be more complicated because platforms may use tiers and minimums, but the same principle applies.

## Watch for Minimum Portfolio Requirements
Some platforms are designed specifically for larger property managers and may impose minimum portfolio sizes.

AppFolio, for example, currently states that its Core residential plan has a **50-unit minimum** and a minimum spend requirement. Its pricing is quote-based rather than a simple publicly posted per-unit rate.

That means a 25-unit landlord should not compare AppFolio with a smaller-portfolio platform simply by looking at feature lists.

The minimum commitment itself is part of the cost.

## Transaction Fees Can Change the Calculation
Property-management platforms often connect software costs with financial transactions.

Consider electronic rent collection.

A platform might charge separately for certain ACH, card, or electronic-funds-transfer transactions. If you process hundreds or thousands of payments every month, even a small per-transaction fee can materially affect the annual software budget.

DoorLoop, for example, currently lists different ACH-related charges by plan, with some fees reduced or eliminated at higher tiers.

This creates another important formula:

**Annual transaction cost = Number of transactions × Fee per transaction × 12**

A property manager processing 500 transactions a month should pay close attention to transaction pricing even if the basic subscription appears inexpensive.

## Don't Forget Onboarding and Implementation Costs
Switching property-management platforms can involve more than the recurring subscription.

Potential costs include:

  - Data migration
  - Account setup
  - Training
  - Implementation
  - Bank-account setup
  - Custom integrations
  - Website setup
  - API access

Buildium, for example, lists a $99 bank-account setup fee on its Essential plan, while some higher plans include specified numbers of bank accounts.

These expenses may be one-time costs, but they should still be included when comparing first-year software expenses.

A useful calculation is:

**First-year cost = 12 months of recurring fees + one-time fees + estimated transaction/add-on costs**

## Compare Pricing at Your Expected Portfolio Size
Don't evaluate software solely on today's unit count.

Create at least three scenarios:

| Portfolio | Why Compare It |
|---|---|
| Current units | Measures today's cost |
| 2-year target | Tests scalability |
| Long-term target | Reveals potential pricing pressure |

For example, if you currently manage 80 units but expect to reach 300, a platform that is inexpensive at 80 units may become considerably more expensive at 300.

Conversely, a platform with a higher starting price may become more economical as the portfolio expands.

## Calculate the Cost Per Managed Unit, Not Just the Cost Per Software Unit
There is another distinction worth making.

Some property managers may have vacant units, owner-held units, or properties that require limited management.

If the platform charges based on every unit entered into the system, compare that with the number of units actually generating management revenue.

For example:

**Software cost:** $600/month
**Units in platform:** 300
**Actively managed/rent-producing units:** 250

Software cost per platform unit:

**$600 ÷ 300 = $2**

Cost per actively managed unit:

**$600 ÷ 250 = $2.40**

The second number may be more useful for evaluating the software against management revenue.

## Consider Labor Savings as Part of the Cost
The cheapest software subscription isn't necessarily the cheapest solution.

Suppose one platform costs $200 more per month but saves 10 hours of administrative work.

If that time would otherwise cost the business $30 per hour:

**10 × $30 = $300**

The additional $200 subscription cost could potentially be offset by $300 of labor value.

This is particularly relevant when comparing features such as:

  - Automated rent collection
  - Accounting
  - Maintenance workflows
  - Owner reporting
  - Tenant communication
  - Leasing automation
  - Inspections
  - Automated reminders

The correct comparison is therefore **total cost versus operational value**, not simply subscription price.

## A Property Management Software Cost Checklist
Before signing a contract, ask the provider:

1.  Is pricing based on units, properties, users, or a combination?
2.  Is there a minimum monthly charge?
3.  Is there a minimum number of units?
4.  What happens when additional units are added?
5.  Are vacant units billed?
6.  Are payment-processing fees separate?
7.  Are ACH transactions charged separately?
8.  What does tenant screening cost?
9.  Are eSignatures included?
10. Is API access included?
11. Are integrations included?
12. Is data migration included?
13. Are onboarding and training included?
14. Are there annual commitments?
15. What happens if the portfolio shrinks?

Getting these answers in writing can prevent a seemingly inexpensive platform from becoming significantly more expensive after implementation.

## Final Considerations
Per-unit and flat-fee pricing each have situations in which they can produce a lower total cost. The key is understanding how the pricing structure behaves as your portfolio changes.

A small landlord may benefit from a platform with a low starting subscription and limited minimums. A growing property manager may place greater value on predictable pricing and a lower marginal cost for additional units.

The most useful calculation is not the advertised monthly price. It is:

**Total annual cost ÷ Average number of actively managed units**

Include subscription charges, transaction fees, paid features, implementation expenses, and other recurring costs in that calculation.

Also model the cost at your **current portfolio size and expected future portfolio size**. A platform that looks inexpensive at 50 units can have a very different economics at 300 or 500 units.

Ultimately, property-management software should be evaluated as an operating expense. The best financial fit is the pricing structure that provides the required accounting, leasing, maintenance, payment, and reporting capabilities at a sustainable cost as the portfolio evolves.
