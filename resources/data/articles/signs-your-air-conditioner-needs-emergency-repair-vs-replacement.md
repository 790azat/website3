An air conditioner that stops cooling does not necessarily need to be replaced. Many failures are caused by relatively contained problems such as a failed capacitor, clogged filter, blocked condensate drain, electrical component, or damaged fan motor.

Other symptoms, however, require immediate attention because continuing to operate the equipment can create safety risks or cause additional property damage. And when an older system develops repeated failures, rising energy costs, and major component problems, replacement may make more financial sense than another repair.

ENERGY STAR recommends considering replacement when an air conditioner or heat pump is more than 10 years old, particularly when it requires frequent repairs or energy bills are increasing.

## When an AC Problem Requires Emergency Attention
Not every malfunction requires an emergency HVAC call. However, certain symptoms should not be ignored.

### Burning Smell or Smoke
A strong burning smell coming from an air conditioner can indicate an electrical or mechanical problem. Smoke is an immediate reason to shut the system off and address the situation rather than continuing to operate it.

If there is visible smoke, sparking, or an electrical burning odor, turn the equipment off if it is safe to do so and contact an appropriately qualified professional. If there is an active fire or immediate danger, use emergency services rather than attempting to diagnose the equipment yourself.

### Repeated Electrical Breaker Trips
A breaker that trips repeatedly should not simply be reset over and over.

The underlying issue could involve an electrical fault, motor problem, compressor issue, wiring, or another component drawing excessive current. Repeatedly resetting the breaker without identifying the cause can allow a dangerous condition to continue.

An HVAC technician or qualified electrician should determine why the circuit is tripping.

### Major Water Leakage
A small amount of condensation can be normal during cooling operation, but significant water around the indoor air handler, ceiling, walls, or electrical components requires prompt attention.

A clogged condensate drain, failed condensate pump, frozen evaporator coil, or drainage problem can allow water to accumulate.

For equipment installed above finished living areas, delaying the repair can turn an HVAC problem into a much larger drywall, flooring, insulation, or mold-remediation expense.

### Refrigerant Leak
Refrigerant problems should be handled by a qualified HVAC technician.

A system that repeatedly loses refrigerant may have a leak rather than simply being "low on refrigerant." EPA recommends locating and repairing leaks rather than repeatedly topping off a leaking residential air-conditioning system.

EPA Section 608 requirements also apply to technicians performing work that could release refrigerant from covered stationary air-conditioning equipment.

If a contractor recommends adding refrigerant without investigating why the charge is low, ask whether the system has been checked for leaks.

## Signs the Problem May Be Repairable
Several common symptoms do not automatically mean the entire air conditioner needs replacement.

### The System Runs but Does Not Cool Properly
Poor cooling can result from:

  - A dirty filter
  - Restricted airflow
  - Dirty coils
  - A malfunctioning blower
  - Thermostat problems
  - Duct leakage
  - Refrigerant problems
  - Outdoor-unit airflow restrictions
  - Equipment that is improperly sized

A technician should diagnose the underlying cause before recommending replacement.

ENERGY STAR notes that uneven temperatures can result from equipment operation, duct problems, or inadequate insulation, meaning that replacing the air conditioner is not necessarily the appropriate solution.

### The Outdoor Fan Is Not Running
If the indoor system operates but the outdoor unit is not functioning, possible causes include electrical components, the fan motor, controls, or the compressor.

Some of these repairs can be substantially less expensive than replacing the entire system.

Do not attempt to bypass electrical controls or operate the system repeatedly while waiting for service.

### The Air Conditioner Is Short-Cycling
Short-cycling occurs when the system repeatedly starts and stops without completing normal cooling cycles.

Potential causes include thermostat problems, restricted airflow, improper sizing, refrigerant issues, or mechanical problems.

Because continued short-cycling can place additional stress on the equipment, the underlying cause should be investigated rather than simply accepting the behavior as normal.

### Unusual Noises
Grinding, screeching, rattling, buzzing, or repeated clicking can indicate a developing mechanical or electrical problem.

The type and timing of the noise can provide useful information to a technician. A new noise is generally easier and less expensive to address when investigated before a component fails completely.

## When Replacement Starts Making More Sense
A repair becomes a replacement discussion when the problem is no longer an isolated failure.

### The System Is More Than 10 Years Old
ENERGY STAR identifies air conditioners and heat pumps more than 10 years old as equipment for which replacement may be worth considering. Age alone does not mean a functioning system must be replaced, but it becomes an important factor when combined with declining performance or repeated repairs.

For landlords, the age of the system should be recorded as part of the property's maintenance history.

### Repairs Are Becoming Frequent
One repair does not necessarily indicate that an air conditioner has reached the end of its useful life.

Several significant repairs within a short period are different.

If the system has required repeated service for motors, electrical components, refrigerant leaks, controls, or other major parts, compare the expected cost of another repair with the cost and expected service life of a replacement system.

ENERGY STAR specifically identifies frequent repairs combined with rising energy bills as a reason to consider replacement.

### Energy Costs Are Increasing Without a Clear Explanation
An increase in electricity costs does not automatically mean the air conditioner is failing. Utility rates, weather, thermostat settings, insulation, occupancy, and other factors can all affect consumption.

However, if comparable usage produces increasingly poor cooling performance while the equipment runs for longer periods, an HVAC evaluation is appropriate.

An older system that requires substantial repairs while also becoming less efficient may be a poor candidate for continued investment.

### Replacement Parts Are Becoming Difficult to Source
Older equipment can become increasingly difficult to maintain when particular components are obsolete, difficult to obtain, or unusually expensive.

The refrigerant used by the system can also affect repair decisions. EPA notes that homeowners should identify the refrigerant in their equipment and work with technicians qualified to handle the refrigerant involved.

A contractor should explain whether a proposed repair is a routine service or whether it involves an aging system for which future service could become increasingly difficult.

## Compare the Repair With the Whole-System Cost
When a major repair is recommended, ask the contractor for two separate numbers:

**Repair scenario:** What will it cost to restore the existing equipment, and what components are being replaced?

**Replacement scenario:** What would a properly sized new system cost, including installation and required associated work?

Then consider:

  - Age of the existing system
  - Previous repair history
  - Expected repair life
  - Energy consumption
  - Warranty coverage
  - Availability of replacement parts
  - Refrigerant type
  - Installation quality
  - Expected service life of the replacement
  - Financing and cash-flow considerations

Avoid making the decision solely from a repair-to-replacement percentage rule. A $2,000 repair on a relatively young, well-maintained system can represent a very different decision from a $2,000 repair on an aging system that has already required several major repairs.

## Do Not Replace the System Without Diagnosing the Building
A new air conditioner cannot correct every comfort problem.

If one room is consistently hot, the problem could involve ductwork, insulation, air leakage, windows, solar exposure, or equipment sizing.

Likewise, replacing an air conditioner without addressing a persistent condensate problem, poor airflow, or inadequate ductwork can leave the underlying problem unresolved.

ENERGY STAR specifically identifies duct problems and inadequate insulation among possible causes of rooms being too hot or too cold.

For replacement projects, ask the contractor to evaluate the complete system rather than simply swapping the outdoor unit.

## Questions to Ask an HVAC Contractor
Before approving an expensive repair or replacement, ask:

1.  What specifically failed?
2.  What evidence confirms the diagnosis?
3.  Is the problem safe to operate temporarily?
4.  Is there a refrigerant leak?
5.  If refrigerant is low, where is the leak?
6.  How old is the existing equipment?
7.  How much have I spent on repairs recently?
8.  Is the proposed repair expected to restore reliable operation?
9.  What would replacement cost?
10. Does the replacement require ductwork or electrical modifications?
11. What warranty applies to parts and labor?
12. What refrigerant does the existing and proposed system use?
13. Is the proposed system correctly sized for the property?

If refrigerant work is involved, EPA recommends using technicians certified under Section 608 for covered stationary air-conditioning service.

## A Practical Repair-or-Replace Decision Process
Landlords and homeowners can use a simple sequence.

**Step 1: Address immediate safety concerns.**
Shut down equipment exhibiting smoke, sparking, serious electrical problems, major water leakage, or other potentially hazardous behavior.

**Step 2: Obtain a diagnosis.**
Determine which component has failed and whether the underlying system remains in reasonable condition.

**Step 3: Review the system's history.**
Look at age, previous repairs, service records, and recurring failures.

**Step 4: Compare costs.**
Compare the proposed repair with a properly specified replacement rather than comparing the repair with the cheapest available new unit.

**Step 5: Consider future operating costs.**
Account for efficiency, maintenance, warranty coverage, and expected reliability.

**Step 6: Address related building problems.**
Check ductwork, insulation, airflow, drainage, and sizing before installing replacement equipment.

## Final Considerations
An air conditioner that suddenly stops working does not automatically need to be replaced. Many failures can be repaired, particularly when the equipment is relatively young and has been properly maintained.

Emergency attention is appropriate when there are electrical hazards, smoke, significant water leakage, or other conditions that could create immediate safety or property-damage concerns. Refrigerant problems also warrant qualified professional service rather than repeated informal recharging.

Replacement becomes more compelling when an air conditioner is older, requires frequent repairs, becomes increasingly inefficient, or develops major component failures. ENERGY STAR identifies equipment over 10 years old, frequent repairs, and rising energy costs among the circumstances in which homeowners should consider replacement.

For landlords, maintaining a detailed HVAC service history can make these decisions easier. Recording installation dates, repairs, parts, refrigerant information, energy performance, and contractor recommendations allows an owner to evaluate the system based on its long-term operating cost rather than making a rushed decision during a summer breakdown.
