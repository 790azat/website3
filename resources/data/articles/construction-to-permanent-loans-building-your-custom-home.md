Building a custom home can give you control over the property's layout, materials, finishes, and features, but financing new construction works differently from financing an existing house.

A traditional mortgage is generally designed for a completed property. A construction loan, by contrast, provides funds while the home is being built. A **construction-to-permanent loan** combines these two stages by financing construction first and then converting into a long-term mortgage once the home is completed.

Construction-to-permanent financing can be structured as either a **single-closing transaction** or a **two-closing transaction**. In a single-close structure, the construction financing and permanent mortgage are established at the same closing. The loan then converts to permanent financing after construction.

For someone planning a custom home, understanding how the financing works before signing a construction contract can help with budgeting, contractor selection, and the overall project timeline.

## What Is a Construction-to-Permanent Loan?
A construction-to-permanent loan is financing designed to cover the cost of building a new residence and then provide long-term mortgage financing for the completed home.

During construction, the lender makes funds available according to an agreed-upon **draw schedule**. The builder or contractor receives money as specific stages of construction are completed.

Once construction is finished and the lender's completion requirements are satisfied, the temporary construction financing converts into a permanent mortgage.

Fannie Mae currently supports both single-closing and two-closing construction-to-permanent transactions. Its guidelines describe the financing as a way to replace interim construction financing with a long-term mortgage.

The basic structure is:

**Land + construction financing → Home construction → Completion → Permanent mortgage**

This differs from taking out a standard mortgage on a home that has already been built.

## How a Construction-to-Permanent Loan Works
The process generally begins before construction starts.

You and the lender establish:

  - The construction budget
  - The loan amount
  - The expected value of the completed home
  - The construction timeline
  - The contractor and construction agreement
  - The draw schedule
  - The permanent mortgage terms
  - The required borrower contribution

The lender then closes the construction financing.

During construction, the lender releases money in stages rather than providing the entire construction budget to the borrower at once. Fannie Mae's single-closing process calls for construction funds to be disbursed according to the construction contract and draw schedule, with inspections used to verify progress before draws are released.

After the house is completed, the lender verifies that the property meets the conditions required for conversion to permanent financing.

## Single-Close Construction-to-Permanent Financing
A **single-close** construction-to-permanent loan combines the construction loan and permanent mortgage into one transaction.

You close before construction begins, and the loan documents establish the terms of the permanent financing.

Once the home is completed, the construction phase converts to the permanent mortgage.

One potential advantage is that you do not need to complete a completely separate mortgage closing at the end of construction.

Fannie Mae describes single-closing financing as providing one closing for both the construction and permanent financing. The lender also manages disbursement of construction funds to the builder, contractor, or other authorized suppliers.

For eligible Fannie Mae single-closing transactions, the construction period generally cannot have a single period longer than 12 months and cannot exceed 18 months in total.

However, individual lenders can establish their own products, requirements, and construction timelines.

## Two-Close Construction-to-Permanent Financing
A **two-close** structure separates the construction loan from the permanent mortgage.

The first closing establishes the construction financing.

After the house is completed, the borrower goes through a second closing for the permanent mortgage, which pays off the construction loan.

The two-close structure can provide additional flexibility because the construction financing and permanent mortgage are treated as separate transactions.

Fannie Mae specifically notes that two-closing transactions can be used when a borrower has an existing construction loan that needs to be converted into permanent mortgage financing.

The tradeoff is that you may have two sets of closing costs and potentially go through underwriting requirements again, depending on the lender and structure.

## What Can the Loan Finance?
Eligible costs depend on the lender and loan program, but construction financing can potentially cover many expenses associated with building the home.

These can include:

  - Land purchase
  - Site preparation
  - Foundation
  - Framing
  - Roofing
  - Plumbing
  - Electrical work
  - HVAC installation
  - Windows and doors
  - Interior finishes
  - Cabinets
  - Flooring
  - Landscaping
  - Garages
  - Certain additional structures
  - Contractor costs
  - Permits and other eligible construction expenses

The construction budget should be established before the loan closes.

A detailed construction contract and cost breakdown can make it easier for the lender to determine how much financing is appropriate.

## Buying the Lot and Building the Home
You do not necessarily need to purchase the land separately.

Depending on the loan structure, financing may cover both the lot and the construction.

For example, Fannie Mae's single-closing purchase structure allows the construction-to-permanent financing to be used to purchase the lot and finance construction, provided the borrower does not own the lot at the time of the first construction advance.

If you already own the lot, different rules can apply.

Fannie Mae allows certain single-closing limited cash-out refinance transactions when the borrower already holds legal title to the lot before the first construction advance.

The treatment of land that you already own should therefore be discussed with the lender before construction begins.

## How the Construction Draw Schedule Works
The lender generally does not release the entire loan at the beginning of the project.

Instead, money is released through **construction draws**.

A hypothetical schedule might look like:

1.  Land and site preparation
2.  Foundation
3.  Framing
4.  Roofing and exterior work
5.  Plumbing, electrical, and HVAC
6.  Interior construction
7.  Finishes
8.  Final completion

Before releasing a draw, the lender may require an inspection confirming that the corresponding work has been completed.

Fannie Mae's construction-to-permanent process specifically describes periodic inspections and draw disbursements during construction.

This system helps control the flow of construction funds and ties loan disbursements to actual project progress.

## The As-Completed Appraisal
One of the most important parts of construction financing is determining what the property is expected to be worth **after construction is complete**.

The lender may use an appraisal based on the proposed plans, specifications, lot, and expected finished property.

This is sometimes called an **as-completed appraisal**.

For example, suppose:

  - Land and construction costs: $650,000
  - Expected completed value: $750,000
  - Construction-to-permanent loan: $600,000

The lender evaluates the relationship between the loan amount and the property's expected completed value, along with other underwriting requirements.

Fannie Mae's current single-close guidelines calculate LTV differently depending on whether the transaction is a purchase or limited cash-out refinance. For a purchase, the relevant comparison generally uses the lesser of the total purchase/construction cost or the property's as-completed appraised value.

Because the appraisal affects financing, major design changes can potentially affect the loan structure.

## What Happens If Construction Costs Increase?
Construction projects do not always stay within their original budgets.

Material prices can change, labor costs can increase, and homeowners may decide to upgrade finishes during construction.

A lender may require documentation for cost changes and may have specific procedures for handling construction overruns.

This is why maintaining a contingency reserve can be important.

Before construction begins, ask the lender:

  - How are cost overruns handled?
  - Can the loan amount be increased?
  - What documentation is required?
  - Can unused contingency funds be redirected?
  - What happens if the final cost exceeds the approved budget?

Fannie Mae's guidelines include specific provisions for certain documented construction overruns in eligible two-closing transactions, illustrating why the treatment of additional costs should be established in advance.

## Interest During Construction
The construction phase does not necessarily work like a standard fully funded mortgage.

Because construction funds are generally advanced over time, the amount outstanding can increase as additional draws are made.

Depending on the loan structure, the borrower may make payments during construction based on the amount drawn or according to another arrangement established by the lender.

Ask the lender for a projected payment schedule that shows:

  - Initial construction payment
  - Expected payment as draws increase
  - Final construction-phase payment
  - Permanent mortgage payment

This can make the transition from construction financing to long-term home financing easier to budget for.

## Construction-to-Permanent Loan Requirements
Qualification requirements vary by lender and loan program, but the lender generally evaluates both **the borrower and the construction project**.

Borrower requirements may include:

  - Credit history
  - Income
  - Employment
  - Debt-to-income ratio
  - Assets
  - Down payment or borrower contribution
  - Cash reserves

Project requirements can include:

  - Construction plans
  - Building specifications
  - Detailed budget
  - Construction contract
  - Contractor information
  - Property appraisal
  - Construction timeline
  - Permits
  - Draw schedule

The lender needs enough information to evaluate both the borrower's ability to repay the mortgage and the financial feasibility of the construction project.

## Choosing a Builder or Contractor
The contractor can play a major role in whether the financing process stays on schedule.

The lender may review the contractor's:

  - Experience
  - Licensing
  - Insurance
  - Construction history
  - Financial information
  - Contract
  - Project budget
  - Timeline

Requirements differ by lender.

Before signing a construction contract, ask the lender which contractor qualifications are required.

It can also be useful to obtain detailed estimates rather than relying on a single overall project figure.

## Down Payment and Borrower Contribution
Construction-to-permanent loans generally require the borrower to contribute some amount toward the project, although the exact requirement depends on the loan program and lender.

The borrower may need funds for:

  - Down payment
  - Land purchase
  - Closing costs
  - Reserves
  - Construction costs not covered by financing
  - Unexpected expenses

Some eligible sources of funds can include gifts, grants, or employer assistance under specific program rules.

For example, Fannie Mae's current single-closing product matrix includes specific rules governing when gifts, grants, or employer funds can contribute toward a borrower's minimum contribution.

The applicable loan program should be reviewed rather than assuming that every type of assistance is permitted.

## What Happens When Construction Is Complete?
Completion is more than simply reaching the final day of construction.

The lender may need documentation confirming that the home is complete and suitable for occupancy.

Under Fannie Mae's single-closing process, key completion documentation can include:

  - An appraisal update or completion report
  - Lien waivers
  - Certificate of Occupancy

These documents help establish that the property has been completed according to the assumptions used for underwriting and that construction-related liens have been addressed.

Once the requirements are satisfied, the construction financing converts into its permanent mortgage structure.

## Advantages of Construction-to-Permanent Financing
For someone building a custom home, potential advantages include:

### One Financing Structure
A single-close loan can combine construction financing and permanent mortgage financing into one transaction.

### Reduced Closing Complexity
A single closing can eliminate the need for a completely separate permanent mortgage closing.

### Customized Home
The financing can be designed around a home built specifically for the borrower's needs rather than an existing property.

### Controlled Construction Funding
Draw schedules allow the lender to release funds as construction progresses.

### Permanent Mortgage Established in Advance
With eligible single-close products, the permanent mortgage terms are established as part of the original transaction. Fannie Mae notes that its single-close structure can provide rate certainty, with certain provisions allowing modifications at conversion.

## Potential Drawbacks
Construction-to-permanent financing also involves additional complexity compared with buying an existing home.

Potential challenges include:

  - Construction delays
  - Budget overruns
  - Contractor problems
  - Appraisal issues
  - More documentation
  - Construction inspections
  - Permit delays
  - Changing material costs
  - Difficulty modifying the project after approval
  - Temporary construction-phase payments

The biggest financial risk is often failing to account for unexpected costs.

A custom home should have a realistic construction budget rather than one based solely on the builder's initial estimate.

## Construction-to-Permanent Loan vs. Construction Loan
The two terms are related but not identical.

A **construction loan** generally provides temporary financing for building the property.

A **construction-to-permanent loan** combines that construction financing with the eventual long-term mortgage.

With a traditional construction loan, the borrower may need to arrange separate permanent financing after construction.

With a single-close construction-to-permanent loan, the permanent mortgage is established as part of the original transaction and the loan converts after completion.

## How to Prepare Before Applying
Before approaching lenders, prepare a complete project package.

Gather:

  - Land purchase information
  - Architectural plans
  - Building specifications
  - Contractor contract
  - Detailed construction budget
  - Construction timeline
  - Estimated property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance estimate
  - Income documentation
  - Asset statements
  - Existing debt information
  - Credit information

Then compare lenders based on more than the advertised interest rate.

Look at:

  - Construction interest rate
  - Permanent mortgage rate
  - Closing costs
  - Origination fees
  - Required reserves
  - Minimum down payment
  - Draw fees
  - Construction period
  - Extension fees
  - Rate-lock terms
  - Conversion requirements

Two loans with similar interest rates can have significantly different total costs.

## Questions to Ask Before Choosing a Loan
Before signing the financing documents, ask:

1.  Is this a single-close or two-close structure?
2.  How long can the construction period last?
3.  What happens if construction is delayed?
4.  How are construction draws released?
5.  How often are inspections required?
6.  What contractor qualifications are required?
7.  How is the completed home's value determined?
8.  How are cost overruns handled?
9.  What down payment is required?
10. What cash reserves must I maintain?
11. What happens to the interest rate during construction?
12. What are the permanent mortgage terms?
13. Will I need to requalify before conversion?
14. What fees apply if the project takes longer than expected?
15. What documentation is required for final conversion?

Getting these answers before construction begins can prevent financing surprises later.

## Is a Construction-to-Permanent Loan Right for a Custom Home?
Construction-to-permanent financing can provide a structured way to finance a custom home from the initial building phase through the long-term mortgage.

The key advantage of the single-close structure is that construction financing and permanent financing can be established together, while the lender manages construction draws until the home is completed. Fannie Mae's current guidelines support both single-closing and two-closing structures, with different requirements for each.

However, the financing should be planned alongside the construction project itself. The land, contractor, architectural plans, construction budget, appraisal, timeline, reserves, and permanent mortgage all need to fit together.

For a prospective custom-home builder, the most useful comparison is therefore not simply the lowest construction interest rate. Consider the **total financing cost, permanent mortgage terms, construction period, draw process, lender requirements, and treatment of cost overruns**.

A well-structured construction-to-permanent loan can turn the financing process into a continuous path from an empty lot to a completed home and, ultimately, a long-term mortgage.
