Homeowners with substantial equity have several ways to borrow against their property, including a **cash-out refinance** and a **home equity line of credit (HELOC)**. Both use the home as collateral, but they work differently and can produce very different interest costs, monthly payments, closing expenses, and tax consequences.

A cash-out refinance replaces the existing mortgage with a larger loan and gives the homeowner the difference in cash. A HELOC is a separate credit line secured by the home's equity, allowing the borrower to draw funds as needed while generally keeping the original mortgage in place.

The right comparison depends heavily on the homeowner's existing mortgage rate, how much money is needed, how long the funds will be outstanding, and how the borrowed money will be used.

## How a Cash-Out Refinance Works
With a cash-out refinance, the existing mortgage is paid off and replaced by a new, larger mortgage.

For example, suppose a homeowner has:

  - $220,000 remaining on the existing mortgage
  - A home worth $400,000
  - A new refinance loan of $280,000

The new mortgage would generally pay off the existing $220,000 balance, while the remaining amount—less applicable closing costs and adjustments—could be provided to the homeowner as cash.

The result is **one new primary mortgage payment** rather than keeping the existing mortgage and adding a second home-equity payment.

The major consideration is that the entire mortgage is repriced under the new loan terms. If the existing mortgage has a substantially lower interest rate than current refinance rates, replacing it could increase the cost of the debt.

The CFPB specifically notes that borrowers considering a cash-out refinance should compare the new rate with their existing mortgage rate because the refinance can be more or less expensive than a HELOC depending on the circumstances.

## How a HELOC Works
A HELOC works more like a revolving credit account secured by the home.

Instead of replacing the existing mortgage, the homeowner establishes a credit line based partly on available home equity. The borrower can then draw money up to the approved limit.

For example, a homeowner could have:

  - Existing mortgage: $220,000
  - HELOC limit: $80,000
  - Initial HELOC draw: $30,000

The original $220,000 mortgage remains in place, while the homeowner makes separate payments on the HELOC balance.

HELOCs generally have **variable interest rates**, meaning the rate and payment can change over time. Some lenders offer the ability to convert part or all of a HELOC balance to a fixed rate, although the resulting rate may be higher than the variable rate.

This structure can be useful when a homeowner does not need the entire available credit line immediately.

## Cash-Out Refinance vs. HELOC Interest Rates
Interest-rate structure is one of the biggest differences between the two options.

A cash-out refinance generally uses a new mortgage interest rate that can be fixed for the life of the loan. This provides predictable principal-and-interest payments.

A HELOC usually has an adjustable rate. If the underlying rate changes, the homeowner's HELOC payment can change as well.

The comparison becomes more complicated when the homeowner already has a low-rate first mortgage.

### Example
Imagine a homeowner has a $250,000 mortgage at 3.5%.

If they refinance the entire balance plus $50,000 of cash at a substantially higher current refinance rate, the new rate applies to the entire replacement mortgage.

With a HELOC, the homeowner could potentially leave the $250,000 mortgage at 3.5% and borrow only the additional $50,000 through the second lien.

That does not automatically make the HELOC less expensive. A HELOC may carry a higher rate than a first mortgage and can fluctuate over time. But preserving a low-rate existing mortgage can be an important consideration.

## Tax Treatment of a Cash-Out Refinance
The tax treatment of mortgage interest depends heavily on **how the borrowed money is used**.

Under current IRS guidance, interest on mortgage debt secured by a qualified home can potentially be deductible when the proceeds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve that home, subject to applicable requirements and limits. Interest associated with proceeds used for personal expenses generally does not qualify for the home mortgage interest deduction.

A cash-out refinance can therefore contain different categories of debt.

For example, suppose a homeowner refinances:

  - $250,000 of existing mortgage debt
  - $75,000 additional cash

If the original mortgage was used to acquire the home, the treatment of the refinanced acquisition debt can continue subject to the IRS rules. The additional $75,000 may have different treatment depending on how those funds are used.

If the cash is used for a qualifying substantial home improvement, the interest may potentially qualify for the home mortgage interest deduction, subject to the applicable limits.

If the cash is used to pay credit cards, purchase a vehicle, fund a vacation, or cover other personal expenses, the associated interest generally does not qualify for the home mortgage interest deduction under current rules.

## Tax Treatment of a HELOC
The same basic use-of-proceeds principle applies to HELOC interest.

The IRS states that interest on a home equity loan or HELOC secured by a main or second home may be deductible when the borrowed funds are used to **buy, build, or substantially improve the residence securing the loan**, subject to applicable dollar limitations and other requirements.

This distinction makes documentation particularly important.

Suppose a homeowner takes a $60,000 HELOC and uses it to renovate the kitchen, replace the roof, or complete another substantial improvement to the home securing the loan. The interest may potentially qualify under the applicable federal rules.

If the same $60,000 is used to pay off credit-card debt, the interest generally isn't deductible as home mortgage interest.

Homeowners should maintain records showing how borrowed funds were spent. Tax treatment can depend on the specific transaction, and taxpayers should consult a qualified tax professional for advice about their circumstances.

## Mortgage Interest Deduction Limits
Even when interest qualifies, there are limits on the amount of mortgage debt that can generate a federal home mortgage interest deduction.

For debt secured after December 15, 2017, IRS Publication 936 generally describes a **$750,000 acquisition-debt limit**, or $375,000 for married taxpayers filing separately, subject to the rules and exceptions described by the IRS. Higher limits can apply to certain older debt.

The calculation can become more complicated when a refinance combines existing acquisition debt with additional borrowing.

This is another reason that homeowners should not assume that every dollar of interest shown on a mortgage or HELOC statement is automatically deductible.

## Closing Costs: Cash-Out Refinance vs. HELOC
A cash-out refinance generally involves many of the same expenses associated with obtaining a mortgage, such as lender charges, appraisal costs, title services, and other closing expenses.

A HELOC can also involve fees, including application, origination, appraisal, title, annual, inactivity, cancellation, or conversion fees depending on the lender and product.

The difference can be significant for borrowers who only need a relatively small amount of money.

For example, refinancing an entire $300,000 mortgage to access $40,000 of equity may involve replacing a large existing loan and paying refinance-related costs on the transaction.

A HELOC could allow the homeowner to borrow only the amount needed while leaving the first mortgage untouched.

On the other hand, a refinance may make sense for a homeowner who wants to restructure the entire mortgage and can obtain terms that justify the closing costs.

## When a Cash-Out Refinance May Fit
A cash-out refinance may be considered when a homeowner:

  - Wants to replace the existing mortgage
  - Needs a relatively large amount of equity
  - Wants a single mortgage payment
  - Can obtain refinance terms that make sense compared with the existing loan
  - Wants a fixed-rate structure
  - Plans a substantial long-term home improvement project

Home improvement can be particularly relevant because qualifying improvements can have different federal tax treatment from personal spending.

However, the homeowner should compare the new mortgage rate with the existing rate before replacing a favorable first mortgage.

## When a HELOC May Fit
A HELOC may be considered when a homeowner:

  - Wants to preserve an existing first-mortgage rate
  - Needs access to funds over time rather than all at once
  - Wants to borrow only what is needed
  - Expects to repay the borrowed amount relatively quickly
  - Is comfortable with a variable interest rate
  - Is financing a project where expenses will occur in stages

A HELOC can be particularly useful for renovations where the final cost is uncertain or contractors are paid at different stages.

However, borrowers should understand that HELOC payments can change and that lenders may impose fees or restrictions on the line of credit.

## Cash-Out Refinance vs. HELOC: Key Differences
| Feature | Cash-Out Refinance | HELOC |
|---|---|---|
| Existing mortgage | Replaced | Usually remains |
| Cash access | Lump sum | Draw as needed |
| Interest rate | Typically fixed for fixed-rate mortgage | Usually variable |
| Number of mortgage payments | Usually one | Existing mortgage + HELOC |
| Closing costs | Can be substantial | Can also include fees |
| Existing low mortgage rate | Lost when replaced | Can generally be preserved |
| Tax treatment | Depends on use of proceeds | Depends on use of proceeds |
| Suitable for staged projects | Less flexible | More flexible |
| Rate/payment predictability | Higher with fixed rate | Lower with variable rate |

## Other Factors to Consider
Homeowners should also consider their future plans.

If you expect to sell the property soon, paying substantial refinance costs may make less sense because there may not be enough time to recover those expenses through lower financing costs or other benefits.

If you expect to remain in the property for many years, the long-term interest cost becomes more important.

Also consider the effect on home equity. Both products use the home as collateral. Failure to repay the debt can ultimately put the property at risk.

A HELOC can also affect a future refinance. The CFPB notes that a homeowner may need approval from the HELOC lender before refinancing the first mortgage, and in some circumstances the HELOC may need to be paid off.

## How to Compare the Two Options
Before choosing between a cash-out refinance and a HELOC, compare the complete cost rather than focusing only on the advertised interest rate.

Review:

1.  Current mortgage rate
2.  New refinance rate
3.  HELOC rate and adjustment terms
4.  Loan amount
5.  Closing costs and fees
6.  Monthly payments
7.  Expected repayment period
8.  Total interest
9.  Tax treatment of the intended use of funds
10. Potential effect on future refinancing or home sale

A lender may also provide different pricing depending on credit history, loan-to-value ratio, property type, occupancy, and other underwriting factors.

## Final Considerations
A cash-out refinance and a HELOC can both turn home equity into usable funds, but they accomplish that goal differently.

A cash-out refinance replaces the existing mortgage and can provide a fixed-rate structure and a single mortgage payment. A HELOC leaves the first mortgage in place and provides a revolving credit line, usually with a variable interest rate.

The tax implications are also closely tied to how the borrowed money is used. Under current IRS guidance, interest associated with qualifying funds used to buy, build, or substantially improve a qualified home may be deductible, while interest associated with personal uses generally is not deductible as home mortgage interest. Applicable debt limits and itemization requirements also matter.

For homeowners considering a renovation, major repair, debt restructuring, or another large expense, comparing the existing mortgage, new financing rate, fees, repayment structure, and potential tax treatment can provide a clearer picture of the actual cost of accessing home equity.
