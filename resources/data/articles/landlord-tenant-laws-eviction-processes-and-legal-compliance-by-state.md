Evicting a tenant is a legal process, not simply a matter of asking someone to leave. Landlords generally must establish a legally recognized reason, provide the required notice, follow applicable procedural rules, and obtain a court order before physically removing a tenant.

The details vary considerably across the United States. States establish different notice periods, termination requirements, court procedures, and tenant protections, while cities and counties may impose additional requirements. A landlord operating in multiple states therefore needs a compliance process that accounts for the location of each property rather than applying one nationwide eviction policy.

## Common Reasons for Eviction
The circumstances that can justify an eviction depend on state and local law and the terms of the rental agreement.

Common grounds include:

  - Nonpayment of rent
  - Material violation of the lease
  - Repeated lease violations
  - Property damage
  - Illegal activity
  - Unauthorized occupants or pets
  - Certain health or safety violations
  - Remaining after a lawful tenancy has ended

Some jurisdictions also restrict when a landlord can terminate a tenancy without alleging tenant misconduct. Additional protections may apply to particular tenants or properties.

Before taking action, landlords should identify the specific statutory basis for termination and determine whether the tenant has a right to cure the violation.

## The Notice Comes Before the Lawsuit
In many states, an eviction lawsuit cannot begin immediately.

The landlord first serves a written termination notice. Depending on the circumstances and jurisdiction, this might be a:

  - Pay-or-quit notice
  - Cure-or-quit notice
  - Unconditional quit notice
  - Notice terminating a periodic tenancy

The notice generally must contain specific information and must be delivered according to legally authorized methods.

The length of the notice can vary substantially. For example, current state-law summaries show different requirements for lease violations in Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, California, and Colorado.

That variation makes using a generic eviction notice particularly risky.

## Pay-or-Quit Notices
A pay-or-quit notice is commonly used when rent has not been paid.

The notice typically gives the tenant a specified period to pay the amount due or vacate, although the exact requirements depend on state and local law.

The amount of time is not uniform nationwide. Some jurisdictions provide only a few days, while others provide longer periods.

Landlords should verify:

  - The exact amount claimed
  - Whether late fees can be included
  - The applicable notice period
  - How the notice must be delivered
  - Whether partial payment changes the landlord's rights
  - Whether local protections apply

A payment ledger should be maintained so that the amount stated in the notice can be supported by the landlord's records.

## Cure-or-Quit Notices
Some lease violations give the tenant an opportunity to correct the problem.

For example, a tenant may have violated a no-pet provision, created a particular nuisance, or breached another lease requirement that state law treats as curable.

The landlord's notice may identify the violation and provide a legally specified period to correct it.

Again, the rules differ. Nolo's current state-law summaries show materially different cure periods and circumstances across states.

Landlords should not assume that a tenant always has the same number of days to cure a violation.

## When an Unconditional Notice May Apply
An unconditional termination notice can require a tenant to leave without providing an opportunity to cure, but these notices are governed by specific statutory circumstances.

Examples can include certain serious lease violations, illegal activity, substantial property damage, or repeated violations.

The rules differ considerably by state. For example, current 2026 state summaries identify specific circumstances and notice periods in jurisdictions including Alabama, Arizona, California, Michigan, Minnesota, and New York.

Because an improperly used unconditional notice can invalidate an eviction case, landlords should verify that the underlying circumstances actually qualify under the applicable statute.

## Filing the Eviction Lawsuit
If the tenant does not comply with a valid termination notice, the landlord may be able to file an eviction lawsuit.

The terminology varies by jurisdiction. The case may be called an eviction proceeding, unlawful detainer, forcible entry and detainer, or an action for possession.

The court process can involve:

1.  Filing the complaint or petition
2.  Serving the tenant
3.  Allowing the tenant to respond
4.  Scheduling a hearing
5.  Presenting evidence
6.  Receiving a judgment
7.  Obtaining an order for possession when applicable
8.  Turning enforcement over to the authorized officer

Nolo's current state guides emphasize that landlords must follow the applicable court procedure and obtain the necessary judicial authority before removing a tenant.

## Self-Help Evictions Are a Major Compliance Risk
A landlord generally cannot bypass the court process by changing the locks, shutting off utilities, removing the tenant's belongings, or physically forcing the tenant out.

These actions are commonly referred to as self-help evictions. Nolo notes that lockouts and utility shutoffs used to force a tenant out are illegal across the United States.

Even when the tenant is behind on rent or has clearly violated the lease, the landlord should follow the legally established procedure.

Improper removal can expose the landlord to additional legal claims, financial liability, or delays in recovering possession.

## Federal Protections Still Matter
State law is not the only source of landlord-tenant requirements.

Federal protections can affect rental operations, including fair-housing requirements and protections applicable to particular categories of tenants.

The Fair Housing Act, for example, prohibits discrimination in housing based on protected characteristics under federal law. Landlords should therefore make sure eviction decisions are based on legitimate, documented grounds and are applied consistently.

Certain federal protections can also apply in specific circumstances. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau notes, for example, that servicemembers can have protections under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act when facing eviction.

## Local Rules Can Add Another Layer
A landlord cannot always determine compliance simply by looking up the state statute.

Some municipalities impose additional tenant protections, notice requirements, rental-registration rules, or restrictions on certain termination practices.

Rent-control and rent-stabilization laws can also materially change the rules for covered properties. Current state guidance, for example, identifies additional considerations for rent-regulated properties in New York.

For a property management company operating across several cities, maintaining a property-specific compliance database can therefore be useful.

## Documentation Is Part of Compliance
Good records can be just as important as knowing the applicable rule.

Maintain documentation of:

  - The signed lease
  - Rent ledger
  - Payment records
  - Communications with the tenant
  - Maintenance requests
  - Inspection records
  - Photos and videos
  - Lease-violation notices
  - Proof of notice delivery
  - Copies of court filings
  - Court orders
  - Repair invoices
  - Relevant correspondence

Records should be factual and consistent. Avoid informal notes that speculate about a tenant's motives or personal circumstances.

## Build a State-Specific Eviction Workflow
Property managers with multiple properties should avoid relying on a single national checklist.

Instead, create a workflow that identifies the rules applicable to each property.

A compliance checklist can include:

**Property:** State, county, and municipality

**Tenancy:** Fixed-term or periodic

**Reason:** Nonpayment, lease violation, holdover, or another permitted ground

**Notice:** Required form, content, timing, and delivery method

**Cure:** Whether the tenant has an opportunity to correct the problem

**Court:** Appropriate filing location and procedure

**Documentation:** Evidence required to support the case

**Special protections:** Any applicable federal, state, or local protections

This approach is particularly important for portfolios spanning multiple jurisdictions.

## When to Involve an Attorney
Eviction law is sufficiently jurisdiction-specific that professional legal advice can be appropriate, particularly when circumstances are unusual.

Consider obtaining legal assistance when:

  - The tenant contests the eviction
  - The property is subject to rent regulation
  - A discrimination claim has been raised
  - The tenant has requested an accommodation
  - Domestic-violence protections may apply
  - A bankruptcy filing is involved
  - A government agency or housing program is involved
  - The landlord is uncertain about the required notice
  - The case involves substantial financial exposure

USAGov directs tenants toward state agencies, housing authorities, legal-aid organizations, and other resources for landlord-tenant disputes, reflecting the importance of jurisdiction-specific assistance.

## Final Considerations
Eviction compliance depends on following the correct process for the specific property, tenancy, and reason for termination.

The general sequence—identify a lawful basis, provide the required notice, allow the applicable cure or response period, file the appropriate court action, and obtain lawful possession—provides a useful framework. The actual requirements, however, can vary substantially from one state to another and may be affected by local regulations or special tenant protections.

For landlords and property managers, the safest operational approach is to maintain state- and property-specific procedures, document every material step, and obtain qualified legal advice when the circumstances are unclear.
