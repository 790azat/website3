Mold, lead-based paint, and asbestos create different risks for landlords, but they have one thing in common: ignoring a known or suspected hazard can turn a maintenance problem into a significant legal, financial, and tenant-safety issue.

The rules are not identical. Federal law establishes specific disclosure and renovation requirements for lead-based paint, while asbestos requirements can apply when materials are disturbed during renovation or demolition. Mold regulation is much more dependent on state and local law, building codes, leases, and the circumstances surrounding the moisture problem. EPA does not set federal standards for airborne mold concentrations in homes.

For landlords, the practical approach is to identify potential hazards, correct underlying building problems, use qualified professionals when necessary, document the work, and understand which requirements apply before leasing or renovating a property.

## Mold: Focus on Moisture Control
Mold is usually a symptom of a moisture problem rather than an isolated maintenance issue. Leaking roofs, plumbing failures, condensation, flooding, poor ventilation, and water intrusion can create conditions where mold develops.

EPA recommends addressing moisture problems promptly. When visible mold is present, routine air sampling is generally not necessary, because there are no federal mold standards against which an indoor air sample can establish compliance. Professional assessment may nevertheless be appropriate for complicated or extensive contamination.

For landlords, the first priority should be identifying and correcting the source of moisture.

That may involve:

  - Repairing roof or plumbing leaks
  - Correcting drainage problems
  - Improving bathroom ventilation
  - Addressing condensation
  - Replacing water-damaged materials
  - Drying affected areas promptly after a water event
  - Checking concealed areas where moisture may accumulate

Simply cleaning visible mold without repairing the underlying leak can allow the problem to return.

### Document Mold Complaints Carefully
When a tenant reports mold or persistent moisture, document the complaint and the response.

Record the date of the report, location of the suspected problem, photographs, inspections, contractors contacted, repairs performed, and follow-up inspections.

EPA specifically advises renters to report plumbing leaks and moisture problems to the property owner or manager promptly.

From the landlord's perspective, a documented maintenance process demonstrates that the complaint was investigated rather than ignored.

State and local housing laws may establish specific requirements concerning habitability, mold disclosure, remediation, or landlord response times. Because these requirements vary substantially, landlords should check the rules applicable to the property's jurisdiction.

## Lead-Based Paint: A Major Compliance Issue in Older Rentals
Lead-based paint deserves particular attention in older rental properties.

Federal lead disclosure requirements generally apply to most housing built before 1978. Before a qualifying rental agreement is signed, landlords and property managers must disclose known information about lead-based paint and lead hazards, provide available records and reports, provide the EPA-approved lead hazard information pamphlet, and include the required Lead Warning Statement.

EPA updated its sample lead disclosure materials in 2024 to make the information provided to prospective renters and buyers more specific.

Landlords should therefore determine the property's construction date and maintain a dedicated file containing previous lead inspections, risk assessments, disclosures, and records of lead-related work.

For multifamily properties, available records may extend beyond an individual apartment to common areas and other units when building-wide evaluations have been performed.

### Deteriorating Paint Requires Attention
Lead-based paint that is intact and properly maintained presents a different situation from deteriorating paint that is peeling, chipping, chalking, or otherwise damaged.

EPA identifies deteriorating lead-based paint as a potential hazard because paint and dust can expose occupants to lead.

Landlords should not simply scrape, sand, or repaint damaged surfaces without considering lead-safe work requirements.

## Lead-Safe Renovation Requirements
Renovation work in pre-1978 housing can create lead dust even when the original project seems minor.

Activities such as sanding, cutting, window replacement, and other work that disturbs painted surfaces can generate hazardous dust and paint chips.

Under EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) requirements, covered renovation work in pre-1978 housing generally must be performed by appropriately certified firms and trained personnel. This can apply even when a landlord performs renovation work on a rental property personally.

Before starting renovation, determine whether the project is covered by the RRP requirements and verify the contractor's certification where required.

Keep records of the contractor, certification, scope of work, tenant notifications, and completion documentation.

## Asbestos: Do Not Disturb Suspect Materials
Asbestos is different from lead paint because the principal concern is the release of airborne fibers when asbestos-containing materials are disturbed.

Older buildings may contain asbestos in materials such as certain floor tiles, pipe insulation, ceiling materials, or other building components. EPA emphasizes that asbestos cannot reliably be identified simply by looking at a material. If a suspect material is damaged or renovation will disturb it, EPA recommends assessment and sampling by an appropriately trained and accredited asbestos professional.

For landlords, this means renovation planning should include an assessment of potentially affected materials before demolition or intrusive work begins.

Do not instruct ordinary maintenance personnel to cut, sand, drill, remove, or otherwise disturb suspect asbestos-containing material simply because the material appears old or damaged.

### Asbestos Management Can Be Preferable to Immediate Removal
The presence of asbestos does not necessarily mean every material must immediately be removed.

EPA's operations-and-maintenance guidance describes approaches such as encapsulation, enclosure, repair, and removal depending on the material's condition and planned work. The agency recommends using trained and accredited asbestos professionals for appropriate abatement activities.

If asbestos-containing material remains in a building, landlords should maintain accurate records identifying its location and condition and establish procedures to prevent accidental disturbance.

EPA also recommends that building owners inform occupants about the location and condition of asbestos-containing materials and emphasize the importance of not disturbing them.

## Renovation Is Where Multiple Hazards Can Overlap
A major remodeling project can expose several environmental hazards simultaneously.

For example, replacing windows in a pre-1978 property could disturb lead-based paint, while demolition of older wall or flooring assemblies could encounter asbestos-containing materials. Water damage behind walls could also create mold conditions.

Before starting substantial work, landlords should consider:

1.  **Building age** — Determine when the property was constructed.
2.  **Existing records** — Locate previous inspections and environmental assessments.
3.  **Scope of work** — Identify which walls, floors, ceilings, windows, or mechanical systems will be disturbed.
4.  **Professional testing** — Use qualified inspectors when suspect materials require identification.
5.  **Contractor credentials** — Confirm required lead-safe or asbestos qualifications.
6.  **Occupant protection** — Determine whether tenants need advance notices, relocation, or restricted access.
7.  **Documentation** — Keep records of assessments, permits, work practices, waste handling, and completion.

EPA's asbestos guidance specifically recommends reviewing inspection and assessment records before renovation and evaluating whether suspect materials could be disturbed.

## Understand the Difference Between Hazard and Liability
The presence of mold, lead paint, or asbestos does not automatically establish landlord liability in every situation.

Liability can depend on factors such as the property's location, applicable housing and environmental regulations, the landlord's knowledge of the condition, the lease, the landlord's response, whether required disclosures were made, whether the condition caused legally recognized damages, and whether renovation requirements were followed.

That distinction is important. A landlord should not assume that every older property containing lead paint or asbestos must be completely remediated immediately, nor should an owner assume that an apparently minor maintenance issue carries no legal significance.

When a tenant reports a potentially hazardous condition, prompt professional evaluation and documented corrective action are generally more useful than making assumptions.

## Build a Hazard-Mitigation System
Landlords with older properties can reduce risk by creating a repeatable environmental-maintenance process.

Maintain a property file containing:

  - Construction date
  - Lead disclosures
  - Lead inspections or risk assessments
  - Asbestos surveys, where applicable
  - Mold and water-damage records
  - Contractor certifications
  - Repair invoices
  - Photographs
  - Inspection reports
  - Tenant notifications
  - Remediation documentation

During routine inspections, look for water intrusion, deteriorating paint, damaged building materials, plumbing leaks, roof problems, and ventilation issues.

The objective is not simply to respond after a tenant reports a problem. Preventive maintenance can identify conditions before they become larger repairs or occupant-safety concerns.

## Final Considerations
Mold, lead-based paint, and asbestos require different mitigation strategies, and landlords should avoid treating them as interchangeable hazards.

For mold, controlling moisture is fundamental. For lead, landlords of qualifying pre-1978 housing need to understand federal disclosure requirements and lead-safe renovation rules. For asbestos, suspect materials should be evaluated before renovation or disturbance, with qualified professionals handling appropriate assessment and abatement.

Most importantly, landlords should document what they know, what tenants report, what professionals find, and what corrective actions are taken.

Because environmental, housing, landlord-tenant, and building requirements can vary by jurisdiction, owners should verify state and local requirements before undertaking remediation or renovation. For significant contamination, disputed liability, or major renovation work, consultation with an appropriately qualified environmental professional and attorney can help establish the correct compliance process.
