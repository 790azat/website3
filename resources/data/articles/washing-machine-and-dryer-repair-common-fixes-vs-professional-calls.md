A washing machine or dryer that suddenly stops working can turn a normal laundry routine into an expensive service call. But not every appliance problem requires a technician.

Some issues—such as an unbalanced washer, clogged dryer vent, tripped breaker, or improperly connected drain hose—can be identified and sometimes corrected with basic troubleshooting. Other problems involve electrical components, gas connections, motors, sealed systems, or internal mechanical failures that are better handled by a qualified appliance repair professional.

The key is knowing **where DIY troubleshooting ends and professional repair begins**.

## Start With the Basic Checks
Before taking apart an appliance, check the simplest possibilities.

Look at:

  - Power supply
  - Circuit breaker
  - Water supply
  - Drain hose
  - Door or lid
  - Control settings
  - Error codes
  - Vent connection
  - Appliance positioning

Modern washers and dryers often display diagnostic codes that can significantly narrow down the problem. Record the exact code before resetting the appliance.

Also locate the model and serial number. A technician will often need this information to identify the correct replacement parts.

## Washing Machine: Not Starting
If the washer does nothing when you press Start, begin with the power supply.

### Check the circuit breaker
Verify that the appliance's circuit breaker has not tripped.

If the breaker trips again immediately after being reset, do not repeatedly reset it. Repeated tripping can indicate an electrical problem requiring professional diagnosis.

### Check the door or lid
Many washing machines will not begin a cycle unless the door or lid is properly closed.

Inspect the latch for:

  - Obstructions
  - Visible damage
  - Misalignment
  - Broken plastic components

Do not bypass a door or lid safety switch.

### Check the controls
Some machines will not start if a cycle has not been selected correctly or if a control lock is active.

Consult the manufacturer's manual for the model-specific procedure.

## Washer Fills but Does Not Drain
A washer that finishes washing but leaves water inside may have a drainage problem.

First check the drain hose.

Make sure it is:

  - Properly connected
  - Not kinked
  - Not crushed
  - Positioned according to the manufacturer's specifications

Some washers also have an accessible drain filter or pump filter.

If the manufacturer's instructions allow homeowner cleaning, inspect the filter for coins, lint, hair, or other debris.

Place towels or a container underneath before opening anything that may contain residual water.

If the pump makes unusual noises, the machine repeatedly fails to drain, or the blockage cannot be safely accessed, a professional may be needed.

## Washer Is Shaking or Walking
A washing machine that moves across the floor during the spin cycle does not necessarily have a mechanical failure.

First check whether the load is balanced.

Large items such as:

  - Blankets
  - Towels
  - Bedding
  - Heavy clothing

can collect on one side of the drum.

Redistribute the load and run the appropriate cycle again.

If the washer continues to shake when properly loaded, check whether it is level.

The appliance's adjustable feet should sit firmly on the floor.

Persistent severe vibration may indicate a problem with suspension components, bearings, dampers, or installation.

A technician should evaluate the machine if the vibration is accompanied by loud banging, grinding, or movement that continues after leveling.

## Washer Is Leaking
A leak should be investigated promptly because water can damage flooring and nearby cabinetry.

Identify where the water is coming from.

Possible locations include:

  - Supply hoses
  - Drain hose
  - Door seal
  - Detergent dispenser
  - Pump area
  - Tub
  - Internal connections

If a supply hose is visibly leaking, turn off the water supply to the washer.

If water is leaking from inside the machine, disconnect power only if it can be done safely and keep the area around electrical components dry.

Do not continue operating a washer that is producing a significant uncontrolled leak.

## Washer Has a Bad Odor
A musty smell does not necessarily indicate a mechanical problem.

Front-loading washers can develop odors when moisture and detergent residue remain inside the door seal, dispenser, or drum.

Depending on the manufacturer's instructions:

  - Leave the door open between cycles
  - Clean the door gasket
  - Clean the detergent dispenser
  - Run the recommended cleaning cycle
  - Use the manufacturer's recommended cleaning product or procedure
  - Avoid excessive detergent

If an odor resembles burning plastic, electrical insulation, or another abnormal smell, stop using the appliance and arrange professional inspection.

## Dryer Is Not Heating
A dryer that tumbles but produces no heat is a common service problem.

Start with the easiest checks.

### Check the circuit breaker
Many electric dryers use a higher-voltage circuit. One side of the circuit can potentially trip while the dryer still appears to have some electrical functionality.

If the breaker repeatedly trips, stop troubleshooting and have the electrical system and appliance evaluated.

### Check the lint filter
A heavily blocked lint filter can restrict airflow.

Clean the filter according to the manufacturer's instructions.

### Check the vent
A restricted dryer vent can significantly reduce airflow and drying performance.

Inspect the accessible portion of the vent connection for:

  - Crushing
  - Kinks
  - Heavy lint accumulation
  - Disconnection

Dryer exhaust systems should be maintained according to the appliance and building requirements.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission advises consumers to clean dryer lint filters before or after each load and keep the dryer exhaust duct and vent free of lint and other debris. ([cpsc.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5098.pdf))

A dryer that takes increasingly long to dry clothes should not simply be operated for longer periods. Reduced airflow can indicate a maintenance or venting problem.

## Dryer Is Taking Too Long to Dry
Long drying times are often related to airflow rather than a failed heating element.

Check:

1.  Lint filter
2.  Exhaust hose
3.  Exterior vent opening
4.  Vent restrictions
5.  Load size
6.  Appropriate cycle selection

If the outside exhaust flap does not open properly when the dryer operates, airflow may be restricted.

For a long or complicated exhaust system, professional vent cleaning may be appropriate.

## Dryer Is Making Strange Noises
Different sounds can provide clues.

### Squealing or screeching
May indicate worn rollers, idler components, bearings, or other moving parts.

### Thumping
Could be caused by an uneven load or an object inside the drum, but persistent thumping may indicate worn drum-support components.

### Scraping or grinding
Can indicate a mechanical component rubbing against another part.

### Buzzing
May involve a motor, electrical component, or another internal part.

If a new mechanical noise appears suddenly and becomes louder, stop using the appliance until the source is identified.

## Dryer Won't Tumble
If the dryer turns on but the drum does not rotate, the problem may involve:

  - Drive belt
  - Idler pulley
  - Motor
  - Drum rollers
  - Door switch
  - Other drive components

Some homeowners can replace a dryer belt on certain models, but access procedures vary significantly.

If disassembly is required, disconnect the appliance from its power source and follow the manufacturer's service instructions. If you are not comfortable working around electrical components or internal moving parts, use a qualified technician.

## Gas Dryer Problems Require Extra Caution
Gas dryers introduce additional safety considerations.

Do not attempt to repair gas lines, gas valves, or combustion components unless you are properly qualified.

If you smell gas:

  - Stop using the appliance.
  - Avoid flames and sparks.
  - Leave the area if necessary.
  - Contact the gas utility or appropriate emergency service from a safe location.
  - Follow professional instructions.

A suspected gas leak is not a normal DIY appliance repair.

## Check Whether the Appliance Is Still Under Warranty
Before paying for a repair, find the appliance's warranty information.

Check:

  - Manufacturer warranty
  - Extended warranty
  - Retailer protection plan
  - Home warranty coverage
  - Appliance service agreement

However, understand what the warranty actually covers.

A warranty may cover a defective component but not damage caused by improper installation, misuse, or lack of maintenance.

Unauthorized repairs can also affect warranty coverage in some circumstances.

## When a Professional Repair Makes Sense
A professional appliance technician is generally appropriate when troubleshooting points toward:

  - Motor failure
  - Control-board problems
  - Internal electrical faults
  - Compressor or sealed-system issues where applicable
  - Gas components
  - Major bearing failures
  - Internal wiring
  - Repeated circuit-breaker trips
  - Serious water leaks
  - Structural drum or tub problems

The same applies when a repair requires substantial disassembly and you do not have the appropriate tools or technical knowledge.

The goal of DIY troubleshooting is to diagnose simple problems—not to turn a minor appliance failure into an electrical, water, or fire hazard.

## Compare Repair Cost With Replacement Cost
Older appliances can eventually reach the point where repeated repairs make less financial sense.

Before authorizing a major repair, consider:

**Repair cost + expected future repairs + operating cost**

versus

**Replacement cost + installation + expected operating cost.**

A relatively inexpensive repair on a newer machine may make perfect sense.

A major motor, control-board, tub, or sealed-system repair on an older appliance may justify comparing replacement options.

For landlords, this calculation should also include tenant disruption, service-call costs, warranty coverage, and the availability of replacement parts.

## Keep a Maintenance Record
For rental properties, appliance records can reveal recurring problems.

Track:

  - Appliance model
  - Installation date
  - Service dates
  - Repair descriptions
  - Parts replaced
  - Repair costs
  - Warranty information
  - Technician
  - Recurring symptoms

If the same dryer requires three service calls in a year, replacing a component—or the entire appliance—may be more economical than continuing to treat each failure separately.

## Preventive Maintenance Can Extend Appliance Life
Simple maintenance can prevent avoidable problems.

For washers:

  - Avoid overloading
  - Use the recommended detergent
  - Clean dispensers
  - Inspect hoses
  - Keep the door gasket clean
  - Follow the manufacturer's cleaning schedule

For dryers:

  - Clean the lint filter regularly
  - Keep the exhaust system clear
  - Inspect the vent connection
  - Avoid overloading
  - Follow manufacturer maintenance instructions

The CPSC specifically recommends regular lint-filter cleaning and keeping dryer exhaust systems clear of lint and debris. ([cpsc.gov](https://www.cpsc.gov/s3fs-public/5098.pdf))

## A Simple Troubleshooting Decision Tree
A useful approach is:

**Appliance won't start → Check power → Check controls/door → Check error code → Consult manual → Professional if unresolved**

**Washer won't drain → Check hose → Check permitted filter access → Stop if water remains or pump fails → Professional**

**Washer shakes → Check load → Level appliance → Professional if severe vibration continues**

**Washer leaks → Identify source → Shut off water if necessary → Stop using if leak is significant → Professional**

**Dryer won't heat → Check power → Clean lint filter → Check accessible vent → Professional if unresolved**

**Dryer takes too long → Check airflow → Inspect vent → Professional cleaning/repair if restriction remains**

**Dryer makes unusual noise → Stop if severe → Identify whether noise is mechanical → Professional if persistent**

This approach keeps simple maintenance separate from repairs that require technical expertise.

## Final Considerations
Washing machines and dryers often give homeowners useful clues before a major failure occurs.

A washer that drains slowly, a dryer that takes twice as long to dry clothes, or an appliance that repeatedly trips a breaker should not simply be ignored.

Start with safe, manufacturer-approved checks: power, water supply, hoses, filters, vents, controls, error codes, and basic installation conditions.

If the problem involves internal electrical components, gas, significant water leakage, motors, bearings, or repeated failures, bringing in a qualified professional is usually the safer choice.

For landlords, keeping detailed appliance-maintenance records adds another layer of protection. It helps identify recurring failures, plan replacements, control maintenance costs, and reduce unexpected appliance emergencies between tenants.

The best repair strategy is not to avoid professional service at all costs. It is to **diagnose simple problems efficiently while recognizing when professional expertise is the more economical and safer option**.
