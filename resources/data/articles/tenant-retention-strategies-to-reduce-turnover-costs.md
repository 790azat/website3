Tenant turnover creates expenses that extend well beyond an empty apartment. When a resident leaves, a property manager may face lost rent, cleaning, repairs, advertising, staff time, inspections, showings, screening, and lease preparation before a new tenant moves in.

Retention therefore has a direct operational and financial purpose: keeping a suitable tenant in place can eliminate many of the costs associated with preparing and re-leasing a unit.

In 2026, renter expectations around maintenance, communication, convenience, and value are making retention an increasingly important part of property management. Buildium's 2026 Renters' Survey found that 28% of surveyed renters planned to move between mid-2026 and mid-2027, while 32% of those planning to move cited lower rent or better value as a reason.

A practical retention strategy focuses on the parts of the resident experience that property managers can actually control.

## Calculate the Real Cost of Turnover
Before investing in retention programs, determine what a typical turnover costs your portfolio.

Track expenses such as:

  - Lost rent during vacancy
  - Cleaning
  - Painting
  - Repairs
  - Landscaping
  - Advertising
  - Leasing commissions
  - Screening
  - Staff labor
  - Utility costs during vacancy
  - Administrative expenses

Also measure the number of days between one tenant leaving and another taking possession.

The National Multifamily Housing Council has cited research indicating that turnover can cost thousands of dollars per resident, demonstrating why even modest improvements in retention can have meaningful financial implications.

Tracking actual costs for your properties is more useful than relying on a generic industry estimate because turnover expenses vary significantly by property type, market, condition, and tenant profile.

## Start Renewal Conversations Early
Waiting until a lease is about to expire can leave little time to address a resident's concerns.

A structured renewal process can begin approximately 90 to 120 days before expiration. This gives the property manager time to identify problems, discuss renewal terms, and address issues before the tenant commits to moving.

A practical timeline might include:

**120 days:** Review tenant history and property condition.

**90 days:** Begin renewal communication.

**60 days:** Present renewal terms and address concerns.

**30 days:** Confirm the tenant's decision and prepare the next steps.

The exact timing should reflect the lease and applicable local requirements.

## Make Maintenance a Retention Priority
Maintenance is one of the strongest controllable factors in the resident experience.

Buildium's 2026 renter survey found that 24% of respondents identified maintenance as their most stressful part of renting, while 39% said better maintenance follow-through would improve their experience. Renters satisfied with maintenance were also substantially more likely to say they planned to renew.

The practical lesson is straightforward: fixing problems matters, but communicating throughout the process matters too.

A maintenance workflow should acknowledge requests, establish priority, provide updates, document the repair, and confirm completion.

## Use Preventive Maintenance
Reactive maintenance can create frustration for both tenants and property owners.

Preventive maintenance involves addressing predictable maintenance requirements before equipment fails. Examples include HVAC servicing, filter replacement, plumbing inspections, roof inspections, appliance servicing, and seasonal property checks.

Buildium's 2026 industry research found that proactive maintenance can contribute to both lower operating costs and resident retention.

A preventive-maintenance schedule can therefore serve two purposes: protecting the physical asset and improving the tenant's experience.

## Respond Quickly and Consistently
Communication is another major retention factor.

Buildium's 2026 renter survey found that 84% of surveyed renters expected a response from their property manager within 24 hours, while 61% expected a response the same day or faster. The survey also found that renters satisfied with communication were more likely to plan to renew.

Property managers do not necessarily need to resolve every issue immediately. They should, however, acknowledge requests and provide realistic expectations.

A simple message explaining that a technician has been contacted can be more useful than leaving the tenant uncertain for several days.

## Make Rent and Account Management Convenient
Administrative friction can affect the resident experience.

Online rent payments, digital lease documents, maintenance portals, automated reminders, and accessible account information can make routine interactions easier.

Digital self-service also reduces the number of simple questions employees need to answer manually.

The National Multifamily Housing Council has reported growing adoption of self-service renewal processes, with its customer-experience technology research identifying property-management systems as a common channel for these activities.

Convenience should not replace human support. Residents should still have a straightforward way to reach a person when a problem is complicated or sensitive.

## Communicate Rent Increases Clearly
Rent is naturally one of the most important factors in a renewal decision.

A tenant who receives a significant increase without explanation may immediately compare alternatives.

That does not mean a landlord should avoid necessary rent adjustments. Instead, communicate changes clearly and within the applicable notice requirements.

Explain what is changing, when the new amount applies, and what the tenant needs to do.

Buildium's 2026 renter research found that perceived value is an important factor in moving decisions and that renters are particularly sensitive to larger rent increases.

Where appropriate, property managers can also explain improvements or services that contribute to the property's value.

## Offer Value Instead of Automatically Discounting Rent
Retention incentives do not always need to be a permanent rent reduction.

Depending on the property and applicable laws, alternatives could include:

  - Flexible payment options
  - Resident benefit programs
  - Renewal-related improvements
  - Small property upgrades
  - Additional services
  - Longer lease options
  - Referral programs

The economics should be calculated before offering an incentive.

For example, spending several hundred dollars on a useful improvement may be preferable to accepting a month of vacancy and thousands of dollars in turnover costs.

## Track Resident Satisfaction
Property managers should not wait for a resident to announce that they are leaving before identifying problems.

Short surveys, periodic check-ins, maintenance feedback, and communication records can reveal recurring issues.

Ask residents about:

  - Maintenance responsiveness
  - Communication
  - Property condition
  - Payment convenience
  - Common-area conditions
  - Value for rent
  - Issues they would like addressed

The objective is not to satisfy every request. It is to identify recurring problems that may influence renewal decisions.

## Use Technology to Identify Retention Risks
Property management software can bring resident information together in one place.

Useful indicators include:

  - Repeated maintenance requests
  - Unresolved work orders
  - Frequent complaints
  - Late payments
  - Lease expiration dates
  - Previous renewal history
  - Communication response times
  - Move-out notices

A resident with several unresolved maintenance requests and an approaching lease expiration may require more attention than one with no outstanding issues.

Automation can also trigger renewal reminders and follow-up tasks before deadlines become urgent.

## Maintain the Property Between Turnovers
Retention should not be viewed as a substitute for maintaining the property.

Regular inspections, preventive maintenance, landscaping, exterior upkeep, and timely repairs help preserve the physical asset and improve the resident experience.

Buildium's 2026 research found that maintenance investment was one of the factors that could influence renters who were undecided about renewing.

Spending on maintenance should still be evaluated economically. The objective is to prioritize work that protects the property, addresses safety or compliance requirements, and improves the overall resident experience.

## Treat Good Tenants Consistently and Fairly
Retention strategies should be applied consistently.

Avoid offering materially different terms based on protected characteristics or other inappropriate criteria. Federal fair-housing laws prohibit discrimination in housing based on protected characteristics covered by federal law.

A documented renewal policy can help property managers apply pricing, incentives, screening, and lease procedures consistently.

## Measure Retention With Operating Metrics
A retention program should be measurable.

Track:

**Renewal rate:****
**Number of renewing tenants divided by leases expiring during the measurement period.

**Turnover rate:****
**Number of tenant turnovers compared with the total number of occupied units.

**Days vacant:****
**Average number of days between tenants.

**Cost per turnover:****
**Total turnover-related expenses divided by completed turnovers.

**Renewal incentive cost:****
**Money spent on retention incentives compared with avoided vacancy and turnover expenses.

**Maintenance response time:****
**Time between a request being submitted and the initial response.

These metrics can reveal whether retention efforts are actually improving portfolio economics.

## Build a Repeatable Renewal Workflow
A scalable property management operation should make retention part of the normal leasing process.

A repeatable workflow could look like this:

1.  Identify leases expiring within 120 days.
2.  Review payment, maintenance, and communication history.
3.  Inspect the property's condition.
4.  Compare current rent with relevant market information.
5.  Identify unresolved resident concerns.
6.  Contact the tenant early.
7.  Present renewal terms.
8.  Address reasonable operational concerns.
9.  Record the tenant's decision.
10. Begin turnover preparation immediately if the tenant plans to leave.

This creates a predictable process rather than relying on individual employees to remember renewal dates.

## Final Considerations
Tenant retention is ultimately about managing the resident experience while protecting the economics of the property.

The most effective strategies tend to focus on controllable factors: reliable maintenance, clear communication, convenient payment and lease processes, transparent renewal terms, and consistent property upkeep.

Retention should also be measured financially. A higher renewal rate is useful only when the retained tenancy remains economically appropriate for the property.

By tracking renewal rates, vacancy days, turnover costs, maintenance performance, and resident concerns, property managers can determine where retention efforts are producing measurable value.

For growing portfolios, the objective is not simply to persuade every tenant to stay. It is to create a predictable operating system that gives suitable residents good reasons to renew while allowing property managers to identify problems early and minimize the cost of unnecessary turnover.
