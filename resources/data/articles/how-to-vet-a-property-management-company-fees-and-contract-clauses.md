Hiring a property management company can reduce the daily workload of owning rental property, but the wrong management agreement can create unexpected costs, limited control, and difficult exit conditions. Before handing over leasing, rent collection, maintenance, and tenant communications, landlords should evaluate both the company and the contract.

A management agreement should clearly establish the manager's responsibilities, compensation, authority, reporting obligations, and termination procedures. State requirements vary, so landlords should also confirm licensing and other applicable rules in the property's jurisdiction. California's Department of Real Estate, for example, advises landlords to verify a manager's license, understand additional fees and repair procedures, and ensure the agreement provides regular accounting and a clear termination date.

## Start With the Property Management Company's Track Record
Before discussing fees, evaluate whether the company actually manages properties similar to yours.

Ask how many units the company currently manages, how many employees or property managers handle those units, and whether the portfolio includes properties similar in size, location, age, and tenant profile.

A company that primarily manages large apartment communities may operate very differently from one specializing in single-family rentals. The appropriate management structure can also differ between long-term rentals, student housing, furnished properties, and commercial buildings.

Request references from current or former owner clients. Ask specifically about communication, maintenance costs, vacancy periods, accounting accuracy, and how the company handles disputes with tenants.

Licensing is another important checkpoint. Requirements differ by state, and some property-management activities may require a real estate license or other authorization. Verify the company's credentials with the relevant state regulator rather than relying solely on information provided in a sales presentation.

## Understand How the Management Fee Is Calculated
Management companies commonly structure compensation as a percentage of rent collected, a flat recurring fee, or a combination of fees. California's Department of Real Estate identifies these structures and notes that additional compensation can apply to leasing, renewals, repairs, and other services.

The percentage itself is therefore only one part of the pricing equation.

Ask the manager:

  - Is the percentage calculated from rent actually collected or scheduled rent?
  - Is there a minimum monthly fee?
  - Is there a separate leasing or tenant-placement charge?
  - Is there a lease-renewal fee?
  - Are vacancy periods subject to a management fee?
  - Are late fees shared with the manager?
  - Are application or inspection fees passed to the owner?
  - Are maintenance coordination fees charged?
  - Does the company add a markup to contractor invoices?
  - Are there accounting, technology, or administrative charges?

A lower headline management fee can become more expensive if the contract contains numerous additional charges.

## Examine Maintenance and Repair Markups
Maintenance is one of the areas where seemingly small contractual provisions can materially affect operating expenses.

Determine whether the management company uses its own maintenance personnel, independent contractors, or affiliated vendors. If vendors are used, ask whether the manager receives any compensation, markup, referral fee, or other financial benefit.

The contract should state who approves repairs and whether the manager can authorize work without obtaining owner approval.

For example, an owner might establish a $500 repair authorization threshold, with higher expenses requiring written approval except in genuine emergencies. The exact threshold should reflect the property, available reserves, and owner's preferences.

Also ask whether the manager provides invoices, photographs, estimates, and completion documentation. Transparent documentation makes it easier to distinguish necessary repairs from routine maintenance and capital improvements.

## Review the Leasing and Tenant-Screening Services
A management company may handle advertising, showings, applications, screening, lease preparation, move-in inspections, and security deposits.

These services should be explicitly identified in the agreement.

Ask what screening procedures are used and whether the company follows consistent criteria. Also determine who makes the final decision to approve an applicant.

The agreement should identify who handles:

  - Advertising and listing costs
  - Applicant screening
  - Lease preparation
  - Security deposits
  - Move-in and move-out inspections
  - Lease renewals
  - Notices to tenants
  - Eviction coordination
  - Collection of unpaid rent

The more clearly these responsibilities are documented, the less room there is for disputes about what the management fee actually covers.

## Pay Close Attention to Owner Reporting
A property manager should provide enough financial information for the owner to understand how the property is performing.

The contract should establish the frequency and format of owner statements and explain when rental proceeds are transferred.

California's Department of Real Estate specifically recommends that management agreements require regular accounting reports and that landlords review the accountings they receive.

At a minimum, owners should understand how the reporting system handles:

  - Rental income
  - Management fees
  - Repair expenses
  - Vendor payments
  - Security deposits
  - Owner distributions
  - Outstanding tenant balances
  - Reserve balances

If the company provides an online owner portal, ask whether invoices, leases, inspection reports, and financial statements can be accessed there.

## Review the Contract's Authority Clause
One of the most important sections determines what the manager can do without the owner's approval.

The agreement might authorize the company to sign leases, collect rent, hire contractors, issue notices, negotiate with tenants, or initiate eviction proceedings.

That authority should match the owner's expectations.

For example, an owner may want the manager to handle routine repairs independently but require approval for major renovations, changes to lease terms, settlements, or expenses above a specified threshold.

A written management agreement should clearly establish the manager's authority and responsibilities. State regulatory guidance similarly emphasizes defining the manager's powers, fees, policies, and contractual responsibilities.

## Examine Termination and Renewal Clauses
Do not wait until you want to leave the company before reading the termination section.

Look for:

  - Initial contract term
  - Automatic renewal provisions
  - Required termination notice
  - Early termination fees
  - Conditions permitting immediate termination
  - Treatment of existing leases after termination
  - Transfer of tenant files
  - Transfer of security deposits
  - Transfer of keys and property records
  - Final accounting requirements

A 30- or 60-day notice period may be manageable, while a lengthy automatic renewal combined with a substantial termination fee can significantly reduce flexibility.

The agreement should also explain what happens immediately after termination. HUD's management-agreement guidance for certain multifamily programs illustrates the importance of clearly addressing the transfer of property cash, records, and other assets when a management agreement ends.

## Review Insurance, Liability, and Indemnification
The contract should explain the parties' responsibilities for insurance and liability.

Ask what insurance the management company carries and request evidence of applicable coverage. Depending on the company's activities, relevant policies may include professional liability, general liability, workers' compensation, or other coverage.

Pay particular attention to indemnification language. An overly broad provision could shift substantial responsibility to the property owner.

The agreement should distinguish between ordinary management responsibilities, owner-controlled decisions, contractor actions, tenant conduct, and circumstances outside either party's control.

For substantial portfolios or complicated agreements, having a real estate attorney review the contract before signing can be worthwhile.

## Watch for Conflicts of Interest
A management company may have relationships with maintenance companies, contractors, insurance providers, or other vendors.

That does not automatically make the relationship inappropriate, but the arrangement should be disclosed and understood.

Ask directly:

  - Does the company receive contractor markups?
  - Does it own or affiliate with maintenance companies?
  - Are owners allowed to use their own contractors?
  - Are there referral fees?
  - Can the manager receive commissions from vendors?
  - Does the company have exclusive vendor agreements?

The National Association of REALTORS® has published ethics guidance illustrating why undisclosed profits from property-related supplies or services can create conflicts with an owner's interests.

## Compare Total Cost, Not the Headline Fee
When comparing management companies, create a simple annual cost model for each proposal.

Include:

**Management fees + leasing fees + renewal fees + maintenance charges + markups + administrative fees + inspection fees + other recurring charges**

Then compare the resulting cost against the services included.

For example, a company charging a higher management percentage might include leasing, inspections, routine coordination, and detailed reporting, while another company with a lower percentage could charge separately for many of those services.

The goal is not simply to find the lowest fee. It is to understand exactly what you are purchasing and how the contract affects your property's operating expenses.

## Final Contract Review Checklist
Before signing, confirm that the agreement clearly answers these questions:

1.  What services are included?
2.  How exactly is the management fee calculated?
3.  What additional fees can be charged?
4.  Who controls repair approvals?
5.  Are maintenance markups permitted?
6.  Which vendors can the manager use?
7.  How are tenant applications screened?
8.  Who handles evictions and legal notices?
9.  How often will financial statements be provided?
10. When are owner funds transferred?
11. What authority does the manager have without approval?
12. What insurance does the manager carry?
13. How long is the agreement?
14. How does automatic renewal work?
15. How can either party terminate?
16. What happens to leases, records, keys, deposits, and funds after termination?

A management agreement is not merely an administrative form. It establishes the financial and operational relationship between the property owner and the management company. Carefully comparing the fee structure, delegated authority, maintenance policies, reporting requirements, conflicts of interest, and termination provisions can help an owner avoid surprises after management begins.

Because property-management licensing, landlord-tenant, trust-account, and contract requirements vary by jurisdiction, owners should verify applicable local rules and consider professional legal review before signing a long-term agreement.
