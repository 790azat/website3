Open-concept floor plans remain popular with homeowners who want larger shared living spaces, better sightlines, and more natural interaction between kitchens, dining areas, and living rooms. But creating an open layout can be considerably more complicated when the wall separating those rooms is load-bearing.

Removing a non-structural partition can be relatively straightforward. Removing a load-bearing wall is a structural remodeling project that may require engineering, temporary supports, a replacement beam, new posts, utility relocation, permits, and repairs to floors and ceilings.

Understanding what is behind the wall—and what is carrying the weight above it—is essential before demolition begins.

## What Is a Load-Bearing Wall?
A load-bearing wall supports part of the building's structural load. Depending on the home's design, that load may come from the roof, upper floors, ceiling structure, or other structural components.

The wall transfers that weight downward through the building and ultimately toward the foundation. A non-load-bearing partition, by contrast, primarily divides interior spaces and is not intended to support the structure above.

One clue professionals examine is the direction of floor or ceiling joists. Walls positioned perpendicular to joists may be carrying their load, although this is not a definitive test by itself. Walls can also contain structural components that are not obvious from the room below.

This is why homeowners should not rely solely on tapping the wall, looking at the basement, or guessing based on its location.

## How to Determine Whether a Wall Is Load-Bearing
Before designing an open-concept renovation, a contractor or structural engineer can investigate:

  - Floor and ceiling joist direction
  - Roof framing
  - Walls directly above or below the proposed opening
  - Existing beams and columns
  - Foundation locations
  - Attic framing
  - Basement support structures
  - Architectural or original construction plans
  - Plumbing, electrical, and HVAC systems inside the wall

A structural engineer can inspect the existing structure and determine what support is necessary after the wall is removed. Angi's 2026 guidance reports structural engineering rates of approximately **$70 to $250 per hour**, with costs depending on the project's complexity and location.

For homeowners, paying for an engineering assessment before demolition can be far less expensive than discovering afterward that the structure was improperly supported.

## What Replaces a Load-Bearing Wall?
Removing a load-bearing wall does not mean eliminating the structural support it provides.

Instead, the load normally needs to be transferred to a new structural system. The most common approach is a horizontal beam supported by posts or other structural supports at its ends.

Depending on the span and loads involved, a project may use:

  - LVL (laminated veneer lumber)
  - Glulam
  - Dimensional lumber built into a structural assembly
  - Steel I-beams
  - Other engineered structural members

The correct beam cannot be selected simply because it fits visually. Its required size depends on factors such as span, loads above, the number of floors, joist configuration, and how the beam transfers its load into the foundation.

This Old House specifically recommends having a structural engineer size a replacement beam when removing a load-bearing wall.

## LVL vs. Steel Beams
For many residential renovations, engineered wood beams such as LVL are practical because they can provide substantial structural capacity while integrating relatively well into residential framing.

Angi's 2026 data places professional LVL beam installation at approximately **$1,500 to $4,000** for materials and labor, although the actual project can be higher when permits, engineering, finishing, or structural reinforcement are included.

Steel becomes more attractive when a long span or significant load requires a relatively compact structural member.

Current Angi estimates put professional steel I-beam installation at approximately **$1,200 to $5,350**, with some projects reaching considerably higher totals depending on beam size, access, delivery, structural modifications, and installation complexity.

The least expensive beam is not automatically the best option. The structural engineer should determine which material and configuration can safely support the required loads.

## How Much Does Removing a Load-Bearing Wall Cost?
The cost depends heavily on the home's construction and the size of the opening.

Angi's July 2026 estimates place load-bearing wall removal at roughly **$1,200 to $3,000** for many projects, with costs potentially exceeding **$3,000** in multi-story homes requiring additional structural work. Broader project estimates can reach approximately $10,000 when more complicated structural modifications are included.

The demolition itself is often not the largest expense.

A homeowner may also need to budget for:

| Expense | Potential consideration |
|---|---|
| Structural engineer | Assessment and beam design |
| Building permits | Required by many local jurisdictions |
| Beam | LVL, engineered wood, or steel |
| Posts/columns | May be required at beam ends |
| Foundation reinforcement | Necessary if new point loads exceed existing capacity |
| Electrical relocation | Wiring may run through the wall |
| Plumbing relocation | Pipes can significantly complicate demolition |
| HVAC relocation | Ductwork may need rerouting |
| Drywall and ceiling repair | Required after structural work |
| Flooring repair | Existing flooring may stop at the former wall |
| Painting and finishing | Needed to make the opening look integrated |

For example, Angi estimates structural engineer assessments associated with steel beam projects at approximately **$350 to $800**, while permits can add roughly $150 to $2,000 depending on the project and location.

## Why Utilities Can Change the Budget
A wall can be structurally removable but still expensive to relocate.

Electrical wiring is common inside interior walls. Plumbing pipes, gas lines, HVAC ducts, and ventilation systems can also pass through the proposed opening.

The Home Depot's current guidance recommends determining whether the wall contains electrical, plumbing, or ductwork before demolition because rerouting these systems can materially change the project's cost and complexity.

A kitchen wall is particularly important to investigate. Removing it may expose plumbing serving a sink or dishwasher, gas lines serving a range, electrical circuits, and ventilation systems.

In some cases, the cost of relocating utilities may make a partial opening more economical than completely removing the wall.

## Partial Openings Can Be an Alternative
Homeowners do not necessarily have to choose between keeping the entire wall and removing it completely.

A partial opening can create a more connected floor plan while preserving some structural support. Options can include:

  - Wide pass-through openings
  - Half walls
  - Large cased openings
  - Structural columns
  - Kitchen islands replacing part of the wall
  - Beam-and-post configurations

This approach can reduce the required beam span and potentially simplify the structural design.

However, even a large opening in a load-bearing wall may require engineering and permits. The structural requirements depend on the resulting opening and the loads carried above it.

## Permits and Inspections
Structural remodeling commonly involves building-code requirements.

The Home Depot notes that most areas require building permits for removing load-bearing walls, and inspections may be required during or after the work.

Permit requirements and fees vary by municipality, so homeowners should contact their local building department before demolition.

A typical process may look like:

1.  Initial structural assessment
2.  Architectural planning
3.  Structural calculations
4.  Engineering drawings
5.  Permit submission
6.  Permit approval
7.  Temporary structural support
8.  Wall demolition
9.  Beam and post installation
10. Electrical/plumbing/HVAC work
11. Inspection
12. Drywall, flooring, and finish repairs

Skipping permits can create problems when selling or refinancing the property if structural alterations were performed without the required approvals.

## How Long Does an Open-Concept Remodel Take?
The actual wall removal may take only a few days, but the complete project can take substantially longer.

Engineering and design must happen before demolition. Permit processing can add additional time, and utility relocation and finish work can extend the schedule.

This Old House classifies load-bearing wall removal as an expert-level project and emphasizes temporary support during demolition and proper redistribution of the structural load.

For homeowners planning around work, children, or temporary housing, the important timeline is therefore not "how long does it take to knock down the wall?" but **how long until the entire room is finished and usable again?**

## Is an Open-Concept Remodel Worth the Cost?
The financial value depends on the property and the renovation.

Removing a wall can make a relatively small home feel significantly larger without adding square footage. It can also improve sightlines and create a more functional kitchen and living area.

However, homeowners should avoid assuming that every remodeling dollar will return dollar-for-dollar through higher resale value.

The strongest financial case usually occurs when the renovation solves a genuine functional problem while remaining appropriate for the neighborhood and the home's overall price level.

A $40,000 structural remodel may make sense in a property where buyers expect modernized kitchens and connected living areas. A much larger investment may be harder to justify if the surrounding properties have substantially different layouts and price points.

## Final Considerations
An open-concept floor plan can dramatically change how a home feels, but removing a load-bearing wall is fundamentally a **structural engineering project**, not simply a demolition project.

Before starting, confirm whether the wall carries structural loads, identify hidden utilities, obtain an engineered beam design, investigate local permit requirements, and calculate the complete project cost—including finishing work after the wall is gone.

Current 2026 estimates suggest that load-bearing wall removal can range from several thousand dollars to substantially more when structural reinforcement, utilities, engineering, and finishing are involved.

The most important budgeting lesson is simple: **price the entire structural system, not just the beam**. The beam may be the visible centerpiece of an open-concept remodel, but engineering, posts, foundation support, permits, utility relocation, demolition, and finishing can determine the actual project cost.
