Heating and cooling equipment often fails when it is working hardest. An air conditioner may break down during the first major heat wave, while a furnace or heat pump can develop problems just as winter temperatures fall.

Preventive maintenance helps landlords and homeowners identify worn components, airflow problems, clogged filters, electrical issues, drainage problems, and other conditions before they become emergency repairs. ENERGY STAR recommends annual pre-season professional checkups, with cooling equipment serviced in spring and heating equipment checked in fall.

A practical maintenance program should combine simple monthly inspections with seasonal professional service.

## Check the Air Filter Regularly
The air filter is one of the simplest HVAC components to maintain and one of the easiest to overlook.

ENERGY STAR recommends checking filters monthly, particularly during periods of heavy heating or cooling use. A dirty filter should be cleaned if it is reusable or replaced when the system requires a disposable filter. At minimum, ENERGY STAR recommends replacement or cleaning every three months, although the appropriate interval depends on the equipment, filter, home conditions, and manufacturer instructions.

A clogged filter restricts airflow and can make the system work harder. In forced-air systems, inadequate airflow can contribute to higher operating costs, equipment wear, and comfort problems.

For rental properties, landlords should make sure tenants know where the filter is located and which replacement type the system requires.

## Inspect the Thermostat
Before each heating and cooling season, test the thermostat.

Check that:

  - The display operates normally.
  - Heating responds when the system is placed in heat mode.
  - Cooling responds when cooling is selected.
  - The system cycles on and off normally.
  - Programmable schedules are appropriate.
  - Batteries are replaced when necessary.

If the thermostat calls for heating or cooling but the equipment does not respond, investigate the problem before the system is needed continuously.

Smart thermostats can also provide additional scheduling and monitoring capabilities. ENERGY STAR notes that certified smart thermostats can provide temperature control, energy-use information, and remote access through connected devices.

## Clear the Outdoor Unit
Outdoor HVAC equipment needs adequate airflow.

Before summer, inspect the area around the condenser or heat-pump outdoor unit. Remove leaves, grass clippings, branches, and other debris that could obstruct airflow.

ENERGY STAR recommends keeping indoor and outdoor equipment clear of dust, snow, leaves, and other debris.

Landscaping should also be managed so vegetation does not gradually grow into the equipment's operating area.

Do not bend or aggressively clean delicate condenser fins. If the outdoor coil is heavily contaminated, professional cleaning may be more appropriate.

## Clean and Inspect Coils
Dirty coils can reduce heat transfer and force cooling equipment to operate longer.

ENERGY STAR's maintenance checklist recommends cleaning the evaporator and condenser coils as part of professional cooling-system maintenance. Dirty coils can reduce cooling performance, increase energy consumption, and contribute to premature equipment wear.

A professional should also evaluate the system's refrigerant charge where applicable. Refrigerant issues are not simply a matter of adding refrigerant; a qualified technician should identify why the charge is outside manufacturer specifications and address leaks or other problems when necessary.

## Check Condensate Drainage
Air conditioners and heat pumps operating in cooling mode produce condensation that must drain away properly.

A clogged condensate drain can cause water to accumulate around the indoor equipment, potentially resulting in property damage and elevated indoor humidity. ENERGY STAR specifically includes condensate-drain inspection in its recommended professional maintenance checklist.

Landlords should pay particular attention to systems located above finished ceilings, in attics, or near areas where a drain overflow could damage flooring, drywall, or other building components.

Signs of a drainage problem can include:

  - Water around the indoor unit
  - Ceiling or wall staining
  - Musty odors
  - Unexpectedly high indoor humidity
  - A safety float switch shutting down the system

These conditions warrant investigation rather than simply restarting the equipment.

## Summer HVAC Maintenance Checklist
Before the cooling season begins, work through the following list:

**□ Replace or clean the air filter**

Check the filter and install the appropriate replacement if necessary.

**□ Inspect the outdoor condenser**

Remove leaves, vegetation, and debris that restrict airflow.

**□ Check supply and return vents**

Make sure furniture, rugs, curtains, or other objects are not blocking airflow.

**□ Inspect condensate drainage**

Look for signs of blockage, leakage, or water accumulation.

**□ Test cooling operation**

Run the system before extreme temperatures arrive and confirm that it reaches the expected operating condition.

**□ Schedule professional service**

Have a qualified HVAC contractor inspect the system before peak cooling demand. ENERGY STAR recommends spring checkups because contractors can become busy once hot weather arrives.

**□ Check unusual sounds**

Grinding, rattling, buzzing, squealing, or repeated cycling can indicate developing mechanical or electrical problems.

## Winter HVAC Maintenance Checklist
Heating systems deserve the same preparation before cold weather arrives.

**□ Replace or clean the filter**

A restricted filter can reduce airflow and increase system workload.

**□ Test the heating system**

Turn the system on before the first severe cold spell rather than waiting for an emergency.

**□ Inspect vents and registers**

Make sure heated-air outlets and return-air openings are not blocked.

**□ Check thermostat operation**

Verify that the thermostat properly calls for heat and that the system cycles normally.

**□ Inspect combustion equipment when applicable**

For furnaces or boilers using gas or oil, professional maintenance can include checking fuel connections, pressure, burner combustion, and the heat exchanger. ENERGY STAR identifies improperly operating fuel connections as a safety concern and recommends professional inspection of these components.

**□ Inspect the heat exchanger**

A cracked or damaged heat exchanger can create significant safety concerns and should be evaluated by a qualified technician.

**□ Schedule fall service**

ENERGY STAR recommends heating-system checkups in fall, before the equipment is needed during sustained cold weather.

## Heat Pump Maintenance Requires Year-Round Attention
Heat pumps operate differently from systems that provide heating and cooling through completely separate equipment.

Because an air-source heat pump can provide both heating and cooling, maintenance is relevant throughout the year. ENERGY STAR recommends keeping filters clean and using the appropriate operating mode rather than relying on automatic switching between heating and cooling during unusual weather.

Outdoor heat-pump units also need adequate clearance. During winter, snow, ice, leaves, and other debris should not obstruct the equipment.

If the system frequently struggles to maintain the selected temperature, develops unusual frost patterns, or repeatedly switches into abnormal operating cycles, professional evaluation may be necessary.

## Inspect Ducts and Airflow
HVAC maintenance is not limited to the equipment itself.

Duct leaks, damaged insulation, disconnected sections, and blocked registers can reduce the amount of conditioned air reaching living spaces.

ENERGY STAR reports that sealing and insulating ducts can improve HVAC efficiency, particularly where ductwork passes through unconditioned areas such as attics, crawlspaces, garages, or unfinished basements.

During a seasonal inspection, look for:

  - Torn or disconnected duct insulation
  - Visible gaps at duct joints
  - Collapsed flexible ductwork
  - Poor airflow from individual registers
  - Excessive temperature differences between rooms
  - Air leakage around accessible connections

Significant duct repairs should generally be evaluated by an HVAC professional.

## Know Which Problems Require a Technician
Routine maintenance can be handled by a homeowner or property manager in many cases, but certain components should be left to qualified professionals.

Call an HVAC contractor when you encounter:

  - Refrigerant problems
  - Electrical faults
  - Gas or oil combustion issues
  - Repeated breaker trips
  - A suspected cracked heat exchanger
  - Major water leakage
  - Compressor problems
  - Significant airflow deficiencies
  - Persistent short cycling
  - Unusual burning smells
  - Equipment that repeatedly shuts down

Never bypass safety controls simply to keep an HVAC system operating.

## Keep a Seasonal Maintenance Record
For rental properties, maintenance records can be especially valuable.

Create a file for each HVAC system containing:

  - Equipment make and model
  - Installation date, if known
  - Filter size
  - Service dates
  - Contractor invoices
  - Repairs performed
  - Parts replaced
  - Warranty information
  - Refrigerant-related service records
  - Recurring problems
  - Expected replacement timeframe

This history can help identify whether a system is experiencing isolated problems or approaching the point where continued repairs no longer make economic sense.

ENERGY STAR recommends considering professional evaluation when older equipment is no longer maintaining comfort or operating efficiently.

## Final Considerations
Preventing HVAC breakdowns is primarily about preparation rather than emergency repair.

Check filters regularly, keep outdoor equipment clear, monitor drainage, test heating and cooling before extreme weather, inspect airflow, and schedule professional seasonal service. These steps can identify developing problems before they become an emergency during the hottest or coldest part of the year.

For landlords, a written maintenance schedule can also reduce tenant complaints and unexpected repair costs. Documenting service history makes it easier to budget for repairs and eventual equipment replacement.

HVAC systems differ significantly by equipment type, manufacturer, climate, and installation. Follow the manufacturer's maintenance instructions and use qualified HVAC professionals for electrical, combustion, refrigerant, and other specialized work.
