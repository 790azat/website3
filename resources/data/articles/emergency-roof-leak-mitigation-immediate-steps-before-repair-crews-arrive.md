A roof leak can turn a relatively small roofing problem into extensive interior damage within hours. Water can spread through insulation, ceilings, drywall, electrical components, and flooring before a roofing contractor arrives.

The goal during an emergency is **not to perform a permanent roof repair yourself**. It is to reduce additional damage, protect people and belongings, document the condition, and make the property as safe as possible until qualified professionals can inspect and repair the roof.

Temporary roof coverings can provide short-term protection after storm damage. FEMA describes temporary plastic sheeting and tarps as measures intended to protect a damaged building and its contents until permanent repairs can be completed.

## 1. Find the Source of the Interior Leak
The location where water appears inside the house is not necessarily where the roof is leaking.

Water can travel along roof decking, rafters, insulation, plumbing penetrations, and ceiling framing before entering the living space. A stain on a bedroom ceiling, for example, could originate several feet away on the roof.

Start by identifying:

  - Where water is entering
  - Whether the leak is actively dripping
  - Whether the affected ceiling is sagging
  - Whether multiple rooms are affected
  - Whether the leak becomes worse during rainfall
  - Whether water is near electrical fixtures or wiring

Avoid cutting into a ceiling simply to locate the leak unless an emergency professional advises you to do so. A water-filled ceiling can release a large amount of water unexpectedly.

## 2. Protect People From Electrical Hazards
Water and electricity create a particularly dangerous combination.

If water is entering around:

  - Ceiling lights
  - Electrical outlets
  - Switches
  - Appliances
  - Electrical wiring
  - The electrical panel

keep people away from the affected area.

If it is safe to do so, power to the affected circuit may need to be switched off. However, **do not touch electrical equipment while standing in water or if the panel itself is wet**.

A qualified electrician should evaluate electrical equipment that has been exposed to significant water.

The same principle applies to extension cords and temporary electrical equipment. Do not run electrical cables through wet areas simply to operate fans or pumps.

## 3. Contain Water Inside the Home
Once the area is safe, concentrate on limiting interior damage.

Place buckets or waterproof containers beneath active drips. Use towels, plastic sheeting, or other absorbent materials around the affected area to prevent water from spreading across flooring.

Move furniture, electronics, artwork, rugs, and other vulnerable belongings away from the leak.

If water is accumulating on a ceiling, do not stand directly underneath it. A visibly sagging ceiling may indicate that water has collected above the ceiling material.

If the ceiling begins to bulge significantly, evacuate the immediate area and contact a professional.

## 4. Document the Damage Before Cleanup
Take photographs and videos as soon as practical.

Document:

  - Exterior roof damage visible from the ground
  - Interior ceiling stains
  - Active leaks
  - Wet flooring
  - Damaged walls
  - Damaged furniture
  - Damaged personal property
  - Fallen or missing roofing materials
  - Broken skylights or roof penetrations
  - Water around electrical fixtures

Try to capture both close-up photographs and wider images showing the location of the damage.

If an insurance claim may be involved, this documentation can help establish what occurred and what property was affected.

Keep receipts for emergency materials and services as well.

## 5. Do Not Climb Onto a Wet or Damaged Roof
This is one of the most important emergency precautions.

A wet roof can become extremely slippery, and storm damage may compromise the structural integrity of the roof deck. Wind can also leave loose materials, exposed nails, broken shingles, or other hazards.

OSHA emphasizes that roof repair involves significant fall hazards and that the structural integrity of the roof should be evaluated before workers begin repairs.

For homeowners, this means a leaking roof is generally **not a reason to climb onto the roof with a ladder and attempt an emergency repair during bad weather**.

If conditions include rain, ice, snow, strong winds, or lightning, stay off the roof.

## 6. Use a Temporary Tarp Only When It Can Be Done Safely
A tarp can sometimes provide short-term protection while waiting for permanent repairs.

FEMA's disaster-recovery guidance recognizes plastic sheeting and tarps as temporary measures intended to prevent additional weather damage until permanent repairs are made.

However, installing a tarp requires working at height and securing material against wind. Improperly installed tarps can become dangerous or fail to stop water.

OSHA's guidance for roof tarping and repair includes fall-protection measures and controlled work areas, demonstrating that even temporary weatherproofing is a genuine roofing operation rather than a simple DIY task.

For most homeowners, the safer approach is to have a roofing contractor or emergency mitigation company install the temporary covering.

## 7. Create a Safe Interior Drying Area
Once active water intrusion has been controlled, begin drying affected areas.

Depending on the situation, this can involve:

  - Fans
  - Dehumidifiers
  - Air circulation
  - Removal of standing water
  - Moving wet belongings to dry locations
  - Separating wet materials from unaffected materials

Do not direct electrical equipment into standing water or use damaged electrical outlets.

Wet insulation, drywall, flooring, and other porous materials may require professional assessment if they remain wet for an extended period.

The longer building materials remain saturated, the greater the potential for secondary damage.

## 8. Call the Right Professionals
A roof leak may involve more than one type of contractor.

A typical emergency response may require:

**Roofing contractor:**
For locating the exterior source of the leak, temporary weatherproofing, and permanent roof repair.

**Water-damage restoration company:**
For substantial interior water extraction, drying, moisture assessment, and damaged-material removal.

**Electrician:**
When water has entered electrical equipment, wiring, outlets, lighting, or the electrical panel.

**Structural professional:**
When there is significant roof-deck, framing, ceiling, or structural damage.

If a storm caused widespread damage, contacting the contractor as quickly as possible can be important because emergency roofing services may become heavily booked.

## 9. Ask for a Temporary-Mitigation Plan
When the roofing contractor arrives, ask what will be done immediately versus permanently.

A useful emergency plan might include:

| Immediate measure | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Roof inspection | Identify likely entry point |
| Temporary tarp | Reduce additional rain exposure |
| Temporary patch | Stop a localized intrusion |
| Damaged-material removal | Prevent further interior damage |
| Moisture assessment | Identify wet building materials |
| Permanent repair estimate | Establish the long-term solution |

Do not assume that a tarp or emergency patch constitutes a completed roof repair.

Temporary protection is designed to buy time.

## 10. Understand When the House May Be Unsafe
A roof leak can sometimes indicate a larger structural problem.

Leave the affected area and seek professional assistance if you observe:

  - A severely sagging ceiling
  - Significant roof deformation
  - Large sections of collapsed ceiling
  - Exposed electrical wiring
  - Smoke or electrical burning odors
  - Major structural movement
  - Large amounts of water entering the home
  - A roof partially collapsed by a storm
  - Unsafe conditions around damaged roofing materials

FEMA's temporary-roofing guidance specifically limits temporary roof assistance to structures that are sufficiently sound for crews to work on them, reinforcing the importance of evaluating structural condition before temporary roof work begins.

## Emergency Roof Leak Checklist
Use this sequence when a roof leak occurs:

**First — protect people**

  - Stay away from sagging ceilings.
  - Keep children and pets away from the affected area.
  - Avoid wet electrical equipment.
  - Do not climb onto a damaged or slippery roof.

**Second — control the water**

  - Place containers under active drips.
  - Protect flooring and furniture.
  - Move valuables away from the affected area.
  - Stop using affected electrical equipment.

**Third — document everything**

  - Photograph interior damage.
  - Photograph visible exterior damage from a safe location.
  - Record when the leak began.
  - Keep receipts for emergency services and materials.

**Fourth — call professionals**

  - Contact a roofing contractor.
  - Arrange water mitigation if necessary.
  - Contact an electrician if electrical equipment was exposed to water.
  - Contact your insurer if you intend to make a claim.

**Fifth — prevent additional damage**

  - Use professional temporary roof covering where necessary.
  - Begin safe drying and ventilation.
  - Follow the contractor's instructions until permanent repairs are completed.

## Final Considerations
The first objective after a roof leak is **damage control, not permanent repair**.

Start by keeping people away from dangerous areas, protecting electrical equipment, containing interior water, documenting the damage, and contacting qualified professionals. Avoid climbing onto a wet or damaged roof simply to place a tarp or patch a leak. OSHA identifies falls as a major hazard in residential roofing work and emphasizes appropriate fall protection and assessment of roof conditions before repair activities begin.

Temporary tarps and other emergency coverings can help prevent additional weather exposure, but they are temporary measures. FEMA's guidance similarly describes temporary roof coverings as protection until permanent repairs can be arranged.

Once the immediate emergency is under control, have the roof professionally inspected. The visible leak may be only the symptom; the underlying problem could involve damaged shingles, flashing, penetrations, roof decking, gutters, or storm-related structural damage.
