Smart-home technology has moved from a novelty feature to a practical property-management tool. For rental owners, two of the most useful technologies are **keyless entry systems and smart thermostats**.

Keyless locks can simplify tenant move-ins, maintenance access, and lockout management. Smart thermostats can give residents greater control over heating and cooling while helping manage energy consumption.

But installing connected devices in a rental property is not simply a matter of buying the newest technology. Landlords need to consider installation costs, tenant usability, cybersecurity, privacy, maintenance, internet connectivity, and how the technology fits into the property's overall operating model.

## Why Smart Technology Can Make Sense for Rental Properties
Traditional locks and thermostats are inexpensive and familiar, so replacing them only makes financial sense when the additional functionality provides a measurable benefit.

For landlords managing multiple units, the advantages can include:

  - Remote access management
  - Easier tenant turnover
  - Reduced physical-key management
  - Faster maintenance access
  - Better control over vacant units
  - More convenient temperature management
  - Potential energy savings
  - Fewer routine administrative tasks

ENERGY STAR notes that smart thermostats can automatically adjust heating and cooling schedules and provide remote control and energy-use information.

The strongest business case generally comes when technology solves an existing operational problem rather than being installed simply because it is marketed as a premium feature.

## Keyless Entry: How It Works for Landlords
Keyless entry systems replace or supplement traditional keys with digital access methods.

Depending on the product, tenants may use:

  - PIN codes
  - Smartphone apps
  - Bluetooth
  - NFC
  - Fingerprints
  - Temporary access codes
  - Physical backup keys

For rental properties, keypad-based systems are often particularly practical because a landlord can assign a unique code to each tenant instead of distributing physical keys.

When a tenant moves out, the code can be deactivated rather than requiring the landlord to collect and replace keys.

## Benefits of Keyless Entry for Rental Properties
### Easier Tenant Turnover
A landlord can create a new access code before a tenant moves in and deactivate the previous resident's credentials after move-out.

This can make turnover more efficient, particularly for landlords managing several properties.

### Easier Maintenance Access
Property managers can sometimes provide a contractor with temporary access rather than physically meeting the technician at the property.

For example, a plumber could receive access for a scheduled repair window and have the credential expire afterward.

This can reduce unnecessary travel and make maintenance scheduling easier.

### Better Lockout Management
Lockouts can create after-hours calls for landlords and property managers.

With an appropriately configured smart lock, a tenant may be able to regain access without waiting for a property manager or locksmith.

That does not eliminate every lockout problem, since batteries, connectivity, mechanical failures, and forgotten codes can still occur. A reliable backup-entry procedure remains important.

### Easier Access Changes
If a tenant loses a physical key, a traditional lock may need to be rekeyed depending on the security circumstances.

With a digital system, credentials can generally be changed without replacing the entire locking mechanism.

The exact capabilities depend on the lock and access-management platform.

## What Landlords Should Look for in a Smart Lock
The cheapest smart lock is not necessarily the best choice for a rental portfolio.

Look for:

**Reliable offline operation:** The lock should have a reasonable method of operating if the property's internet connection goes down.

**Battery alerts:** The system should notify the appropriate person before batteries become critically low.

**Temporary credentials:** Useful for maintenance contractors, cleaners, and property managers.

**Access management:** Owners should be able to deactivate former tenant credentials.

**Audit information:** Depending on the system, access records may help establish when credentials were used.

**Physical backup:** A mechanical override or another emergency access method can be valuable.

**Weather resistance:** Exterior locks need to be appropriate for the property's environment.

**Long-term support:** The manufacturer should provide firmware and software updates for the expected life of the device.

The FTC recommends changing default credentials, using two-factor authentication where available, keeping devices updated, and disabling unused connected features.

## Smart Thermostats for Rental Properties
A smart thermostat connects heating and cooling controls to software, typically allowing temperature settings to be adjusted through a device or app.

ENERGY STAR-certified smart thermostats are independently evaluated using field data and are required to meet criteria related to scheduling, temperature accuracy, energy feedback, and energy savings.

For landlords, the technology can be useful in both occupied and vacant properties.

### Tenant Comfort
Residents can adjust temperature settings without physically interacting with a traditional thermostat.

Depending on the system, they may also be able to establish schedules or receive information about HVAC operation.

### Vacant-Unit Management
A vacant rental does not necessarily need the same heating or cooling schedule as an occupied property.

A smart thermostat can allow the property manager to monitor and adjust settings remotely, helping prevent unnecessary HVAC operation between tenants.

However, landlords should not shut systems down in a way that creates risks such as frozen pipes, excessive humidity, or property damage.

### Maintenance Monitoring
Some connected thermostats provide information about HVAC runtime or system behavior.

ENERGY STAR-certified smart thermostats are required to provide information about HVAC energy use, such as monthly runtime.

This information can potentially help identify unusual operation before it develops into a larger maintenance problem.

For example, an HVAC system running substantially longer than usual could justify an inspection.

## The C-Wire and Installation Question
One practical issue landlords should investigate before purchasing smart thermostats is compatibility with the existing HVAC system.

Many smart thermostats require a consistent source of electrical power, and ENERGY STAR notes that most—but not all—models use a C-wire or common wire. The absence of a C-wire does not automatically prevent installation, but it can affect which models are compatible or require an adapter.

For a rental portfolio, standardizing on compatible equipment can simplify future maintenance.

Instead of installing a different thermostat in every property, landlords may benefit from selecting a small number of approved models that technicians already understand.

## Tenant Privacy Matters
Smart-home devices create an additional consideration that traditional locks and thermostats do not: **data**.

Connected devices can potentially record information about access events, device usage, temperature settings, occupancy-related behavior, or other activity depending on the product.

The FTC has highlighted privacy and security risks associated with Internet-connected devices and recommends practices such as limiting unnecessary data collection and protecting devices against unauthorized access.

Landlords should therefore avoid treating smart-home data as an unrestricted source of tenant surveillance.

A sensible policy should define:

  - What information is collected
  - Who can access it
  - How long records are retained
  - Why the information is needed
  - Which vendors receive the information
  - How tenant access credentials are handled

Cameras and audio-enabled devices require particular caution because they create substantially greater privacy concerns than a basic keypad lock or thermostat.

## Be Careful With Smart-Home Fees
Smart-home technology can be incorporated into rental operations, but landlords should be transparent about costs.

The FTC's enforcement action involving Invitation Homes included allegations concerning undisclosed rental fees associated with services including smart-home technology and utility management.

The practical lesson for landlords is straightforward: **do not hide mandatory technology charges inside an advertised rental price or disclose them only after the tenant has committed to the property.**

Any mandatory recurring charge should be handled according to applicable pricing, leasing, and consumer-protection requirements.

## Build a Backup Plan
Smart technology should make a property easier to operate—not create a single point of failure.

A rental property's access system should have a contingency plan for:

  - Dead batteries
  - Internet outages
  - App failures
  - Lost smartphones
  - Forgotten PINs
  - Hardware failures
  - Software-account problems
  - Manufacturer discontinuation

The same principle applies to smart thermostats.

If the Wi-Fi network fails, the heating or cooling system should still have an appropriate method of operating. ENERGY STAR's certification criteria specifically require certified smart thermostats to function as a basic thermostat even without connectivity to the service provider.

## Standardize Technology Across the Portfolio
Landlords with multiple properties should consider standardization before buying devices.

Using one approved smart-lock platform and one thermostat platform can simplify:

  - Staff training
  - Tenant instructions
  - Maintenance
  - Replacement parts
  - Software administration
  - Account management
  - Troubleshooting

A centralized property-management workflow can also connect smart-home operations with tenant turnover.

For example:

**Move-out → deactivate tenant credentials → inspect property → complete repairs → create contractor access → complete cleaning → activate new tenant credentials → provide tenant instructions.**

This is much more efficient than treating every smart device as an independent piece of technology.

## Calculate the Financial Return
Smart-home upgrades should be evaluated like any other capital expenditure.

Consider:

**Upfront equipment + installation + subscription costs + expected replacement costs**

against:

**Potential maintenance savings + reduced administrative time + energy savings + improved turnover efficiency + potential rental-market appeal.**

Suppose a smart-lock installation costs $300 per unit and saves several maintenance trips per year. The value may come primarily from operational savings rather than higher rent.

A smart thermostat may provide value through energy management and HVAC monitoring rather than allowing a landlord to charge a specific premium.

The financial case should therefore be based on the property's actual operating model.

## Smart Home Technology vs. Traditional Equipment
| Feature | Traditional System | Smart System |
|---|---|---|
| Physical keys | Usually required | Often reduced |
| Remote access | Limited | Usually available |
| Temporary contractor access | Difficult | Often available |
| Tenant turnover | Key collection/rekeying | Credential management |
| Temperature control | Local | Local + remote, depending on model |
| Energy scheduling | Manual/programmed | Automated options |
| Internet dependency | None | Varies |
| Cybersecurity considerations | Minimal | Important |
| Upfront cost | Lower | Higher |
| Maintenance complexity | Lower | Higher |

Neither approach is universally appropriate. A single low-cost rental may not justify extensive smart-home infrastructure, while a larger portfolio can benefit from centralized access and maintenance tools.

## Final Considerations
Keyless entry and smart thermostats can make rental properties easier to manage, particularly when landlords are dealing with frequent turnover, multiple maintenance vendors, or vacant units.

The best implementation is usually practical rather than excessive.

Choose reliable devices, standardize where possible, maintain physical or operational backups, secure connected accounts, communicate clearly with tenants, and evaluate the total cost of ownership before installing technology across an entire portfolio.

Smart-home technology should ultimately solve a property-management problem. When it reduces friction for tenants and landlords without creating unnecessary privacy, security, or maintenance risks, it can become a useful part of a modern rental-property strategy.
