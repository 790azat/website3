Your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio is one of the key measurements mortgage lenders use when evaluating a home loan application. It compares your monthly debt obligations with your gross monthly income and helps lenders assess how much of your income is already committed to debt payments.

The calculation itself is straightforward, but mortgage underwriting can be more complicated because lenders may use specific rules for qualifying income, debts, housing expenses, and loan programs.

Understanding your DTI before applying for a mortgage can help you estimate how a lender may view your application, identify debts that could affect your borrowing capacity, and compare different home financing options.

## What Is a Debt-to-Income Ratio?
Your DTI ratio is calculated by dividing your total monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income.

The basic formula is:

**DTI = Total Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income × 100**

For example, if your qualifying monthly debt payments total $2,400 and your gross monthly income is $7,000:

**$2,400 ÷ $7,000 × 100 = 34.3% DTI**

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) describes DTI as one way lenders measure a borrower's ability to manage the monthly payments required to repay borrowed money. Different lenders and loan programs can have different DTI requirements.

## Step 1: Calculate Your Gross Monthly Income
The first step is determining your **gross monthly income**, which generally means income before taxes and other payroll deductions.

If you earn a fixed annual salary, divide your annual income by 12.

For example:

  - Annual salary: $96,000
  - Gross monthly income: $8,000

If you receive variable income, commissions, bonuses, self-employment income, rental income, or other qualifying income, the lender may have specific rules for determining how much can be included.

Mortgage underwriting therefore does not necessarily use the same income number you see on a monthly bank statement.

For a mortgage application, lenders generally verify income and assets rather than relying solely on an applicant's own calculation. The CFPB notes that lenders must consider and verify income or assets and debts when determining a borrower's ability to repay for covered mortgage transactions.

## Step 2: List Your Monthly Debt Payments
Next, identify the monthly obligations that may be included in your mortgage DTI calculation.

Common debts can include:

  - Existing mortgage payments
  - Auto loans
  - Credit card payments
  - Student loans
  - Personal loans
  - Other installment loans
  - Certain lines of credit
  - Alimony or child support when applicable

Mortgage-related obligations can also include the proposed payment for the new home.

The CFPB's regulatory guidance defines total monthly debt obligations for ability-to-repay purposes as including the covered mortgage payment, mortgage-related obligations, simultaneous loans, current debt obligations, alimony, and child support.

Not every expense in your household budget is necessarily treated as a debt for DTI purposes.

For example, groceries, utilities, phone bills, and entertainment expenses may affect your real-world affordability but are generally not treated the same way as installment or revolving debt in the standard DTI calculation.

## Step 3: Calculate the Proposed Housing Payment
When applying for a mortgage, lenders generally evaluate the payment associated with the **new mortgage**, not simply your current housing expense.

The qualifying housing payment can include more than principal and interest.

Depending on the loan, it may include:

  - Principal
  - Interest
  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Mortgage insurance
  - Homeowners association dues
  - Certain other required housing expenses

The CFPB notes that the total monthly payment sent to a mortgage company can be higher than principal and interest because it may include property taxes and homeowners insurance.

This distinction is important when estimating your future DTI.

## Step 4: Add Your Monthly Debts
Suppose your gross monthly income is **$8,000** and your anticipated housing payment is **$2,200**.

You also have:

  - Auto loan: $450
  - Student loan: $300
  - Credit card payments: $250

Your total monthly obligations would be:

**$2,200 + $450 + $300 + $250 = $3,200**

Your DTI would then be:

**$3,200 ÷ $8,000 × 100 = 40%**

Your estimated total DTI would therefore be **40%**.

## Front-End and Back-End DTI
Mortgage discussions sometimes distinguish between a housing ratio and a total DTI ratio.

The **housing ratio** focuses primarily on the proposed housing expense relative to gross income.

The **total DTI**, sometimes called the back-end ratio, incorporates the housing payment plus other qualifying debts.

For example, with $8,000 in gross monthly income:

**Housing payment:** $2,200
**Other monthly debts:** $1,000
**Total monthly obligations:** $3,200

Housing ratio:

**$2,200 ÷ $8,000 = 27.5%**

Total DTI:

**$3,200 ÷ $8,000 = 40%**

Lenders can evaluate both housing expenses and total debt obligations depending on the mortgage program and underwriting system.

## What DTI Is Acceptable for a Mortgage?
There is no single DTI percentage that applies to every mortgage.

Requirements vary by lender, loan type, underwriting method, credit profile, and other factors.

For example, Fannie Mae's current Selling Guide states that manually underwritten loans generally have a maximum total DTI of **36%**, with the possibility of going up to **45%** when specified credit score and reserve requirements are satisfied. For loans underwritten through Fannie Mae's Desktop Underwriter (DU), the maximum allowable DTI is **50%**, subject to the applicable requirements.

These figures should not be treated as universal mortgage limits. FHA, VA, USDA, jumbo, conventional, and other mortgage programs can have different underwriting requirements.

The CFPB also explains that federal ability-to-repay rules do not prescribe one universal DTI threshold that every creditor must use.

## How a Higher DTI Can Affect Your Mortgage
A higher DTI indicates that a larger share of your gross income is already committed to debt obligations.

That can affect:

  - Mortgage eligibility
  - Maximum borrowing amount
  - Loan program availability
  - Required compensating factors
  - Underwriting review
  - The amount of home a lender is willing to finance

A lender may also consider factors beyond DTI, including credit history, assets, employment, loan-to-value ratio, reserves, and the characteristics of the mortgage.

This means DTI should be viewed as one part of the mortgage qualification process rather than a standalone approval test.

## How Credit Cards Affect DTI
Credit card debt can have a meaningful effect on mortgage qualification because lenders generally consider required monthly payments on revolving debt.

Suppose your gross monthly income is $7,500 and your proposed housing payment plus other debts totals $2,700.

Your DTI would be:

**$2,700 ÷ $7,500 × 100 = 36%**

If an additional credit-card obligation of $300 is included, your total monthly obligations become $3,000:

**$3,000 ÷ $7,500 × 100 = 40%**

The difference can matter when you're close to a lender's applicable underwriting threshold.

## How Student Loans Affect DTI
Student loans can also affect mortgage qualification.

The exact payment used for underwriting can depend on the loan program and circumstances. Lenders may use an actual documented payment when applicable or apply program-specific rules when the payment is deferred, income-driven, or otherwise difficult to establish.

Because student-loan treatment varies, borrowers should ask their lender how their particular payment will be calculated rather than assuming the amount shown on a recent statement will always be used.

## How Auto Loans Affect DTI
Auto loans are generally straightforward to account for because they have a required monthly payment.

For example, a $500 monthly auto payment adds $500 to your qualifying monthly obligations.

If you have several installment loans, adding their monthly payments before applying for a mortgage can provide a useful preliminary estimate of your DTI.

Paying down or eliminating a monthly debt obligation can reduce DTI, although borrowers should consider the effect on their cash reserves before using savings to pay off debt.

## DTI and Your Down Payment
DTI and loan-to-value (LTV) measure different aspects of a mortgage application.

DTI compares debt obligations with income.

LTV compares the amount financed with the property's appraised value. The CFPB explains that lenders can use LTV when determining how much a borrower can borrow and what interest rate or mortgage insurance requirements may apply.

A borrower can therefore have a relatively low DTI but still have a high LTV if making a small down payment.

Mortgage underwriting considers these factors together rather than relying on DTI alone.

## How to Lower Your DTI Before Applying
If your DTI is higher than you would like, several approaches may help.

### Pay Down Revolving Debt
Reducing credit-card balances can lower required monthly payments and potentially improve your DTI.

### Pay Off a Small Monthly Obligation
If a relatively small loan has a significant monthly payment, eliminating it may reduce monthly qualifying debt.

However, check with the mortgage lender before making a large payment because the way debts are treated can depend on the loan program.

### Increase Qualifying Income
A higher qualifying gross income can reduce the percentage represented by your existing debt.

Not all income automatically qualifies, so documentation and lender rules matter.

### Avoid Taking on New Debt
Opening a new auto loan, personal loan, or substantial credit obligation before closing can change your DTI and trigger additional underwriting review.

If you're actively applying for a mortgage, discuss significant financial changes with your lender first.

## DTI Does Not Equal Affordability
One of the most important distinctions is that **qualifying for a mortgage is not the same as comfortably affording the mortgage**.

A lender's DTI calculation does not capture every expense in your household budget.

You may also need to pay for:

  - Utilities
  - Groceries
  - Transportation
  - Childcare
  - Home maintenance
  - Repairs
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Property taxes
  - Savings
  - Emergency expenses

The CFPB recommends focusing on what is affordable within your overall budget rather than simply determining the maximum amount a lender is willing to provide.

For homeowners, it is also important to account for future repair and maintenance costs when determining a comfortable mortgage payment.

## DTI Example: Comparing Two Mortgage Scenarios
Consider a borrower earning $9,000 gross per month.

### Scenario A
  - Proposed housing payment: $2,500
  - Auto loan: $500
  - Student loan: $300
  - Credit cards: $200

Total debt:

**$3,500**

DTI:

**$3,500 ÷ $9,000 × 100 = 38.9%**

### Scenario B
The borrower reduces monthly debt obligations by $500 before applying.

New total debt:

**$3,000**

DTI:

**$3,000 ÷ $9,000 × 100 = 33.3%**

This example illustrates why monthly payment obligations can matter as much as the outstanding balance when evaluating mortgage qualification.

## Final Considerations
Calculating your debt-to-income ratio is relatively simple: divide your qualifying monthly debt obligations by your gross monthly income and multiply the result by 100.

The more complicated part is determining **which income and debts the lender will actually use**. Mortgage programs and underwriting systems can apply different rules, which is why there is no universal DTI percentage that guarantees mortgage approval.

Before applying, calculate your estimated housing payment, add your other recurring debt obligations, and compare the result with your gross monthly income. Then ask potential lenders how they calculate DTI for the specific mortgage program you're considering.

Finally, avoid treating the maximum qualifying DTI as a target budget. A mortgage payment that passes underwriting can still leave too little room for repairs, insurance, taxes, savings, and everyday household expenses. A realistic affordability analysis should consider the complete cost of owning the home, not just the debt ratio used to qualify for the loan.
