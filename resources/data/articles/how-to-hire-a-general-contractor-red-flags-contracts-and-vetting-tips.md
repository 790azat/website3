Hiring a general contractor is one of the most important decisions in a major home renovation or repair project. The contractor may coordinate demolition, subcontractors, materials, permits, inspections, scheduling, and final completion, so a poor hiring decision can affect both the budget and the quality of the finished work.

A professional-looking website or attractive estimate is not enough to establish whether a contractor is appropriate for a project. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) recommends checking licensing and insurance, obtaining multiple written estimates, researching complaints and references, and reviewing a written contract before work begins.

A structured vetting process can help homeowners compare contractors based on documentation, experience, communication, pricing, and contract terms rather than simply choosing the lowest estimate.

## Start With Several Contractor Candidates
Before requesting detailed bids, create a shortlist of contractors who regularly perform the type of work required.

Sources can include:

  - Recommendations from people you trust
  - Local builders' associations
  - Previous contractors or tradespeople
  - Professional referrals
  - Local construction directories
  - Online reviews and business listings

The FTC recommends getting multiple estimates and researching contractors before signing an agreement. It also advises checking online reviews critically and searching the contractor's name alongside terms such as "complaint" or "scam."

Do not assume that a large number of positive reviews proves reliability. Look for consistency across multiple sources and pay attention to whether reviews describe projects similar to yours.

## Verify Licensing and Insurance
Licensing requirements vary by state and locality, and some jurisdictions regulate general contractors differently from specialized trades.

Check the contractor's license through the applicable state, county, or municipal authority rather than relying exclusively on a license number printed on an advertisement.

Insurance should also be verified. Ask for evidence of appropriate liability coverage and, where applicable, workers' compensation coverage.

The FTC recommends considering contractors who are licensed and insured and confirming those credentials with the relevant government authority.

A contractor who is reluctant to provide documentation should receive additional scrutiny before being considered.

## Ask for Relevant Project Experience
A contractor can have years of experience while still being poorly suited to a particular renovation.

Ask whether the company has completed projects comparable to yours. A kitchen renovation, whole-house remodel, roofing project, addition, and structural repair can involve very different coordination requirements.

Questions to ask include:

  - How many similar projects have you completed?
  - Who will supervise the work?
  - Which trades will be subcontracted?
  - Who handles permits and inspections?
  - How long is the typical project?
  - Can you provide recent references?
  - Who will be my primary contact?

For complicated projects, ask for examples that demonstrate experience with the specific structural, mechanical, electrical, or design requirements involved.

## Check References Carefully
References are more useful when the questions go beyond "Were you satisfied?"

Ask previous customers:

  - Was the project completed on schedule?
  - Did the final price differ significantly from the original estimate?
  - How were change orders handled?
  - Did subcontractors show up as scheduled?
  - Was the job site kept reasonably organized?
  - How did the contractor respond when something went wrong?
  - Were permits and inspections handled properly?
  - Would you hire the contractor for another project?

If possible, visit a recently completed project with the owner's permission. Seeing workmanship in person can reveal details that photographs do not show.

## Watch for Contractor Red Flags
Certain behaviors should make a homeowner pause before signing.

The FTC identifies several common warning signs associated with home-improvement scams, including unsolicited door-to-door offers, pressure to make an immediate decision, demands for full payment upfront, cash-only payment requests, and contractors who tell homeowners to obtain required permits themselves.

Other concerns include:

  - Refusing to provide a written estimate
  - Refusing to identify subcontractors
  - Providing vague descriptions of materials
  - Offering a price dramatically below competing bids without explanation
  - Asking you to sign incomplete documents
  - Pressuring you to sign immediately
  - Asking you to transfer an insurance check
  - Insisting on unusual payment methods
  - Refusing to provide proof of insurance
  - Changing the scope or price verbally without documentation

Following a major storm, homeowners should be especially cautious. The FTC warns that unlicensed contractors and scammers may target disaster-affected properties with promises of immediate repairs.

## Compare Estimates Line by Line
Do not compare contractor estimates solely by their total price.

A useful estimate should identify the project's scope, materials, labor, expected schedule, and other significant costs. The FTC recommends that written estimates include a description of the work, materials, completion date, and price.

For a larger project, compare items such as:

| Estimate category | What to check |
|---|---|
| Demolition | What is being removed and disposed of? |
| Materials | Brand, grade, quantity, and allowances |
| Labor | Included trades and labor scope |
| Permits | Who obtains and pays for them? |
| Subcontractors | Which trades are outsourced? |
| Site protection | Dust, debris, flooring, landscaping |
| Cleanup | Daily and final cleanup |
| Change orders | Process and markup |
| Payment schedule | Amount and project milestones |
| Warranty | Coverage and duration |

A large difference between estimates is a reason to investigate the scope, not automatically a reason to select the cheapest proposal.

## Make Sure the Contract Is Detailed
The contract should turn the contractor's proposal into a clear agreement.

At minimum, the document should identify the parties, property, scope of work, price, payment schedule, expected start and completion dates, and procedures for changes.

The FTC recommends that homeowners make sure contracts include the contractor's name, address, telephone number, and license number where applicable, estimated start and completion dates, and promises relating to the scope and cost of labor and materials.

A detailed contract should also address:

  - Specific materials and products
  - Allowances for items not yet selected
  - Permit responsibilities
  - Change-order procedures
  - Cleanup and debris removal
  - Warranty obligations
  - Subcontractor responsibilities
  - Insurance requirements
  - Dispute procedures
  - Conditions for termination
  - Final payment requirements

Never sign a contract containing blank sections that someone could complete later. The FTC specifically advises consumers to make sure blank spaces are filled before signing.

## Understand Change Orders
Even a carefully prepared renovation estimate can change when walls are opened or hidden conditions are discovered.

A change order should document what is changing, why it is changing, and how the change affects the price and schedule.

For example, if demolition reveals damaged framing or outdated electrical wiring, the contractor may need to propose additional work.

Avoid relying on verbal agreements. A written change-order process creates a record that both parties can review before additional work begins.

The contract should also specify whether contractor or subcontractor markups apply to additional materials and labor.

## Be Careful With Upfront Payments
A contractor may reasonably require a deposit to reserve labor or purchase materials, but homeowners should be cautious about demands for the entire project price before work begins.

The FTC advises consumers not to pay the full amount upfront and notes that some states limit permissible down payments. Local rules should therefore be checked before signing.

The payment schedule should correspond to meaningful project milestones.

For example, a large renovation might divide payments among contract signing, material delivery, completion of defined construction stages, and final completion. The exact structure should reflect the project and applicable law.

Do not allow payment milestones to become so far ahead of completed work that the contractor has little financial incentive to finish.

## Keep Insurance and Financing Separate
If a renovation is being funded through a loan, compare financing independently rather than automatically accepting financing arranged by the contractor.

The FTC specifically warns homeowners against agreeing to contractor-arranged financing without independently reviewing and comparing the loan terms. Homeowners should understand the interest rate, fees, repayment period, collateral requirements, and total cost before signing.

For insurance-funded repairs, the same principle applies: verify coverage directly with the insurer rather than relying on the contractor's interpretation of what the policy covers.

After weather-related damage, the FTC advises homeowners not to sign over insurance checks to contractors and to verify their coverage independently.

## Confirm Who Handles Permits
Permit responsibility should be explicitly stated in the contract.

Depending on the jurisdiction and project, permits may be required for structural changes, electrical work, plumbing, mechanical systems, additions, or other construction.

A contractor who tells the homeowner to obtain a permit that the contractor should ordinarily be handling deserves additional scrutiny. The FTC identifies this behavior as a potential warning sign in home-improvement transactions.

After completion, keep copies of permits and inspection records with the project documents. They can become useful when making future repairs or selling the property.

## Inspect the Work Before Final Payment
The final payment should not be made simply because the contractor says the project is complete.

Walk through the property and compare the finished work against the contract and approved change orders.

Check:

  - Materials and finishes
  - Doors and windows
  - Cabinets and built-ins
  - Flooring
  - Paint
  - Fixtures
  - Electrical outlets and lighting
  - Plumbing fixtures
  - Exterior work
  - Cleanup
  - Outstanding punch-list items

The FTC advises homeowners not to make the final payment until the work is completed and they are satisfied with it.

For major structural or mechanical work, appropriate inspections should also be completed before final closeout.

## Final Considerations
Hiring a general contractor is ultimately a documentation and verification process as much as a pricing decision. Check licensing and insurance, investigate relevant experience, contact references, compare written estimates, and make sure the contract clearly defines the work and payment schedule.

Red flags deserve attention before money changes hands. Pressure to sign immediately, demands for full upfront payment, cash-only requests, missing credentials, vague estimates, blank contracts, and reluctance to provide documentation are all reasons to slow the process down.

A well-structured contract cannot eliminate every construction problem, but it can establish clear expectations about scope, materials, costs, timing, changes, and responsibilities. Taking time to vet the contractor before construction begins can make the renovation easier to manage and give the homeowner a clearer record if disagreements arise.
