For landlords and property managers, Fair Housing Act compliance is not limited to the moment a rental application is approved or denied. Fair housing rules can affect advertising, applicant screening, leasing decisions, rent policies, maintenance, property access, reasonable accommodations, and interactions with tenants.

The federal Fair Housing Act generally prohibits housing discrimination based on **race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, and disability**. State and local laws may provide additional protections.

The safest operational approach is to build consistent, documented procedures so that applicants and tenants are evaluated and treated according to legitimate property-management criteria rather than protected characteristics.

## What the Fair Housing Act Covers
The Fair Housing Act applies to most housing transactions and prohibits discrimination in renting, selling, and other housing-related activities.

For landlords, that can involve decisions about:

  - Whether to advertise a property
  - Who receives information about a rental
  - Application requirements
  - Tenant screening
  - Lease approval
  - Security deposits
  - Rental terms and conditions
  - Property rules
  - Maintenance and repairs
  - Access to amenities
  - Lease renewals
  - Evictions and other housing-related actions

There are limited exemptions under federal law, and state or local laws can be broader. Landlords should therefore evaluate the specific property and jurisdiction rather than assuming an exemption applies.

## 1. Make Rental Advertising Neutral
Discrimination can begin before an applicant submits an application.

HUD's fair-housing advertising guidance identifies discriminatory preferences or limitations in housing advertisements as a potential Fair Housing Act issue.

Avoid advertising language that expresses a preference for or against people based on protected characteristics.

For example, descriptions such as:

  - "Perfect for young professionals"
  - "Ideal for Christian families"
  - "No children"
  - "Adults only"
  - "Prefer native English speakers"

can create fair-housing concerns depending on context.

Instead, describe the **property and its objective features**:

  - Two-bedroom apartment
  - Quiet residential building
  - Near public transportation
  - Maximum occupancy according to applicable law
  - No-smoking property
  - On-site parking
  - Accessible entrance

The distinction is important: advertise the characteristics of the **property**, not the characteristics of the people you want to live there.

## 2. Be Careful With Online Advertising
Digital advertising creates another layer of compliance risk.

HUD has specifically addressed the potential for housing advertisements and targeting tools to restrict who receives housing information based on protected characteristics.

Landlords and property managers should review:

  - Audience targeting
  - Geographic targeting
  - Exclusion settings
  - Retargeting audiences
  - Automated campaign optimization
  - Platform-generated audience segments

A landlord may want to reach a particular market, but targeting a housing advertisement in a way that excludes people because of protected characteristics can create legal problems.

HUD's current housing-provider FAQ also recommends that targeted outreach be part of a broader, non-selective marketing campaign rather than restricting housing advertisements to a particular protected group.

## 3. Use Written Tenant-Screening Criteria
Tenant screening should be based on objective criteria that are established before applications are reviewed.

A landlord might establish requirements involving:

  - Minimum income
  - Credit history
  - Rental history
  - Employment or income verification
  - Prior evictions
  - References
  - Criminal-record information where legally permissible
  - Maximum occupancy
  - Required documentation

The specific criteria should be appropriate for the property and consistent with applicable federal, state, and local laws.

Most importantly, **apply the same criteria consistently**.

Changing requirements from one applicant to another without a legitimate reason can create unnecessary fair-housing risk.

### Screening software does not eliminate the landlord's responsibility
Automated screening systems can make decisions faster, but using a third-party service does not automatically make a screening process compliant.

HUD has stated that tenant-screening companies and housing providers using automated or AI-based screening tools need to consider Fair Housing Act requirements, including the need for transparent and non-discriminatory screening practices.

Property managers should understand what their screening provider actually evaluates rather than treating a software-generated recommendation as unquestionable.

## 4. Understand Adverse Action Requirements
Fair housing compliance and consumer-reporting compliance can overlap.

If a landlord takes adverse action based on information in a tenant-screening report, the Fair Credit Reporting Act can require an adverse-action notice. This can apply not only to outright rejection but also to certain conditional decisions, such as requiring a larger deposit, higher rent, or a co-signer because of information in the report.

The notice generally needs to identify the consumer-reporting company and explain the applicant's rights concerning the report.

A property-management system should therefore preserve records showing:

1.  Which screening criteria were used.
2.  Which report was obtained.
3.  What decision was made.
4.  Whether the report influenced that decision.
5.  Whether an adverse-action notice was required.

## 5. Apply Rental Terms Consistently
Fair housing problems can arise even after someone becomes a tenant.

Landlords should apply policies consistently concerning:

  - Rent payment requirements
  - Late fees
  - Parking
  - Amenities
  - Guest policies
  - Pet policies
  - Lease violations
  - Property access
  - Maintenance requests
  - Renewal decisions

For example, giving one tenant additional privileges because of a personal relationship while refusing comparable treatment to another tenant can create problems if the difference is connected to a protected characteristic.

The goal is not necessarily to make every tenant's situation identical. Legitimate differences can exist. The key is that differences in treatment should have a lawful, documented reason unrelated to protected status.

## 6. Handle Disability Accommodations Correctly
Disability-related requests require particular care.

Under the Fair Housing Act, housing providers may be required to make **reasonable accommodations** to rules, policies, practices, or services when necessary to give a person with a disability an equal opportunity to use and enjoy their home.

Depending on the circumstances, examples can include changes to:

  - No-pet policies for assistance animals
  - Parking arrangements
  - Communication procedures
  - Payment procedures
  - Building rules

Reasonable modifications are different. A modification generally involves a physical change to a dwelling or common area that may be necessary because of a disability. HUD explains that the Fair Housing Act contains specific requirements concerning these modifications.

Because accommodation and modification rules can depend on the property, request, and applicable law, landlords should have a defined process for receiving and evaluating these requests rather than handling them informally.

## 7. Treat Maintenance as a Compliance Issue
Maintenance is not simply an operational function.

HUD housing guidance identifies delaying maintenance or failing to provide services associated with a rental unit as conduct that can raise discrimination concerns when connected to protected characteristics.

A property manager should therefore track maintenance requests using a centralized system.

Useful records include:

  - Date and time of request
  - Nature of the problem
  - Priority level
  - Assigned contractor
  - Appointment date
  - Completion date
  - Tenant communications
  - Photos or supporting documentation

A standardized maintenance system helps demonstrate that emergencies and ordinary repairs are handled according to objective criteria rather than the identity of the tenant.

## 8. Train Employees and Contractors
Fair housing compliance extends beyond the property owner.

Leasing agents, property managers, maintenance personnel, contractors, and other representatives may interact directly with applicants and tenants.

Training should cover:

  - Protected classes
  - Advertising rules
  - Screening procedures
  - Consistent application of policies
  - Accommodation requests
  - Appropriate tenant communications
  - Documentation
  - Escalation procedures

A written policy is much more useful when employees know how to apply it in real situations.

## 9. Keep an Audit Trail
Good documentation can help a landlord demonstrate that decisions were based on legitimate operational criteria.

Maintain records of:

  - Rental advertisements
  - Screening criteria
  - Applications
  - Screening reports
  - Approval and denial decisions
  - Adverse-action notices
  - Lease terms
  - Maintenance requests
  - Accommodation requests
  - Relevant tenant communications
  - Policy changes

Records should be retained according to applicable federal, state, and local requirements.

Avoid informal comments in emails, text messages, or internal notes that could be interpreted as expressing preferences about protected groups. Communications should remain focused on legitimate property-management considerations.

## A Practical Fair-Housing Compliance Checklist
Before advertising a property:

**☐** Review the advertisement for discriminatory language
**☐** Confirm digital targeting does not improperly exclude protected groups
**☐** Establish written screening criteria
**☐** Verify criteria comply with applicable law
**☐** Train leasing personnel

During applications:

**☐** Apply the same criteria consistently
**☐** Document legitimate reasons for exceptions
**☐** Follow consumer-reporting requirements
**☐** Provide required adverse-action notices

During tenancy:

**☐** Apply lease policies consistently
**☐** Track maintenance requests
**☐** Establish a process for accommodation requests
**☐** Document significant decisions
**☐** Train employees and contractors

### Final Considerations
Fair Housing Act compliance is best treated as an operating system rather than a one-time legal checklist. The strongest process combines neutral advertising, predetermined screening standards, consistent lease administration, documented maintenance procedures, appropriate accommodation handling, and careful recordkeeping.

Landlords should also remember that the federal Fair Housing Act is only one layer of housing law. State and local governments can impose additional protected classes, rental requirements, notice rules, and tenant protections.

When a situation involves a potential discrimination complaint, unusual accommodation request, disputed screening decision, or state-specific housing rule, obtaining advice from a qualified housing attorney or compliance professional can help prevent an operational mistake from becoming a legal dispute.
