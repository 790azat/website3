Cracks in a concrete driveway or sidewalk are not always a sign that the entire surface needs to be replaced. Small, stable cracks can often be sealed to keep water out and slow further deterioration. Larger cracks, uneven slabs, extensive surface deterioration, or movement caused by poor drainage or tree roots may require a more substantial repair.

The key is identifying **why the concrete cracked before choosing a repair material**. The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) notes that crack sealing can prevent water from entering a crack, but it does not restore the structural connection between two separated pieces of concrete. Where cracking creates a trip hazard or reflects serious underlying damage, replacement may be the more appropriate solution.

## Why Concrete Driveways and Sidewalks Crack
Concrete naturally expands and contracts as temperatures change. Other factors can include:

  - Poorly compacted or unstable soil
  - Water accumulating beneath the slab
  - Freeze-thaw cycles
  - Tree-root growth
  - Heavy vehicle loads
  - Improperly placed control joints
  - Insufficient drainage
  - Aging and surface deterioration
  - Construction or curing problems

Freeze-thaw conditions can be particularly damaging. Water that enters a crack can saturate the material underneath the slab and freeze, potentially causing the concrete to lift and the crack to widen.

This is why simply filling a recurring crack without addressing drainage or movement may provide only a temporary improvement.

## How to Tell What Kind of Crack You Have
Before buying a repair product, inspect the crack carefully.

### Hairline or Narrow Surface Cracks
Very small cracks that are not widening, displacing the slab, or allowing significant water penetration are often candidates for sealing.

These are primarily a maintenance issue rather than evidence that the entire slab has failed.

### Wider Cracks
Wider cracks require more preparation and a suitable flexible or cementitious repair product.

For example, manufacturer guidance for concrete crack repair products commonly distinguishes between narrow cracks and larger openings requiring different materials or backer rod.

### Cracks With Vertical Displacement
If one side of a crack is noticeably higher than the other, the problem is more than a surface opening.

Vertical movement can result from settlement, tree roots, erosion, or problems with the underlying base.

Filling the gap will not correct the difference in elevation.

### Multiple Interconnected Cracks
A network of cracks resembling a web or "alligator cracking" can indicate broader deterioration. FHWA notes that this type of cracking can be associated with poor sub-base drainage or serious structural damage and may not be a good candidate for simple crack sealing.

## When Can You Repair the Crack Yourself?
DIY repair is generally most appropriate when:

  - The slab remains level.
  - The crack is relatively small.
  - The surrounding concrete is solid.
  - There is no significant crumbling.
  - The crack is not rapidly expanding.
  - There is no obvious drainage or soil problem.
  - The surface does not create a serious trip hazard.

A homeowner can often handle a small, stable crack using a product specifically labeled for exterior concrete.

However, follow the manufacturer's instructions for crack width, depth, temperature, curing, and surface preparation.

## How to Repair a Concrete Crack
### 1. Clean the Crack
Remove dirt, vegetation, loose concrete, dust, and other debris.

A repair material needs a clean, sound surface to bond properly. QUIKRETE recommends removing deteriorated material and brushing the crack clean before applying its concrete repair products.

For some sealants, the crack also needs to be completely dry.

### 2. Open the Crack if Required
Some repair systems require the crack to be widened before filling.

For example, QUIKRETE's concrete crack-repair instructions recommend widening the crack to approximately ¼ inch and creating suitable edges before applying its repair material.

Do not automatically widen every crack. The correct preparation depends on the repair material being used.

### 3. Remove Unsound Concrete
If the crack has crumbling or flaking edges, remove the loose material.

Leaving deteriorated concrete underneath a repair compound gives the new material a weak surface to bond to.

### 4. Install Backer Rod for Deep Cracks When Required
Some deep cracks require a backer rod before sealant is applied.

The backer rod limits the depth of sealant and helps the material perform properly rather than filling a deep void with expensive sealant.

QUIKRETE recommends backer rod for certain cracks deeper than approximately ½ inch when using its polyurethane sealant system.

### 5. Apply the Appropriate Repair Material
Depending on the crack, products can include:

  - Flexible concrete crack sealants
  - Polyurethane sealants
  - Acrylic-latex concrete repair products
  - Cement-based repair materials
  - Polymer-modified repair compounds

For example, QUIKRETE lists acrylic-latex concrete repair products for cracks up to ½ inch wide and separate products for deeper or larger repairs.

Choose based on the manufacturer's specified application rather than simply buying the strongest-looking product.

### 6. Tool and Finish the Repair
Remove excess material and finish the surface according to the product instructions.

The goal is to leave the repair approximately flush with the surrounding concrete while maintaining the seal.

## What About Spalling and Crumbling Concrete?
A crack is different from **spalling**, where pieces of the concrete surface break away.

Small areas of shallow surface deterioration may be repairable with a suitable patching compound or resurfacing product.

For example, QUIKRETE identifies its concrete patching compound for non-structural repairs involving surface imperfections and cracks up to ¼ inch deep.

More extensive deterioration may require partial-depth or full-depth repair.

FHWA notes that successful concrete repair requires removal of deteriorated material and appropriate preparation of the repair boundaries.

If large sections are crumbling, exposing aggregate, or losing structural integrity, patching over the surface may only conceal the problem temporarily.

## When Should You Replace the Concrete?
Replacement becomes more appropriate when the slab has significant structural or movement-related problems.

Consider professional evaluation when you see:

  - Large sections of interconnected cracking
  - Major vertical displacement
  - Severe settlement
  - Large holes or missing concrete
  - Extensive spalling
  - Exposed reinforcement
  - Repeated cracking after previous repairs
  - Water consistently collecting beneath or around the slab
  - Tree roots lifting the concrete
  - A significant trip hazard

FHWA guidance specifically notes that when sidewalk cracks create or are likely to create a tripping hazard, replacement can be a better and necessary option rather than repeatedly sealing the crack.

## Fix the Cause Before Replacing the Slab
One of the most important steps is determining why the concrete failed.

### Poor Drainage
Water flowing beneath a driveway or sidewalk can erode or destabilize the supporting material.

Correcting drainage may involve:

  - Redirecting downspouts
  - Improving grading
  - Installing drainage channels
  - Repairing leaking water lines
  - Improving runoff paths

If the underlying water problem remains, new concrete may eventually crack again.

### Tree Roots
Tree roots can lift sidewalk panels and create substantial vertical displacement. FHWA identifies tree-root damage as a common cause of buckled and cracked pedestrian surfaces.

Simply filling the crack does not solve the problem.

Root management, slab replacement, or an alternative design may be necessary depending on the location and tree.

### Soil Settlement
If the underlying soil settles, the concrete slab can lose support and crack.

In some situations, lifting or leveling the existing slab may be possible instead of completely replacing it. The appropriate technique depends on the severity of settlement and the condition of the slab.

## Repair, Resurface, or Replace?
| Condition | Typical approach |
|---|---|
| Small stable crack | Clean and seal |
| Narrow crack with minor deterioration | Flexible repair compound |
| Deep crack | Appropriate sealant and possible backer rod |
| Shallow surface spalling | Patch or resurface |
| Uneven slab | Investigate settlement or root movement |
| Extensive interconnected cracks | Professional evaluation |
| Severe displacement | Replacement or structural correction |
| Major underlying drainage problem | Correct drainage first |
| Extensive slab deterioration | Full or partial replacement |

The important distinction is that **crack filler repairs the opening, not necessarily the cause of the cracking**.

## How to Prevent New Concrete Cracks
After repairing the existing damage, reduce the conditions that caused it.

Keep water away from the slab by maintaining gutters and downspouts and correcting obvious drainage problems.

Avoid allowing heavy vehicles to regularly cross concrete that was not designed for those loads.

Keep expansion and control joints in good condition. Proper joints help manage where concrete cracks as it moves.

In climates with freezing temperatures, avoid practices that allow water to remain trapped beneath or around the slab. Freeze-thaw cycles can accelerate cracking and surface deterioration.

Also inspect repaired areas periodically. A crack that begins reopening shortly after repair may be signaling movement underneath the slab rather than a failure of the repair product itself.

## Special Considerations for Sidewalks
Sidewalk repairs involve an additional concern: **pedestrian safety**.

Even a relatively narrow crack can become hazardous if one section of concrete rises above the other.

FHWA identifies vertical displacement of approximately ½ inch or more as a potential trip and accessibility problem in sidewalk environments.

Local rules can also determine who is responsible for sidewalk repairs. In some jurisdictions, the adjacent property owner may be responsible for maintaining or replacing portions of the sidewalk, while other municipalities handle repairs differently.

Before replacing a sidewalk that borders a public right-of-way, check local requirements for permits, materials, dimensions, and responsibility.

## Final Considerations
Repairing a cracked driveway or sidewalk starts with identifying **the type of damage and its underlying cause**.

Small, stable cracks can often be cleaned and sealed to limit water penetration. Proper preparation is critical: remove loose material, follow the repair product's requirements, and use a compatible sealant or patching material.

Larger cracks, displaced slabs, interconnected cracking, extensive spalling, or recurring failures require a different approach. FHWA emphasizes that crack sealing is primarily a preventive measure and does not restore the structural connection between separated concrete sections.

If water, roots, settlement, or unstable soil caused the original damage, fix that problem before investing in a new slab. Otherwise, the same forces can damage the replacement.

For homeowners, the most cost-effective repair is not necessarily the cheapest patch. A properly diagnosed problem—and the appropriate decision between **sealing, patching, leveling, partial replacement, and full replacement**—is more likely to produce a durable result.
