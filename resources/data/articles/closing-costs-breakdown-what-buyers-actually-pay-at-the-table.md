Buying a home requires more cash than the down payment alone. At closing, buyers may pay lender charges, title services, government fees, prepaid expenses, escrow deposits, and other transaction costs. The exact amount depends on the mortgage, property, location, lender, and purchase agreement.

One important distinction is that **closing costs are not the same as cash to close**. Closing costs generally represent the upfront expenses associated with obtaining the mortgage and completing the real estate transaction, while cash to close is the actual amount the buyer must bring to the closing. Cash to close can include the down payment, closing costs, deposits already paid, seller credits, and other adjustments.

Understanding the difference can make it easier to estimate how much money you will need before signing the final documents.

## What Are Closing Costs?
Closing costs are the fees and prepaid expenses associated with purchasing a home and obtaining a mortgage. They can include costs charged by the lender, third-party service providers, government agencies, and insurance or tax authorities.

Common categories include:

  - Mortgage origination charges
  - Discount points
  - Appraisal fees
  - Credit report fees
  - Title search and title insurance
  - Recording and government fees
  - Prepaid interest
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Initial escrow deposits
  - Property tax adjustments
  - Other settlement services

The CFPB notes that closing costs are separate from the down payment when calculating total closing costs on the Closing Disclosure.

However, all of these amounts ultimately affect the cash you need to complete the purchase.

## Lender and Mortgage Fees
Some of the first costs buyers encounter come directly from the mortgage lender.

### Origination charges
Origination charges compensate the lender for processing and making the mortgage. Depending on the lender, they may include underwriting, processing, application, administrative, or origination fees.

These charges can vary between lenders, which makes them particularly important when comparing mortgage offers. The CFPB recommends paying attention to lender-controlled costs rather than simply comparing the estimated property taxes or homeowners insurance shown on different Loan Estimates.

### Discount points
Mortgage points are upfront charges paid to the lender in exchange for a lower interest rate. One point generally equals 1% of the loan amount, although the rate reduction associated with a point can vary.

For example, on a $300,000 mortgage, one point would equal $3,000. Whether paying points makes financial sense depends on the available rate reduction, how long you expect to keep the mortgage, and how much cash you have available at closing.

## Appraisal and Credit-Related Costs
Mortgage lenders commonly require an appraisal to establish an opinion of the property's market value. The appraisal helps the lender evaluate whether the property provides sufficient collateral for the loan.

A credit report fee may also appear among the loan-related charges.

These are generally third-party costs rather than pure lender revenue, and their amounts can vary depending on the transaction and provider.

Buyers should review these charges on the Loan Estimate and compare them with the final figures on the Closing Disclosure.

## Title Services and Title Insurance
Title-related expenses can represent a significant portion of closing costs.

A title search examines public records for potential issues involving ownership, liens, unpaid taxes, or other claims against the property. Lenders generally require a lender's title insurance policy to protect their financial interest in the property.

Buyers may also choose to purchase an owner's title insurance policy. Unlike lender's title insurance, an owner's policy is designed to protect the homeowner's financial interest against certain covered title claims arising from before the purchase.

Title services are often among the closing services buyers can shop for. The CFPB recommends comparing providers and looking at the total cost rather than assuming that a lender-recommended provider offers the lowest price.

## Government and Recording Fees
Government agencies may charge fees for recording the deed, recording the mortgage or deed of trust, and transferring ownership.

Some jurisdictions also impose transfer taxes, recording taxes, or other transaction-related charges.

These expenses generally aren't controlled by the mortgage lender and may vary significantly by location. State and local rules can also determine whether the buyer or seller pays particular charges.

This is one reason a closing-cost estimate from a buyer in one state may look very different from one in another state.

## Prepaid Expenses
Not every amount paid at closing is technically a fee for obtaining the mortgage. Some amounts are prepaid expenses that cover future homeownership costs.

Common examples include:

  - Interest from the closing date through the end of the month
  - The first year's homeowners insurance premium
  - Property taxes
  - Other insurance-related payments

These amounts can make the cash required at closing substantially higher even though they are not all lender charges.

For example, if you close late in the month, prepaid interest may be relatively small. A closing earlier in the month can result in more days of prepaid interest.

## Initial Escrow Deposits
If your mortgage includes an escrow account, you may need to deposit money into that account at closing.

The lender can use the escrow account to collect money for future property tax and homeowners insurance payments. The initial deposit establishes a balance that allows those bills to be paid when they become due.

The amount required depends on factors such as the property's tax schedule, insurance premium, closing date, and applicable escrow rules.

This is another reason two buyers purchasing similarly priced homes can have very different amounts of cash required at closing.

## Buyer Credits and Seller Credits
The amount you personally bring to closing can be reduced by credits.

A seller may agree to contribute toward eligible closing costs as part of the purchase contract. The lender may also provide lender credits that offset certain closing expenses.

However, these credits are not necessarily free money.

A lender credit typically comes in exchange for a higher interest rate, while a seller credit may be reflected in the negotiated purchase price or other contract terms.

When comparing offers, buyers should consider both the upfront savings and the long-term mortgage cost.

## Closing Costs vs. Cash to Close
This distinction is one of the most important parts of the Closing Disclosure.

**Closing costs** are the total upfront costs associated with the mortgage and real estate transaction, excluding the down payment.

**Cash to close** is the actual amount the buyer must provide at closing after accounting for the down payment, closing costs, deposits already paid, seller credits, lender credits, and other adjustments.

A simplified example might look like this:

| Item | Example Amount |
|---|---|
| Purchase price | $400,000 |
| Down payment | $80,000 |
| Closing costs | $10,000 |
| Earnest money already paid | \\-$10,000 |
| Seller credit | \\-$5,000 |
| **Approximate cash to close** | **$75,000** |

The actual calculation can include additional adjustments, so buyers should rely on their final Closing Disclosure rather than a simple estimate.

## The Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure
The mortgage process gives buyers two important documents for tracking costs.

The **Loan Estimate** is provided after applying for a mortgage. The lender generally must provide it within three business days of receiving the application. It provides estimated loan terms, monthly payments, and closing costs.

The **Closing Disclosure** contains the final loan terms and costs. Federal rules generally require the lender to provide it at least three business days before closing.

Use that period to compare the Closing Disclosure with your earlier Loan Estimate.

Pay particular attention to:

  - Loan amount
  - Interest rate
  - Origination charges
  - Points
  - Title services
  - Government fees
  - Prepaid expenses
  - Escrow deposits
  - Seller credits
  - Lender credits
  - Cash to close

If an amount changed significantly, ask the lender or settlement agent for an explanation before signing.

## How Buyers Can Manage Closing Costs
Closing costs cannot always be eliminated, but buyers can prepare for them and potentially reduce certain expenses.

Start by comparing mortgage offers based on the total cost of the loan rather than the advertised interest rate alone. A lower rate accompanied by substantial points may require more cash upfront.

Next, identify services you are permitted to shop for. Title services and certain other closing services may be independently selected, depending on the transaction and local rules.

You can also ask whether seller credits or lender credits are available and what tradeoffs they involve.

Finally, keep a cash reserve beyond the amount shown in your initial estimate. Property taxes, insurance, prepaid interest, and other transaction adjustments can change the final amount.

## What to Bring to the Closing Table
Your settlement agent or lender will tell you exactly how the funds must be delivered. Depending on the transaction, you may need a wire transfer or cashier's check for the required amount.

Before sending money, verify wiring instructions through a trusted, independently confirmed contact. Mortgage and real estate transactions can be targets for wire fraud, so buyers should be cautious about last-minute payment instructions.

Most importantly, review the final Closing Disclosure before closing. The number that ultimately matters for your immediate cash requirement is **Cash to Close**, not simply the line labeled Total Closing Costs.

## Final Considerations
Closing costs can include far more than a lender's origination fee. Appraisals, title services, government charges, prepaid interest, homeowners insurance, property taxes, escrow deposits, and other transaction expenses can all affect the amount of cash required to purchase a home.

The most useful way to budget is to separate three numbers: **your down payment, your closing costs, and your final cash to close**.

Review your Loan Estimate early, compare providers where permitted, and carefully check the Closing Disclosure at least three business days before the scheduled closing. Understanding each category can help you avoid surprises when it is time to transfer the funds and sign the final documents.
