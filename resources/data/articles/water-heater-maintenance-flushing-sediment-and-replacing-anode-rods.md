A traditional tank water heater can work reliably for years, but it needs more than an occasional temperature adjustment. Two of the most important preventive-maintenance tasks are **flushing sediment from the tank** and **inspecting or replacing the anode rod**.

Sediment can accumulate at the bottom of a storage tank, reducing heating efficiency and contributing to rumbling or popping noises. Meanwhile, the anode rod protects the steel tank from corrosion by sacrificing itself over time. Manufacturers such as Rheem recommend regular tank flushing and periodic anode-rod inspection as part of routine maintenance.

Understanding these two components can help homeowners prevent avoidable repairs and recognize when professional service is appropriate.

## Why Water Heater Sediment Builds Up
Sediment consists primarily of minerals and other material carried by the incoming water supply. Hard-water conditions can accelerate mineral accumulation, particularly calcium and magnesium deposits.

As sediment settles on the bottom of a tank, it can interfere with efficient heat transfer. On gas models, water trapped beneath the sediment can produce steam bubbles that create popping or rumbling sounds. On electric models, deposits can also affect heating elements and overall performance.

Common warning signs include:

  - Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds
  - Longer heating or recovery times
  - Less available hot water
  - Cloudy or discolored hot water
  - Increasing energy consumption without a major change in usage
  - Visible mineral deposits around plumbing connections

Rheem currently recommends annual flushing for many tank water heaters, with more frequent maintenance potentially appropriate in hard-water conditions.

## How Often Should You Flush a Water Heater?
For a typical tank-style water heater, **once a year is a reasonable maintenance interval**, provided the manufacturer's instructions do not specify otherwise.

Homes with particularly hard water may benefit from flushing approximately every six months. Water quality, household size, water consumption, and the amount of sediment already present can all affect the appropriate schedule.

A water heater that has never been maintained should not automatically be subjected to aggressive DIY flushing. Older tanks can have substantial mineral buildup, and attempting to operate a stuck or deteriorated drain valve can create leaks or other problems.

Always check the owner's manual before beginning.

## How to Flush a Tank Water Heater
The basic process is straightforward, but hot water and electrical or gas equipment create genuine safety hazards.

### 1. Shut down the water heater
Turn off the energy source according to the manufacturer's instructions. For an electric heater, this generally means switching off the appropriate circuit breaker. Gas models require the gas control to be placed in the appropriate off or pilot position according to the manual.

Also close the cold-water supply entering the tank.

### 2. Allow the water to cool
Do not immediately drain extremely hot water into a location where it could cause burns or damage.

### 3. Connect a garden hose
Attach a suitable hose to the tank's drain valve. Direct the other end to a safe drainage location or appropriate container.

### 4. Open the drain
Open the drain valve and allow water to leave the tank. Opening a hot-water faucet elsewhere in the home can help introduce air into the system.

### 5. Flush until the water is clear
Once the tank has drained, the incoming cold-water supply can be used to help wash remaining sediment from the tank, following the manufacturer's procedure.

Rheem's published maintenance guidance specifically recommends draining and flushing tank-style units to remove accumulated sediment.

### 6. Refill before restoring power
This step is particularly important for electric water heaters. The tank should be completely filled before the heating elements are energized. Operating an electric element without adequate water can damage it.

Follow the manufacturer's restart procedure rather than relying on a generic sequence.

## What Is an Anode Rod?
The **anode rod is a sacrificial component designed to protect the inside of a steel water-heater tank from corrosion**.

Instead of allowing the tank itself to corrode first, the anode rod provides material that is preferentially consumed. Depending on the model and water chemistry, anode rods may use materials such as magnesium or aluminum.

Once the rod is substantially depleted, its ability to protect the tank is reduced. Rheem describes anode-rod inspection as part of routine tank-water-heater maintenance.

This makes the anode rod one of the relatively inexpensive components that can play an important role in protecting a much more expensive tank.

## When Should an Anode Rod Be Replaced?
There is no single replacement interval that applies to every water heater.

Water chemistry, temperature, usage, tank design, and the type of anode all affect how quickly the rod deteriorates. Rheem's current guidance recommends inspecting the anode periodically and replacing it when it is heavily consumed or coated with mineral deposits.

During an inspection, a technician may look for:

  - Significant thinning of the rod
  - Large sections where the core material is exposed
  - Heavy corrosion
  - Calcium or mineral accumulation
  - A rod that has been substantially consumed

If the anode is nearly depleted, replacing it can restore corrosion protection. Waiting until the tank itself begins leaking is a very different—and usually much more expensive—problem.

## Can You Replace an Anode Rod Yourself?
Technically, some homeowners can replace an anode rod themselves, but it is considerably more involved than simply checking the tank's exterior.

The rod is normally installed through an opening at the top of the tank and may be difficult to remove because of corrosion, mineral deposits, limited clearance, or a tightly installed fitting.

The job can require:

  - A correctly sized replacement anode
  - A large socket or specialized tool
  - Significant torque to loosen the existing rod
  - Adequate overhead clearance
  - Careful resealing during installation

Because water heaters combine water, electricity or gas, pressure, and high temperatures, professional service is appropriate if you are uncertain about the procedure. Rheem specifically notes that anode-rod inspection and replacement may require tools and safety precautions better handled by a qualified plumber.

## Should You Flush or Replace the Water Heater?
Maintenance is most valuable before the tank begins leaking.

A water heater that is relatively young, heating normally, and showing no signs of tank corrosion may be a good candidate for continued preventive maintenance.

Replacement becomes more relevant when you see:

| Warning sign | What it may indicate |
|---|---|
| Water leaking from the tank body | Internal tank failure |
| Severe external corrosion | Advanced deterioration |
| Repeated component failures | Aging system |
| Persistent sediment-related problems | Heavy accumulation or declining condition |
| Inadequate hot-water production | Heating or capacity problem |
| Unit approaching the end of its expected service life | Higher replacement risk |

A leak originating from the tank itself generally cannot be solved by flushing or replacing the anode rod. Maintenance can slow deterioration, but it cannot repair a failed tank.

## A Simple Annual Water Heater Maintenance Checklist
Homeowners can use the following checklist as a starting point, while following the specific manufacturer's manual:

**Every year:**

  - Inspect the tank and surrounding plumbing for leaks.
  - Flush the tank according to manufacturer instructions.
  - Check for unusual popping or rumbling.
  - Inspect the anode rod at the recommended interval.
  - Check the temperature-and-pressure relief system.
  - Inspect connections for corrosion.
  - Check gas-water-heater venting where applicable.
  - Review the owner's manual for model-specific maintenance requirements.

Rheem's published tank-water-heater checklist includes draining/flushing, anode inspection, T\&P valve inspection, heating-component checks, and venting inspection as applicable.

## Final Considerations
Flushing sediment and maintaining the anode rod address two different threats to a tank water heater: **mineral buildup and internal corrosion**.

For many households, annual flushing is a practical starting point, while homes with hard water may require more frequent attention. The anode rod should also be inspected periodically rather than replaced blindly on a fixed schedule.

The most important rule is to follow the specific manufacturer's instructions. If the water heater is old, heavily corroded, leaking, or has a drain valve that will not operate properly, professional inspection is safer than forcing a maintenance procedure that could turn a manageable issue into a water-damage event.
