A dripping faucet, slow drain, weak water pressure, or running toilet does not always require an emergency plumbing call. Some common plumbing problems have simple causes that homeowners can identify and, in certain cases, correct themselves.

The important distinction is knowing **what is safe to troubleshoot and where DIY troubleshooting should stop**.

A few minutes of diagnosis can help homeowners describe the problem accurately to a plumber, prevent unnecessary service calls, and sometimes resolve a minor issue before it causes water damage. But leaks involving pressurized supply lines, sewage, gas appliances, or concealed plumbing should generally be handled by a qualified professional.

## Start by Identifying the Type of Plumbing Problem
Before touching a fixture, determine what is actually happening.

Ask:

  - Is water leaking continuously or only when a fixture operates?
  - Is the problem affecting one fixture or the entire property?
  - Is water pressure low everywhere or only at one faucet?
  - Is the drain slow or completely blocked?
  - Is the water clear, cloudy, discolored, or contaminated?
  - Is there a sewage odor?
  - Did the problem begin suddenly or develop gradually?

This first distinction can eliminate unnecessary troubleshooting.

For example, if every faucet suddenly has very low pressure, replacing an aerator on one sink is unlikely to solve the problem.

## 1. Troubleshooting a Dripping Faucet
A faucet that continues dripping after being turned off usually has a worn or damaged internal component.

The exact component depends on the faucet design and may include:

  - Cartridge
  - Washer
  - O-ring
  - Valve seat
  - Sealing component

Before replacing anything, confirm that the leak is actually coming from the faucet.

Dry the fixture and surrounding area completely, then turn the faucet on and off. Observe where the water first appears.

If the leak is coming from a supply connection underneath the sink rather than the faucet itself, the repair is different.

### What You Can Check
Turn off the fixture's water supply if necessary and inspect:

  - Supply-line connections
  - Faucet mounting area
  - Shutoff valves
  - Visible tubing
  - Cabinet floor for accumulated water

If the faucet needs to be disassembled, follow the manufacturer's instructions for the specific model.

If you cannot identify the faucet type or the connection is damaged, a plumber may be able to diagnose it more efficiently than replacing parts randomly.

## 2. Fixing a Running Toilet
A toilet that continues running can waste water and usually indicates a problem inside the tank.

Remove the tank lid and observe the mechanism while the toilet is operating.

Common causes include:

  - Flapper not sealing
  - Incorrect water level
  - Faulty fill valve
  - Chain or handle problem
  - Mineral buildup
  - Worn internal components

If the flapper does not seal correctly, water can continuously leak from the tank into the bowl.

Check whether the chain is excessively tight or tangled and whether the flapper is sitting correctly.

If water continues flowing even after basic adjustments, the fill valve or another internal component may need replacement.

A homeowner can often replace standard toilet components with basic tools, but a plumber should be considered if the tank, bowl, supply connection, or shutoff valve is leaking.

## 3. Clearing a Slow Bathroom Sink
A slow sink drain is often caused by hair, soap residue, toothpaste, and other material accumulating near the stopper or drain opening.

Start with the simplest inspection.

Remove and clean the stopper if the design allows it. Then inspect the visible portion of the drain for debris.

If the blockage remains, a plunger may help.

When using a plunger:

1.  Add enough water to cover the plunger cup.
2.  Seal the overflow opening if applicable.
3.  Position the plunger firmly over the drain.
4.  Pump several times.
5.  Remove it and check whether drainage improves.

Avoid repeatedly pouring chemical drain cleaners into a plumbing system simply because the first attempt did not work.

Chemical drain products can create safety hazards and may complicate subsequent plumbing work.

If several fixtures begin draining slowly at the same time, the blockage may be farther downstream and should be evaluated by a professional.

## 4. Dealing With a Clogged Toilet
A toilet clog is usually one of the first plumbing problems homeowners can attempt to address themselves.

Use a **toilet plunger**, rather than assuming a sink plunger is appropriate.

Make sure the plunger forms a good seal over the drain opening and use controlled plunging rather than excessive force.

If the toilet repeatedly backs up, the obstruction may be farther into the drain line.

Do not continue flushing a toilet that is already close to overflowing.

If water rises toward the rim, close the toilet's shutoff valve or lift the tank lid and stop the fill mechanism.

A recurring blockage, sewage backup, or toilet that clogs despite normal use warrants professional diagnosis.

## 5. Investigating Low Water Pressure
Low water pressure can have several causes.

Start by determining whether the issue affects:

**One fixture:** The aerator, faucet, cartridge, or local supply connection may be responsible.

**One area of the property:** A localized plumbing restriction may exist.

**The entire property:** The issue could involve the main supply, pressure-regulating equipment, shutoff valve, municipal supply, or a larger plumbing problem.

For a faucet with reduced flow, remove the aerator if possible and inspect it for mineral deposits or debris.

Clean it and reinstall it.

If water pressure suddenly drops throughout the house, check whether the main shutoff valve is fully open.

Do not attempt to adjust pressure-regulating equipment unless you understand the system and the manufacturer or a qualified professional confirms the appropriate procedure.

## 6. Checking a Slow or Gurgling Drain
A drain that gurgles may indicate that air is moving through a partially obstructed drainage system.

Pay attention to whether other fixtures are affected.

For example:

  - Sink drains slowly
  - Toilet bubbles when the sink drains
  - Shower drains slowly
  - Multiple fixtures produce unusual sounds

When several fixtures show symptoms at once, the problem may be in a shared drain line rather than an individual fixture.

That is generally a better reason to call a plumber than repeatedly treating one drain at a time.

## 7. Finding a Small Water Leak
A leak does not always appear directly beneath its source.

Check:

  - Under sinks
  - Around toilets
  - Behind appliances
  - Near water heaters
  - Around exposed pipes
  - Basement or crawl-space plumbing
  - Ceiling stains
  - Cabinet interiors

Dry the suspected area and place a paper towel beneath the connection. Recheck it after the fixture has been used.

This can help identify whether water is actively entering from a particular connection.

Do not ignore small recurring leaks.

Water can damage cabinetry, flooring, drywall, insulation, and structural components over time.

## 8. Check the Water Heater Safely
If hot water disappears, first determine whether the problem affects the entire property or only one fixture.

For an electric water heater, check whether the appropriate electrical breaker has tripped.

For a gas water heater, observe the manufacturer's safety instructions and do not attempt repairs involving gas connections.

If you smell gas, leave the area and contact the appropriate emergency service or utility provider rather than attempting to diagnose the appliance yourself.

Other signs that require professional attention include:

  - Water leaking from the tank
  - Significant corrosion
  - Repeated breaker trips
  - Unusual burning smells
  - Gas odors
  - Persistent lack of hot water
  - Loud popping or unusual operating sounds

## 9. Check the Main Water Shutoff
Every homeowner should know where the property's main water shutoff is located.

It may be near:

  - The water meter
  - A basement
  - A mechanical room
  - A garage
  - An exterior service area
  - Where the main water line enters the building

Knowing its location before an emergency occurs can prevent substantial water damage.

If a supply pipe suddenly breaks, shutting off the main water supply may be much more important than trying to identify the failed component immediately.

Do not force a seized shutoff valve. A damaged valve can create a larger leak.

## 10. When You Should Call a Plumber Immediately
DIY troubleshooting should stop when the situation presents a meaningful risk of injury, contamination, structural damage, or uncontrolled water flow.

Call a qualified plumber when you encounter:

  - A burst or uncontrolled supply pipe
  - A major leak inside a wall or ceiling
  - Sewage backup
  - Persistent loss of water pressure
  - Multiple blocked drains
  - Repeated toilet backups
  - A leaking water heater
  - Major corrosion
  - A failed main shutoff
  - Water near electrical equipment
  - Plumbing damage that requires opening walls or floors

If water is actively damaging the property, shut off the water supply if it can be done safely and contact an appropriate professional.

## Don't Confuse Plumbing With a Gas Problem
Some homes use gas-fired water heaters or other gas appliances.

A suspected gas leak is not a normal plumbing DIY problem.

If you smell gas or suspect a gas leak:

  - Do not operate electrical switches unnecessarily.
  - Do not create flames or sparks.
  - Leave the area if appropriate.
  - Contact the gas utility or emergency service from a safe location.
  - Follow the instructions of the relevant emergency professionals.

Gas-related emergencies should not be treated as ordinary plumbing troubleshooting.

## Keep a Basic Plumbing Toolkit
A homeowner does not need a professional plumber's entire toolbox.

Useful items include:

  - Adjustable wrench
  - Slip-joint pliers
  - Screwdrivers
  - Flashlight
  - Bucket
  - Towels
  - Toilet plunger
  - Small drain tool
  - Replacement faucet aerators
  - Basic plumbing tape where appropriate

The goal is to perform simple inspections and minor maintenance—not to improvise repairs on complex plumbing systems.

## Take Photos Before Calling a Plumber
If a professional is needed, photographs can make the initial diagnosis easier.

Take clear photos of:

  - The entire fixture
  - The suspected leak
  - Connections
  - Valves
  - Pipes
  - Water stains
  - Equipment labels
  - Any visible damage

A short video can also show intermittent leaks or unusual sounds that may not occur while the plumber is present.

This information can help the plumber arrive with more appropriate tools or replacement parts.

## Don't Make the Problem Worse
One of the biggest DIY plumbing mistakes is continuing after the homeowner has lost confidence in the diagnosis.

Avoid:

  - Over-tightening fittings
  - Forcing shutoff valves
  - Mixing chemical drain cleaners
  - Removing pressurized plumbing components
  - Opening gas appliances
  - Cutting into walls without identifying the source
  - Repeatedly flushing a blocked toilet
  - Ignoring recurring leaks

A small service call is generally less expensive than turning a minor plumbing issue into water damage or a failed repair.

## Keep Track of Recurring Plumbing Problems
If the same fixture repeatedly develops problems, record:

  - Date of each incident
  - Type of problem
  - Repair performed
  - Replacement parts
  - Plumber used
  - Cost
  - Whether the problem returned

For homeowners, this can help identify aging components.

For landlords, the information becomes even more valuable because recurring maintenance patterns can reveal when replacing a fixture or plumbing component is more economical than continuing to repair it.

## Final Considerations
Troubleshooting a plumbing problem does not mean attempting every repair yourself.

The most useful DIY approach is **diagnosis first, repair second**.

Identify whether the problem is isolated or widespread, locate the visible source, check simple components, protect the property from additional water damage, and document what you find.

Simple problems such as a clogged toilet, dirty faucet aerator, or running toilet may be manageable with basic tools and manufacturer instructions. Problems involving sewage, concealed leaks, major supply lines, water heaters, gas, or repeated system failures are better handled by a qualified professional.

Knowing the difference can save money without turning a minor plumbing problem into a much more expensive one.
