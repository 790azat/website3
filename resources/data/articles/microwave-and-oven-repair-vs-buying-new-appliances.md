When a microwave or oven stops working, homeowners often face a practical question: should the appliance be repaired or replaced? The answer depends on the appliance's age, the nature of the failure, repair cost, availability of parts, installation requirements, and the price of a comparable new model.

A relatively inexpensive repair can extend the useful life of an otherwise reliable appliance. On the other hand, an older appliance with repeated failures or an expensive control-board or heating-system problem may make replacement more economical.

Current 2026 cost data puts professional microwave repairs at roughly **$100 to $200 on average**, while professional oven repairs commonly run about **$130 to $310**, although complex repairs can cost substantially more.

## Start With the Appliance's Age
Age is one of the most important factors in a repair-versus-replacement decision.

A newer appliance that has experienced its first significant failure may still have many years of useful service remaining. If the problem is limited to a replaceable component, repairing it can avoid the cost and inconvenience of purchasing and installing another appliance.

An older appliance requires a different calculation. If it has already needed several repairs, replacement parts are becoming difficult to find, or the manufacturer no longer supports the model, another repair may provide only a short extension of its useful life.

Consumer Reports' repair-or-replace methodology considers the appliance's age, original purchase price, repair cost, depreciation, and replacement cost rather than relying on a single percentage rule.

## Microwave Repair Costs
Microwave repairs are often less expensive than oven repairs, but the appliance's configuration makes a significant difference.

According to 2026 Angi data, microwave repairs generally cost **$100 to $200**, with an average around $175. Small repairs can cost less, while major repairs can exceed $500.

The type of microwave also matters:

| Microwave type | Typical repair range |
|---|---|
| Countertop | 75–200 |
| Over-the-range | 100–300 |
| Built-in | 150–400 |
| Drawer | 150–400 |

Built-in and drawer models can require additional labor because technicians may need to work around cabinetry. Over-the-range models can also involve ventilation and electrical connections.

### Common Microwave Problems
A microwave that stops heating may have a problem involving its magnetron, diode, capacitor, transformer, control system, or another component. Other failures can involve the door switch, turntable motor, fan, lighting, or control panel.

Some problems are relatively straightforward. Others can make repair uneconomical, particularly on an older countertop unit.

A microwave should not be opened casually for DIY diagnosis. Consumer Reports warns that microwave capacitors can retain potentially dangerous electrical charges even after the appliance has been unplugged.

## When a Microwave Repair Makes Sense
Repair is generally easier to justify when the microwave:

  - Is relatively new
  - Has otherwise operated reliably
  - Has a readily available replacement part
  - Has a repair cost well below the price of a comparable replacement
  - Is integrated into cabinetry or ventilation
  - Is covered by a warranty or service agreement

Built-in and over-the-range microwaves deserve particular consideration because replacing them can involve installation work in addition to the appliance purchase.

Consumer Reports notes that replacing an over-the-range microwave may require professional installation, with electrical, cabinet, or ventilation modifications potentially adding significantly to the total project cost.

## When Microwave Replacement Makes More Sense
Replacement becomes more attractive when the microwave is older, has experienced multiple failures, or requires a major repair approaching the cost of a new appliance.

Current retail pricing varies widely. Angi's 2026 data places countertop microwaves at roughly **$50 to $500**, over-the-range models at approximately **$150 to $600**, and built-in models at approximately **$400 to $2,000**, before considering more extensive installation requirements.

That means a $200 repair can be reasonable on a newer built-in appliance but difficult to justify on an inexpensive older countertop model.

## Oven Repair Costs
Ovens are generally more expensive and complicated to repair than basic countertop microwaves.

Angi's 2026 data puts professional oven repairs at approximately **$130 to $310**, with an average around $200. Electric oven repairs are commonly listed around $100 to $250, while gas, convection, and steam models can have broader ranges depending on the failed component.

Common repair costs include:

  - Thermostat: around $150
  - Door repair or replacement: approximately 100–400
  - Control board: approximately 150–300
  - Igniter: around $200
  - Heating element: around $275
  - Door glass: around $200
  - Fan motor: around $175
  - Thermal fuse: around $200

These are national planning figures rather than guaranteed quotes. Labor rates, appliance configuration, replacement-part availability, and local service charges can change the final invoice.

## When an Oven Should Usually Be Repaired
Repair is often worth considering when the oven has a sound body, reliable controls, and a single failed component.

For example, replacing a thermostat, heating element, door gasket, igniter, or similar component can restore normal operation without requiring an entirely new appliance.

Repair can also make sense when the oven is part of a range or kitchen configuration that would be expensive or difficult to replace with a matching model.

Before authorizing work, ask the technician for the diagnosis, parts cost, labor charge, warranty on the repair, and total estimated price.

## When Oven Replacement Becomes More Practical
A replacement becomes more financially compelling when the oven is older and the repair involves a major component, particularly if other components have already begun failing.

Angi recommends considering replacement when repair costs exceed roughly **50% of the value of a new appliance**, particularly for ovens that are more than 15 years old. This is a guideline rather than a strict rule.

A homeowner should also consider whether the appliance still meets household needs. If the oven has poor temperature control, inadequate capacity, damaged insulation, obsolete controls, or recurring electrical problems, spending money on another repair may not address the broader limitations.

## Compare the Total Replacement Cost
The sticker price of a new appliance is only part of the calculation.

Before choosing replacement, include:

  - Appliance purchase price
  - Delivery
  - Installation
  - Electrical work if required
  - Gas connection work where applicable
  - Ventilation modifications
  - Cabinet adjustments
  - Removal and disposal of the old appliance
  - Extended warranty or service-plan costs

This is especially important for built-in ovens and over-the-range microwaves.

A $500 replacement that requires another $300 in installation is actually an $800 project. If a $200 repair restores a newer appliance, the repair may have a considerably different financial profile.

## Consider Warranty and Parts Availability
Always check the warranty before paying for a repair.

A manufacturer's warranty, retailer protection plan, or extended service contract may cover some parts or labor. Consumer Reports also recommends checking warranty coverage before arranging professional microwave repairs.

Parts availability matters for older appliances. A technician may be able to diagnose the problem but discover that the required control board, heating component, or other part is discontinued.

Ask about parts availability before approving an expensive repair, particularly for an older model.

## A Simple Repair-or-Replace Framework
A practical way to approach the decision is to consider five questions:

**1. How old is the appliance?****
**A newer appliance generally has more remaining useful life to justify a repair.

**2. What failed?****
**A replaceable component may be economical, while extensive electrical or control-system failures can be expensive.

**3. How much will the repair cost?****
**Get a written or clearly itemized estimate when possible.

**4. What would an equivalent replacement actually cost?****
**Include installation and disposal, not just the retail price.

**5. Has the appliance already required several repairs?****
**Repeated failures suggest that another repair may not provide the value expected from a newer appliance.

## Final Considerations
The decision between microwave or oven repair and replacement should be based on the appliance's age, condition, repair requirements, and complete replacement cost rather than one universal rule.

For a newer appliance with a relatively inexpensive, isolated failure, professional repair can extend its useful life without the expense and disruption of replacement. For an older appliance with repeated problems, discontinued parts, or a repair approaching a substantial portion of replacement cost, purchasing a new model may be more practical.

Most importantly, do not attempt repairs involving microwave high-voltage components, gas connections, or complicated electrical systems without appropriate training. A professional diagnosis can establish whether the appliance needs a manageable component replacement or whether continued repairs are becoming difficult to justify.
