Refinancing a mortgage replaces an existing home loan with a new one, typically to change the interest rate, monthly payment, loan term, or amount borrowed. Some homeowners also refinance to access home equity or restructure their existing debt.

The refinance process is similar to the original mortgage process, but the closing stage has some important differences. Understanding the steps from application through funding can help borrowers identify costs, review loan terms, and prepare for the final signing.

According to Freddie Mac, a mortgage refinance typically takes about **30 to 45 days**, although the timeline can vary depending on the lender, appraisal, underwriting, documentation, and overall market demand.

## Step 1: Determine Your Refinancing Goals
Before applying, identify what you want the new mortgage to accomplish.

Common refinancing goals include:

  - Lowering the interest rate
  - Reducing the monthly payment
  - Shortening the repayment period
  - Switching from an adjustable-rate mortgage to a fixed-rate loan
  - Accessing home equity through a cash-out refinance
  - Removing certain loan-related costs when eligibility allows

The goal matters because the lowest available rate does not necessarily produce the lowest overall cost. A refinance can involve appraisal fees, lender charges, title services, recording costs, and other expenses.

Freddie Mac estimates that refinancing costs can generally total around **3% to 6% of the loan principal**, although actual costs vary by lender, borrower, property, and location.

## Step 2: Compare Refinance Offers
Homeowners do not have to refinance with their current mortgage lender.

You can request offers from multiple lenders and compare the proposed interest rate, annual percentage rate (APR), loan term, monthly payment, upfront costs, and other conditions.

After submitting an application, the lender generally provides a **Loan Estimate within three business days** after receiving the required application information. The Loan Estimate gives borrowers an early picture of the proposed loan and estimated costs.

When comparing offers, consider the total cost rather than focusing only on the advertised interest rate.

A slightly lower rate may come with higher points or fees, while a loan with fewer upfront costs could have a higher rate.

## Step 3: Submit Your Mortgage Application
Once you select a lender, you'll complete the refinance application and provide financial documentation.

A lender may request:

  - Recent pay stubs
  - W-2 forms
  - Tax returns
  - Bank and investment account statements
  - Information about existing debts
  - Your current mortgage statement
  - Homeowners insurance information
  - Documentation for additional income

The lender evaluates your income, assets, credit history, debts, and other factors when determining whether you qualify for the new mortgage.

Avoid making major financial changes during this period without discussing them with your lender. New debt, large unexplained deposits, changes in employment, or other financial changes can create additional underwriting questions.

## Step 4: Complete the Home Appraisal
Depending on the refinance program and circumstances, the lender may order an appraisal to determine the property's current market value.

The home's value can affect the loan-to-value ratio (LTV), which is important for many refinancing programs.

For example, a homeowner with substantial equity may have access to different loan structures or pricing than someone whose current mortgage balance is close to the property's market value.

An appraisal can also become particularly important with a **cash-out refinance**, because the amount of equity available influences how much can potentially be borrowed.

## Step 5: Go Through Underwriting
During underwriting, the lender reviews the application and supporting documents in greater detail.

The underwriter may verify:

  - Income
  - Employment
  - Credit history
  - Assets
  - Existing mortgage debt
  - Property value
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Other debts and financial obligations

The lender may request additional documentation or explanations during this stage.

Responding quickly can help prevent unnecessary delays. Freddie Mac notes that being prepared and responsive to lender questions can help keep the refinance process moving.

Once underwriting is complete, the lender can issue approval and move the loan toward closing.

## Step 6: Receive and Review the Closing Disclosure
One of the most important steps before closing is receiving the **Closing Disclosure**.

For covered mortgage transactions, the lender must provide the Closing Disclosure at least **three business days before closing**. The document contains the final loan terms, projected payments, closing costs, and other transaction details.

Compare the Closing Disclosure with your most recent Loan Estimate.

Check items such as:

  - Loan amount
  - Interest rate
  - Monthly principal and interest
  - APR
  - Loan term
  - Closing costs
  - Prepaid expenses
  - Escrow amounts
  - Credits
  - Cash required at closing
  - Prepayment penalty, if applicable

If something is significantly different from what you expected, ask the lender or settlement agent for an explanation before signing. The CFPB recommends reviewing the Closing Disclosure carefully and resolving questions before the closing appointment.

## Step 7: Prepare for the Closing Appointment
A refinance closing is similar to the closing you completed when purchasing the home, although there is no property purchase taking place.

Refinance closings commonly take place at a title company, settlement office, attorney's office, or another authorized location, depending on state law and the transaction.

You may need:

  - Government-issued photo identification
  - Any requested financial or insurance documents
  - Funds for closing costs that are not financed
  - Banking information when applicable

Some transactions may allow electronic or remote signing, depending on the lender, closing agent, and applicable state requirements.

## Step 8: Sign the Refinance Documents
At closing, you'll review and sign a series of legally binding documents.

Important documents can include:

  - Closing Disclosure
  - Promissory Note
  - Mortgage or deed of trust
  - Initial escrow disclosure
  - Affidavits and certifications
  - Notice of the right to cancel

The promissory note establishes your obligation to repay the new mortgage. The mortgage or deed of trust establishes the lender's security interest in the property.

Take time to read the documents and ask questions about anything you do not understand. There is generally no reason to rush through the signing process.

## Step 9: Pay Closing Costs and Finalize the Loan
Depending on the refinance structure, closing costs can be paid from your own funds, rolled into the new loan, offset through lender credits, or handled through another permitted arrangement.

A so-called "no-cost" refinance does not necessarily mean that refinancing is free. Costs may instead be incorporated into the loan balance or offset through a higher interest rate.

Consider how the financing structure affects both your monthly payment and the total amount you'll pay over the life of the mortgage.

## Step 10: Understand the Three-Day Right of Rescission
A major difference between buying a home and refinancing is the **right of rescission** that generally applies to covered refinance transactions involving a borrower's principal residence.

For a qualifying refinance, the borrower generally has until midnight of the **third business day** after the transaction to cancel the mortgage contract. The clock begins after the required conditions have been met, including signing the credit contract and receiving the applicable disclosures and notice of the right to cancel.

The rescission period does not apply to every mortgage transaction, so borrowers should confirm the rules applicable to their specific refinance.

Importantly, exercising the right to rescind a refinance does not eliminate the original mortgage debt. The existing mortgage remains in place if the refinance is cancelled.

## Step 11: Loan Funding and Servicing
After the closing requirements and any applicable rescission period are completed, the new loan can be funded and the refinance finalized.

The new mortgage generally pays off the existing mortgage. Your future payments will then be made according to the terms of the new loan.

Your mortgage servicing company may remain the same or may change after the refinance. Make sure you know:

  - Where future payments should be sent
  - Your first payment date
  - Your new payment amount
  - Your escrow balance
  - Whether property taxes and insurance continue to be escrowed
  - How to contact the new servicer if servicing changes

Keep copies of the final closing documents for your records.

## How Long Does Mortgage Refinancing Take?
A refinance commonly takes around **30 to 45 days**, although there is no universal timeline. A straightforward transaction may move faster, while appraisal problems, documentation issues, underwriting questions, title complications, or lender volume can extend the process.

Preparing financial documents before applying and responding promptly to lender requests can help reduce avoidable delays.

## What to Check Before Signing
Before finalizing a refinance, review the transaction as a whole rather than concentrating on the new interest rate.

Consider:

  - Total closing costs
  - New loan balance
  - New monthly payment
  - New repayment term
  - Total interest over the expected holding period
  - Cash received in a cash-out refinance
  - Whether the refinance changes mortgage insurance
  - Break-even period
  - Whether you expect to remain in the property long enough to recover the refinancing costs

A lower monthly payment can result from extending the repayment period, even if the borrower pays more total interest over time. Comparing the full loan structure helps put the monthly payment into context.

## Final Considerations
Refinancing a mortgage involves several stages, from comparing lenders and submitting financial documents to appraisal, underwriting, disclosure review, closing, and final funding.

The closing stage deserves particular attention because it is when the borrower reviews and signs the final loan documents. The Closing Disclosure should be received at least three business days before closing for covered transactions, giving borrowers time to compare the final terms and costs with their earlier Loan Estimate.

For eligible refinance transactions, borrowers may also have a three-business-day right to rescind after closing. Understanding these timelines and reviewing the financial details carefully can make the refinancing process easier to navigate.
