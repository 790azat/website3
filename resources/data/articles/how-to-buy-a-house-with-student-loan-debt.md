Student loan debt does not automatically prevent you from buying a home. Mortgage lenders generally consider student loans as part of your existing monthly debt, but borrowers can still qualify for a mortgage when their income, credit history, savings, and overall debt profile meet the lender's requirements.

The key is understanding how your student loan payment affects your **debt-to-income ratio (DTI)**, mortgage qualification, available cash, and monthly housing budget.

## How Student Loans Affect Mortgage Qualification
When you apply for a mortgage, the lender evaluates your income and recurring debts to determine whether you can reasonably afford the new housing payment.

DTI is calculated by dividing your monthly debt obligations by your gross monthly income. Different mortgage programs and lenders can have different DTI requirements.

For example, suppose your gross monthly income is $7,000 and your existing monthly debts include:

  - Student loan: $450
  - Auto loan: $350
  - Credit card payments: $200
  - Proposed mortgage payment, taxes, and insurance: $2,000

Your total monthly obligations would be $3,000, resulting in a DTI of approximately 42.9%.

The important point is that lenders generally consider the **required student loan payment**, rather than simply looking at the total amount you owe.

## What Student Loan Payment Does the Lender Use?
The payment used for mortgage underwriting can depend on the loan program and the documentation available.

For conventional mortgages sold to Fannie Mae, if the credit report shows a monthly student loan payment, the lender may generally use that payment. If the reported amount is incorrect, the lender can use documentation such as the borrower's most recent student loan statement.

Fannie Mae also has specific rules for student loans showing a $0 payment or loans in deferment or forbearance.

For an income-driven repayment plan, Fannie Mae allows the lender to use a documented $0 payment in qualifying circumstances. For deferred or forbearance loans, the lender may use either a payment calculated as 1% of the outstanding balance or a fully amortizing payment based on documented repayment terms.

This means two borrowers with identical student loan balances could potentially have different qualifying payments depending on their repayment status and mortgage program.

## Buying a House While on an Income-Driven Repayment Plan
Income-driven repayment plans can reduce required monthly payments on eligible federal student loans by basing payments on factors such as income and family size.

A lower documented student loan payment can affect mortgage qualification because DTI calculations generally use monthly debt obligations.

However, borrowers should not change a student loan repayment plan solely to qualify for a mortgage without considering the long-term consequences.

Changing the repayment structure can affect:

  - Monthly student loan payments
  - Total interest paid
  - Loan repayment period
  - Eligibility for federal programs
  - Potential forgiveness benefits
  - Mortgage qualification

Before making a change, compare the mortgage impact with the long-term cost of the student loan.

## FHA Loans and Student Debt
FHA-insured mortgages have their own rules for calculating student loan obligations.

Under current FHA guidance, student loans must generally be included in the borrower's liabilities regardless of payment status. When the reported monthly payment is greater than zero, FHA guidance generally uses the reported or documented payment. When the credit report shows a zero payment, FHA's underwriting rules use a percentage of the outstanding balance unless applicable documentation supports another treatment.

Because FHA and conventional mortgages can treat student loan payments differently, it can be useful to compare multiple mortgage programs before choosing a loan.

An FHA mortgage may also involve mortgage insurance costs, which need to be included when calculating the overall monthly housing expense.

## Conventional Mortgages and DTI
Conventional mortgage underwriting also considers student loan payments when calculating total monthly obligations.

Fannie Mae's current guidance states that its manually underwritten loans generally have a maximum total DTI of 36%, with an increase up to 45% possible when specified credit score and reserve requirements are satisfied. Automated underwriting can involve different eligibility criteria.

These figures should not be treated as a personal affordability target. A lender's maximum qualification amount can be higher than the mortgage payment that comfortably fits your household budget.

The CFPB specifically recommends considering your broader expenses and financial priorities rather than simply borrowing the maximum amount a lender is willing to approve.

## Calculate Your DTI Before Applying
Before contacting lenders, calculate your estimated DTI.

Start with your gross monthly income, then add your recurring debt payments and the estimated monthly housing expense.

A simplified formula is:

**DTI = Total Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income × 100**

For example:

  - Gross monthly income: $8,000
  - Student loan: $500
  - Auto loan: $400
  - Credit cards: $150
  - Proposed housing payment: $2,200

Total debt = $3,250

**DTI = $3,250 ÷ $8,000 = 40.6%**

Your lender may calculate the ratio differently depending on the mortgage program and which income and debt obligations qualify.

## Don't Ignore Your Down Payment
Student loan borrowers sometimes focus heavily on DTI and overlook the cash required to purchase the property.

You may need money for:

  - Down payment
  - Closing costs
  - Prepaid interest
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Property tax and escrow deposits
  - Moving expenses
  - Initial repairs
  - Emergency reserves

The CFPB recommends accounting for costs beyond the mortgage itself, including taxes, insurance, closing costs, moving expenses, repairs, and home improvements.

Using every dollar of savings for a down payment can leave you financially exposed immediately after purchasing the home.

## Should You Pay Off Student Loans Before Buying?
Paying off student loans before applying for a mortgage can reduce your monthly debt obligations, but it is not always the right approach.

Suppose you have $30,000 in student debt and $40,000 in savings. Using $30,000 to eliminate the student loans might remove a monthly payment, but it would leave only $10,000 for the down payment, closing costs, reserves, and unexpected expenses.

A different borrower might have substantial savings and find that paying down debt meaningfully improves their mortgage qualification.

The decision depends on:

  - Student loan balance
  - Required monthly payment
  - Interest rate
  - Income
  - Credit profile
  - Available savings
  - Down payment requirement
  - Mortgage amount
  - Emergency reserves

Run the numbers before moving money.

## Improve Your Mortgage Profile Before Applying
You do not necessarily need to eliminate your student debt to strengthen your mortgage application.

Focus on the factors lenders actually evaluate.

### Keep payments current
Late payments can damage your credit history and may complicate mortgage underwriting.

### Avoid taking on unnecessary new debt
A new auto loan, personal loan, or large credit card balance can increase your monthly obligations and affect your DTI.

### Check your credit reports
Credit-report errors can affect mortgage eligibility and the interest rate you receive. The CFPB recommends checking your credit information and correcting errors before applying for a mortgage.

### Build cash reserves
Having money available after closing can make homeownership more manageable, particularly when repairs, maintenance, insurance, and property taxes become due.

### Maintain stable income
Mortgage lenders generally verify income, employment, assets, debts, and other financial information when evaluating the application.

## Get Mortgage Preapproval Before Shopping
Preapproval can help you determine how student debt affects your actual purchasing power.

Instead of estimating your qualification using an online mortgage calculator alone, provide the lender with your:

  - Student loan statements
  - Pay stubs
  - Tax documents when applicable
  - Bank statements
  - Credit information
  - Other debt documentation

Ask the lender specifically which student loan payment they will use for underwriting.

This is particularly important if your credit report shows a $0 payment, your loans are in deferment or forbearance, or you recently changed repayment plans.

## Compare Mortgage Programs
Student loan borrowers should consider more than one mortgage structure when possible.

A conventional loan, FHA mortgage, VA loan for eligible borrowers, and USDA mortgage can have different requirements for credit, down payment, debt, and underwriting.

The right comparison should include the **total cost of the mortgage**, not simply the minimum down payment.

Consider:

  - Interest rate
  - APR
  - Monthly principal and interest
  - Mortgage insurance
  - Closing costs
  - Student loan payment treatment
  - Down payment requirement
  - Cash reserves
  - Long-term repayment cost

## Can You Buy a House With Student Loans?
Yes. Having student loan debt does not automatically disqualify you from getting a mortgage.

The more important questions are whether your student loan payment fits within the mortgage program's underwriting requirements and whether the resulting housing payment is affordable alongside your other financial obligations.

Before applying, calculate your DTI, review your credit reports, document your student loan repayment terms, preserve adequate savings, and compare mortgage programs.

Most importantly, distinguish between **qualifying for a mortgage** and **comfortably affording a home**. A lender's approval reflects its underwriting standards; your own budget should also account for property taxes, insurance, maintenance, repairs, utilities, student loan payments, and other financial goals.
