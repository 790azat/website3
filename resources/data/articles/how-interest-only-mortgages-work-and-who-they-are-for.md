An interest-only mortgage is structured so that the borrower's scheduled payment can cover only the interest on the loan for a specified period. Unlike a traditional amortizing mortgage, those payments do not reduce the principal balance during the interest-only period.

Interest-only financing can create a lower initial mortgage payment, but that lower payment does not mean the home is becoming less expensive to finance. The borrower is postponing principal repayment rather than eliminating it.

When the interest-only period ends, the mortgage typically transitions to payments that include principal and interest. Because the remaining balance must then be repaid over the remaining loan term, the monthly payment can increase significantly. The CFPB specifically cautions borrowers not to assume they will necessarily be able to sell or refinance before that payment increase occurs.

Understanding how the payment changes, how much principal remains, and what the loan costs over its full term is essential before choosing this type of financing.

## What Is an Interest-Only Mortgage?
With a conventional amortizing mortgage, each principal-and-interest payment includes money toward reducing the loan balance.

With an interest-only mortgage, the scheduled payment during the initial interest-only period can consist only of accrued interest.

For example, suppose you borrow:

**Loan amount:** $500,000
**Interest rate:** 6%
**Interest-only payment:** approximately $2,500 per month

The simplified calculation is:

$500,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = $2,500

That $2,500 payment covers the interest for the month but does not reduce the $500,000 principal balance.

Taxes, homeowners insurance, HOA dues, and other housing costs can be additional.

By comparison, a fully amortizing 30-year mortgage at the same 6% rate would have a principal-and-interest payment of approximately $2,998 per month.

The difference creates more initial cash-flow flexibility, but the interest-only borrower still owes the original principal.

## How the Interest-Only Period Works
An interest-only mortgage normally has an initial period during which the required payment does not include principal.

The exact structure depends on the loan.

For example, a mortgage could have an interest-only period followed by an amortization period. During the first phase, the borrower makes interest-only payments. After that period expires, the payment changes so that principal can be repaid over the remaining term.

The CFPB defines an interest-only mortgage as a loan with scheduled payments requiring payment of only interest for a specified period.

The length of the interest-only period is therefore one of the most important terms to examine.

## What Happens When the Interest-Only Period Ends?
This is the central feature borrowers need to understand.

Suppose you take a $500,000 mortgage at 6% with five years of interest-only payments followed by 25 years of principal-and-interest payments.

During the first five years, the simplified principal-and-interest obligation remains:

**$2,500 per month in interest**

The $500,000 balance does not decline through the required interest-only payments.

Once the five-year period ends, the remaining $500,000 must be amortized over the remaining 25 years.

At the same 6% rate, the principal-and-interest payment would be approximately **$3,221 per month**.

The actual payment can differ depending on the loan's rate structure and other terms.

This illustrates why the initial payment should never be used by itself to judge affordability.

## Are Interest-Only Mortgages Adjustable-Rate Loans?
Not necessarily.

An interest-only feature describes **how the payment is applied**, while a fixed-rate or adjustable-rate feature describes **how the interest rate behaves**.

An interest-only mortgage can therefore have different rate structures depending on the lender and product.

With a fixed-rate structure, the interest rate remains fixed according to the loan terms.

With an adjustable-rate mortgage, the rate can change according to the index, margin, adjustment schedule, and applicable caps.

A mortgage can therefore combine an interest-only payment period with an adjustable interest rate.

The CFPB recommends examining whether the rate can change, how long the term lasts, and whether the mortgage contains features such as an interest-only period or prepayment penalty when comparing loans.

## Interest-Only vs. Traditional Mortgage
The main difference is principal repayment.

|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| \*\*Feature\*\* | \*\*Interest-Only Mortgage\*\* | \*\*Fully Amortizing Mortgage\*\* |
| Initial payment | Lower in many cases | Higher |
| Principal reduction initially | None from required payment | Yes |
| Equity from scheduled payments | Generally does not increase from principal repayment | Builds through principal payments |
| Payment after initial period | Can increase substantially | Usually follows the original amortization schedule |
| Long-term balance | Can remain high for years | Declines over time |
| Cash-flow flexibility | Higher initially | Lower initially |
| Payment certainty | Depends on rate structure | Depends on rate structure |

A traditional mortgage gradually converts monthly payments into greater principal repayment as the loan ages.

With an interest-only mortgage, the borrower has to make a deliberate plan for the principal balance.

## Who Uses Interest-Only Mortgages?
Interest-only financing can appeal to borrowers whose financial circumstances make temporary payment flexibility particularly valuable.

Potential borrowers can include:

  - High-income professionals with variable compensation
  - Business owners with uneven cash flow
  - Borrowers expecting significant future income
  - Buyers purchasing higher-value properties
  - Some investors
  - Borrowers who intend to make voluntary principal payments
  - Buyers who have a defined short-term financing strategy

The important distinction is that an interest-only mortgage is generally more appropriate for someone who understands the future payment structure and has a realistic plan for the principal.

It should not be viewed simply as a way to qualify for a larger home because the initial payment is lower.

## High-Income Borrowers and Variable Income
Some borrowers have strong annual income but experience substantial variations throughout the year.

For example, a business owner may receive most of their income through periodic distributions, while a salesperson may receive a significant portion through commissions.

An interest-only structure can reduce the required monthly mortgage payment during periods when cash flow is lower.

However, lenders still have to evaluate a borrower's ability to repay a mortgage. Under the federal ability-to-repay rule, creditors generally must make a reasonable, good-faith determination that the borrower can repay the loan. Lenders generally consider income, assets, employment, credit history, and monthly expenses rather than relying only on an introductory payment.

## Can You Make Principal Payments During the Interest-Only Period?
Often, yes, depending on the loan terms.

An interest-only payment generally represents the **required minimum payment**, not necessarily the maximum amount the borrower is allowed to pay.

If the loan does not impose restrictions on additional principal payments, a borrower may voluntarily pay more than the required amount.

For example, suppose the required payment on a $500,000 loan is $2,500 per month.

A borrower could potentially choose to pay $3,500:

  - $2,500 toward required interest
  - $1,000 toward principal

The additional $1,000 would reduce the balance if applied to principal under the loan's terms.

That can reduce future interest costs and the eventual payment transition.

However, borrowers should verify whether the loan has any prepayment restrictions or penalties.

## How Much Does an Interest-Only Mortgage Cost?
The lower initial payment can be misleading if you look only at monthly cash flow.

Consider a hypothetical $500,000 mortgage at 6%.

With five years of interest-only payments:

**Monthly interest:** $2,500
**Annual interest:** $30,000
**Five-year interest:** $150,000

At the end of those five years, the borrower could still owe the full $500,000.

A fully amortizing mortgage would have reduced the principal during those five years.

Therefore, the relevant comparison is not simply:

**Interest-only payment vs. regular mortgage payment**

It is:

**Total interest + principal repayment + fees + future payment obligations**

The CFPB recommends considering the overall cost of a mortgage, including the interest rate, points, closing costs, APR, loan term, and potentially risky features.

## Interest-Only Mortgages and Home Equity
Home equity generally consists of the property's value minus the mortgage debt secured by it.

When a borrower makes principal payments, the mortgage balance declines and equity can increase, assuming the property value remains unchanged.

With required interest-only payments, the loan balance does not decline from those payments.

For example:

**Home value:** $700,000
**Mortgage balance:** $500,000
**Initial equity:** $200,000

If the property value remains $700,000 and the borrower makes only interest payments for several years, the mortgage could still have a $500,000 balance.

The borrower therefore would not build additional equity through scheduled principal reduction during that period.

Property appreciation could still increase equity, but appreciation is not guaranteed.

## What Are the Main Risks?
The biggest risk is the payment transition.

A borrower who can comfortably afford a $2,500 interest-only payment may not be able to comfortably afford a $3,200 or $3,500 payment later.

Other risks include:

### Principal Does Not Automatically Decline
Required interest-only payments generally do not reduce the loan balance.

### Refinancing Is Not Guaranteed
A borrower may intend to refinance before the payment increases, but future rates, property values, credit, income, or lending standards could make refinancing more difficult.

The CFPB specifically warns borrowers not to assume they will be able to refinance or sell the property before an interest-only payment increases.

### Property Values Can Fall
If the home's value declines while the mortgage balance remains high, refinancing or selling can become more complicated.

### Rates May Increase
If the mortgage is also adjustable-rate, both the payment structure and interest rate can create additional uncertainty.

### More Interest May Be Paid
Delaying principal repayment generally means the borrower carries a larger balance for longer, potentially increasing total interest costs.

## Interest-Only Mortgages and Qualified Mortgages
Interest-only loans have an important regulatory distinction.

Under the CFPB's Qualified Mortgage framework, an interest-only period is generally considered a risky feature that prevents a loan from meeting the general Qualified Mortgage requirements.

That does not mean every interest-only mortgage is prohibited.

It means that interest-only financing can fall outside the standard Qualified Mortgage category and may be offered through different lending channels and underwriting structures.

Borrowers should ask the lender what type of mortgage they are being offered and what consumer protections and underwriting requirements apply.

## Why Conventional Financing May Not Be Available
Borrowers should not assume that an interest-only mortgage can be obtained through the same conventional mortgage channels used for a standard owner-occupied loan.

For example, Fannie Mae's current Selling Guide identifies mortgages with an interest-only feature among products it does not purchase.

As a result, interest-only financing may be available through other lenders or specialized mortgage products rather than standard conforming conventional financing.

This can affect:

  - Interest rates
  - Loan limits
  - Down payment requirements
  - Reserve requirements
  - Property eligibility
  - Documentation
  - Underwriting
  - Closing costs

Shopping across lenders is particularly important for this type of mortgage.

## Interest-Only Mortgages for Investment Properties
An interest-only structure can sometimes appeal to real estate investors because reducing the required payment can improve short-term property cash flow.

For example, an investor purchasing a rental property may prefer to preserve cash for:

  - Property improvements
  - Maintenance
  - Vacancy periods
  - Additional investments
  - Operating reserves

However, the lower mortgage payment does not automatically mean the property generates positive cash flow.

An investor should calculate rental income against the complete operating expenses and debt obligations.

The mortgage balance also remains higher when required payments do not reduce principal.

## How to Qualify
Qualification depends heavily on the lender and the specific mortgage product.

A lender may review:

  - Credit history
  - Income
  - Employment
  - Assets
  - Debt-to-income ratio
  - Cash reserves
  - Property value
  - Down payment
  - Loan amount
  - Existing debts
  - Expected future payment

Borrowers with complex income may face additional documentation requirements.

The federal ability-to-repay framework generally requires lenders to evaluate the borrower's financial ability to repay rather than qualifying the borrower solely using the initial interest-only payment.

## Questions to Ask an Interest-Only Mortgage Lender
Before accepting an offer, ask:

1.  How long is the interest-only period?
2.  What happens when it ends?
3.  What will the estimated payment become?
4.  Is the interest rate fixed or adjustable?
5.  If adjustable, when can the rate change?
6.  What are the rate caps?
7.  How much principal will remain when the interest-only period ends?
8.  Can I make additional principal payments?
9.  Is there a prepayment penalty?
10. What is the APR?
11. What are the closing costs?
12. What happens if I refinance later?
13. What income and reserve requirements apply?
14. Is this a Qualified Mortgage?
15. Which lender or investor is providing the financing?

The most important answer is usually the projected payment **after** the interest-only period.

## How to Evaluate an Interest-Only Mortgage
Before choosing this structure, calculate three separate numbers:

**1. Initial required payment**

What you must pay during the interest-only period.

**2. Future required payment**

What you may have to pay once principal repayment begins.

**3. Total financing cost**

How much interest, fees, and other financing costs you expect to pay over the period you intend to hold the mortgage.

Also consider what happens if your plan changes.

If your strategy depends on selling the property, receiving a large bonus, refinancing, or earning substantially more income in the future, consider whether you could still afford the mortgage if that event does not occur.

## Final Considerations
Interest-only mortgages can provide lower required payments during an initial period by postponing principal repayment. That can create cash-flow flexibility for certain borrowers, but it does not reduce the amount originally borrowed.

Once the interest-only period ends, the borrower may face substantially higher payments because the remaining principal must be repaid over a shorter remaining period.

The structure can make sense for borrowers who have strong financial resources, variable cash flow, or a clearly defined strategy for managing the principal balance. It requires more careful planning than simply comparing the initial monthly payment.

Borrowers should evaluate the **initial payment, future payment, interest-rate structure, remaining principal, APR, fees, refinancing assumptions, and total expected financing cost** before choosing an interest-only mortgage.

Most importantly, do not base the decision on an assumption that you will automatically be able to refinance or sell before the payment increases. Future property values, interest rates, income, and lending standards can all change.
