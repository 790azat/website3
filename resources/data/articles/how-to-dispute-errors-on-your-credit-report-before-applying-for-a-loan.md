A credit report can affect your ability to qualify for a loan and the terms a lender offers. An incorrect account, inaccurate late payment, wrong balance, duplicate debt, or identity-theft account can make your credit profile appear different from your actual financial history.

Before applying for a mortgage, auto loan, personal loan, or other major financing, reviewing your credit reports gives you an opportunity to identify and dispute inaccurate information.

Under federal law, consumers have the right to dispute inaccurate or incomplete information on their credit reports. The process is generally free, and consumers can dispute information with both the credit reporting company and the company that supplied the information.

## Why Review Your Credit Report Before Applying for a Loan?
Lenders can use information in your credit reports as part of their evaluation of a loan application. Credit information can affect whether you qualify and, depending on the loan, the pricing and terms you receive.

An error does not automatically mean you will be denied, but inaccurate information can affect the credit profile a lender reviews.

Common credit-report problems include:

  - Accounts that do not belong to you
  - Incorrect late-payment records
  - Wrong account balances
  - Incorrect credit limits
  - Duplicate accounts
  - Closed accounts reported as open
  - Incorrect dates
  - Identity-theft accounts
  - Incorrect personal information

The CFPB specifically recommends checking for inaccurate or incomplete information in these areas.

Checking your reports early is especially useful when preparing for a mortgage because a dispute investigation can take time.

## Get Your Credit Reports
Start by obtaining your credit reports from the three nationwide credit reporting companies:

  - Equifax
  - Experian
  - TransUnion

You can obtain your reports through AnnualCreditReport.com. The FTC notes that consumers have the right to obtain free reports from the three major nationwide credit bureaus.

Review all three reports rather than assuming that they contain exactly the same information.

An account might appear on one report but not another. A creditor could also report a different balance, account status, or payment history to different credit reporting companies.

If an error appears on multiple reports, you may need to dispute it separately with each company reporting the inaccurate information.

## What Errors Should You Look For?
Go through each account carefully rather than only checking your credit score.

### Accounts You Do Not Recognize
Confirm that every account actually belongs to you.

An unfamiliar account can result from identity theft, a mixed credit file, or another reporting problem.

The CFPB identifies accounts belonging to another person and identity-theft accounts as examples of errors consumers should look for.

### Incorrect Account Status
Check whether accounts are accurately described.

Examples include:

  - A closed account reported as open
  - A paid account showing an incorrect balance
  - A current account reported as delinquent
  - An account showing you as the owner when you were only an authorized user

### Incorrect Payment History
Review individual payment entries carefully.

An account could incorrectly show a late payment even though you paid on time.

If you believe a late payment is wrong, gather bank statements, payment confirmations, account statements, or correspondence that support your position.

### Incorrect Dates
Credit reports contain various dates that can affect how account information is reported.

Look for potential errors involving:

  - Account opening date
  - Date of last payment
  - Date of first delinquency
  - Account closure date

### Duplicate Accounts
The same debt should not incorrectly appear multiple times as separate obligations.

The CFPB lists duplicate accounts as one of the data-management problems consumers should watch for when reviewing credit reports.

### Incorrect Balances or Credit Limits
Compare reported balances and credit limits with your latest account statements.

An incorrect credit limit can affect the information used to calculate revolving-credit utilization, while an incorrect balance can make your outstanding debt appear higher or lower than it actually is.

## Gather Supporting Documentation
Before submitting a dispute, collect evidence supporting your position.

Depending on the error, useful documentation may include:

  - Bank statements
  - Credit card statements
  - Loan statements
  - Payment confirmations
  - Account-closure letters
  - Correspondence with the creditor
  - Court or legal documents
  - Identity-theft documentation
  - Copies of previous communications

The CFPB recommends explaining what is wrong, why it is wrong, and including copies of documents supporting the dispute. Keep the original documents for your records.

The more specifically you can identify the inaccurate information, the easier it can be for the company to investigate the issue.

## Dispute the Error With the Credit Reporting Company
Once you have identified an error, dispute it with each credit reporting company that reports the inaccurate information.

Your dispute should generally include:

  - Full name and contact information
  - The account or item being disputed
  - Account number, if applicable
  - A clear explanation of the error
  - Why the information is inaccurate or incomplete
  - The correction you are requesting
  - Supporting documentation

If you submit the dispute by mail, the CFPB recommends considering certified mail and requesting a return receipt so you have documentation that the dispute was received.

Online disputes may also be available through the credit reporting company's website.

## Contact the Company That Reported the Information
Credit reporting companies receive account information from businesses known as **furnishers**.

Furnishers can include:

  - Banks
  - Credit card companies
  - Mortgage lenders
  - Auto lenders
  - Collection companies
  - Other businesses that report consumer-account information

The CFPB recommends disputing inaccurate information with both the credit reporting company and the company that supplied the information.

For example, if your credit card issuer incorrectly reports a late payment, you can dispute the entry with the credit reporting company and separately contact the credit card issuer.

The furnisher generally must investigate a properly submitted dispute concerning information it provided.

## What Happens After You File?
The credit reporting company generally investigates the dispute and forwards relevant information to the company that supplied the disputed information.

If the furnisher determines that the information is inaccurate or cannot be verified, it must generally correct or remove the information and notify the relevant credit reporting companies.

The credit reporting company generally has **30 days** to investigate a dispute.

Certain circumstances can extend the investigation period to **45 days**, including some situations in which the consumer provides additional relevant information during the investigation.

After the investigation, you should receive the results.

If a correction is made, review the updated report to make sure the information was actually corrected.

## How Long Should You Wait Before Applying for a Loan?
There is no universal waiting period after disputing an error.

The practical issue is whether the correction has been completed and reflected accurately in the relevant credit reports.

The FTC recommends, when possible, waiting until a mistake has been removed and the credit information is accurate, complete, and current before applying for a major loan such as a mortgage or auto loan.

For example, if you plan to apply for a mortgage in three months, reviewing your credit reports today gives you substantially more time to identify and resolve problems than waiting until your lender has already started underwriting.

## What If You Are Already Applying for a Mortgage?
If you discover an error after submitting a mortgage application, notify your lender and continue working through the dispute process.

Ask the loan officer how the disputed information will be treated during underwriting.

Do not assume that an unresolved dispute will automatically result in a denial or automatically be ignored.

Mortgage underwriting can involve additional requirements depending on the loan program and the nature of the disputed information.

Providing the lender with documentation can help explain the issue while the credit-reporting investigation is underway.

## What If the Error Is Related to Identity Theft?
An unfamiliar account can sometimes be more than a simple reporting mistake.

If someone used your personal information to open an account or obtain credit, treat the situation as potential identity theft.

The CFPB recommends using IdentityTheft.gov for suspected identity theft and recovery assistance.

You may need to provide additional documentation to demonstrate that the account or transaction was not authorized by you.

Identity-theft accounts should not simply be treated as ordinary disputes without investigating the underlying cause.

## What If the Information Is Accurate but Negative?
A dispute is intended to correct information that is inaccurate or incomplete.

It generally cannot be used simply to remove accurate negative information because it is hurting your credit profile.

For example, if you genuinely made a late payment and the creditor accurately reported it, filing a dispute does not generally require the creditor or credit reporting company to remove it.

The CFPB states that accurate negative information generally cannot be removed simply because it is negative. Most negative information can generally remain on a credit report for seven years, although some categories have different reporting periods.

This distinction is important when preparing for a loan.

The goal of a dispute is to make your credit report **accurate**, not to remove legitimate negative information.

## Be Careful With Credit-Repair Companies
Consumers do not need to pay a company to dispute inaccurate information.

Federal law gives consumers the right to dispute inaccuracies directly with credit reporting companies and furnishers. The CFPB warns consumers about companies that promise to remove current, accurate negative information from credit reports.

Be particularly cautious of claims that a company can:

  - Remove all negative information
  - Guarantee a specific credit score increase
  - Delete accurate late payments
  - Create a new credit identity
  - Eliminate legitimate debts from your credit history

An accurate negative account cannot generally be erased simply because a company submits a dispute on your behalf.

## What If the Dispute Is Denied?
A company may determine that the information is accurate after investigating your dispute.

If you disagree with the result, review the explanation and determine whether you have additional evidence.

Depending on the circumstances, you may:

1.  Submit additional supporting documentation.
2.  Continue communicating with the furnisher.
3.  Contact the credit reporting company again.
4.  Request that a statement explaining the dispute be included in your credit file.
5.  Submit a complaint to the CFPB when appropriate.

The CFPB states that consumers can request a statement of dispute when an issue remains unresolved.

If you intend to submit a CFPB complaint about inaccurate information, the agency generally requires consumers to first dispute the information directly with the relevant credit reporting company.

## Keep a Complete Dispute File
Maintain copies of everything associated with the dispute.

Your records can include:

  - Original credit report
  - Copy showing the disputed item
  - Dispute submission
  - Supporting documents
  - Confirmation number
  - Certified-mail receipt
  - Furnisher correspondence
  - Investigation results
  - Updated credit report

Keeping a timeline can be particularly useful if the same information appears again after being corrected.

## Credit Report Review Before a Mortgage Application
If you are preparing to apply for a mortgage, review your credit reports before beginning the application process.

A simple checklist includes:

  - Verify your name and identifying information
  - Check every account for ownership
  - Review payment history
  - Check account balances
  - Check credit limits
  - Look for duplicate accounts
  - Review account status
  - Check important dates
  - Look for possible identity theft
  - Gather documentation for genuine errors
  - Submit disputes to the appropriate credit reporting companies
  - Contact the furnishers
  - Review the investigation results
  - Confirm corrections on updated reports

This process can help ensure that the credit information used during the loan process is as accurate as possible.

## How Credit Report Errors Can Affect Loan Preparation
Correcting an error does not guarantee loan approval or a particular interest rate.

Lenders consider multiple factors, which can include:

  - Credit history
  - Income
  - Debt-to-income ratio
  - Assets
  - Employment
  - Down payment
  - Loan type
  - Property information for a mortgage

However, correcting inaccurate information removes one potential source of incorrect data from the lender's review.

If a disputed account affects your credit score, credit utilization, payment history, or reported debt, the correction could change the information available to a lender.

The actual effect depends on the specific error and the lender's underwriting process.

## A Simple Example
Suppose you are preparing to apply for a $350,000 mortgage.

You review your credit reports and discover that a credit card issuer has reported a $4,000 balance even though you paid the account down to $500.

You gather:

  - Recent account statements
  - Payment confirmations
  - Bank records

You then dispute the inaccurate balance with the relevant credit reporting company and the credit card issuer.

If the investigation confirms that the balance was reported incorrectly, the information can be corrected.

The important point is not that the correction guarantees a particular mortgage rate. Instead, the lender will have more accurate information when evaluating your application.

## When to Start Reviewing Your Credit
Consider reviewing your credit reports several months before applying for a major loan.

That gives you time to:

  - Identify potential errors
  - Gather documentation
  - Submit disputes
  - Wait for investigations
  - Review updated reports
  - Address additional problems if necessary

This can be particularly useful when preparing for a mortgage because the application may involve extensive financial documentation and underwriting.

## Final Considerations
Disputing an error on your credit report is generally free, and consumers have the right to challenge inaccurate or incomplete information directly with both the credit reporting company and the company that supplied the information.

The process starts with reviewing your reports carefully, identifying the specific information that is wrong, gathering supporting evidence, and submitting a clear dispute. Credit reporting companies generally have 30 days to investigate, although certain circumstances allow up to 45 days.

If you are preparing for a mortgage or another major loan, starting early gives you more time to resolve legitimate reporting errors before the application reaches underwriting.

Just as importantly, distinguish between an **inaccurate negative item** and a **negative item that is accurate**. A legitimate late payment or debt generally cannot be removed simply because it could make borrowing more difficult.

The objective is to enter the loan process with a credit report that is **accurate, complete, and supported by your financial records**.
