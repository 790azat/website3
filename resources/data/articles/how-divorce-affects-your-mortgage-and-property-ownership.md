Divorce can turn a jointly owned home into one of the most complicated financial issues to resolve. Even when a divorce decree says one spouse will keep the house and make the mortgage payments, that agreement does not automatically remove the other spouse from the mortgage or change the lender's rights.

The distinction between **property ownership and mortgage liability** is especially important. A spouse can be removed from the home's title while remaining legally responsible for the mortgage. Conversely, someone may acquire an ownership interest in a property without becoming personally responsible for an existing mortgage.

The exact rules vary by state and by the mortgage program, but understanding how title, debt, equity, refinancing, and mortgage assumptions interact can help divorcing homeowners avoid costly mistakes.

## Who Owns the Home After Divorce?
The first question is who has legal ownership of the property.

Ownership is generally established through the property's deed or other applicable title records. A divorce settlement or court order can determine which spouse is awarded the home, but the required legal steps for changing title depend on state law.

For example, a divorce agreement might provide that:

  - Spouse A keeps the house.
  - Spouse B transfers their ownership interest.
  - Spouse A assumes responsibility for the mortgage.
  - Spouse B receives a portion of the home's equity.

But completing the ownership transfer does not automatically change the mortgage contract.

The CFPB specifically warns that signing over ownership of a home does **not** remove a person's joint responsibility for the mortgage.

## Mortgage Liability Is Different From Home Ownership
This distinction is critical.

Suppose both spouses originally signed a $400,000 mortgage. During the divorce, the court awards the house to one spouse.

If the departing spouse signs a deed transferring their ownership interest, that person may no longer own the property. However, if their name remains on the mortgage note, the lender may still consider them responsible for the debt.

A divorce decree generally governs the obligations between the spouses. It does not automatically rewrite the contract with the mortgage lender.

The CFPB explains that a divorce decree or property settlement can assign responsibility for a debt to one spouse, but the creditor can generally continue to pursue anyone whose name remains on the loan unless that person is contractually released.

## What Happens to the Existing Mortgage?
There are several possible ways to handle a jointly financed home after divorce.

### One spouse keeps the home and refinances
The spouse who keeps the property can refinance the existing mortgage into their own name, assuming they qualify.

The new mortgage pays off the old joint mortgage, removing the former spouse from the original loan.

Refinancing can also potentially provide cash to compensate the departing spouse for their share of the home's equity.

However, refinancing means applying for a new mortgage. The remaining spouse must generally qualify based on their own income, credit, debts, assets, and other underwriting factors.

### One spouse keeps the existing mortgage
In some circumstances, the spouse who keeps the property may be able to assume liability for the existing mortgage rather than refinancing it.

This can be particularly important when the existing mortgage has a substantially lower interest rate than currently available mortgages.

The CFPB reported in 2024 that mortgage servicers and applicable federal housing program guidelines provide processes for certain successor homeowners to assume existing mortgage obligations following divorce, although requirements vary by loan type and investor.

An assumption is not automatically available for every mortgage. The servicer and mortgage investor's rules determine whether the remaining spouse can assume the loan and whether the departing spouse can be released from liability.

### Both spouses remain on the mortgage temporarily
Some divorcing couples may leave the existing mortgage in place while one spouse lives in the property and makes the payments.

This can be workable as a temporary arrangement, but it creates continuing financial ties.

If the spouse living in the home misses payments, the mortgage delinquency can affect the credit of everyone still legally responsible for the loan.

It can also make it harder for the departing spouse to qualify for another mortgage.

The CFPB notes that someone who remains legally responsible for an old joint mortgage can encounter difficulty qualifying for a new mortgage.

## What Is a Mortgage Assumption?
A mortgage assumption allows an eligible borrower to take responsibility for an existing mortgage under the applicable loan's terms.

This can be different from refinancing.

With a refinance:

**Old mortgage → paid off → new mortgage**

With an assumption:

**Existing mortgage → remains in place → new borrower assumes responsibility**

An assumption can therefore preserve the existing interest rate and other loan terms when permitted.

However, the spouse seeking to assume the mortgage may need to demonstrate the ability to make the payments, and the existing borrower may need to be formally released from liability.

The CFPB's 2024 review specifically noted that homeowners following divorce can encounter problems when seeking assumptions and that federal rules and mortgage-program guidelines can require servicers to process qualifying requests.

## What Happens to the Home's Equity?
The couple also needs to determine how the home's equity will be divided.

A simplified calculation is:

**Home value − Mortgage balance = Home equity**

Suppose:

  - Home value: $600,000
  - Mortgage balance: $350,000
  - Estimated equity: $250,000

If the spouses have equal ownership interests and the divorce agreement provides for an equal division, the departing spouse might receive approximately $125,000.

But the actual calculation can be more complicated.

The agreement may account for:

  - Different ownership percentages
  - Contributions toward the down payment
  - Separate property
  - Renovation expenses
  - Other marital assets and debts
  - Selling costs
  - Tax considerations
  - State-specific property laws

A home appraisal or comparative market analysis can help establish a reasonable value before an equity settlement is finalized.

## Buying Out a Spouse's Share
A buyout occurs when one spouse keeps the home and compensates the other for their ownership interest.

For example:

**Home value:** $700,000
**Mortgage balance:** $400,000
**Equity:** $300,000

If the spouses agree that each owns 50% of the equity, the departing spouse's share could be approximately $150,000 before considering other adjustments.

The spouse keeping the home could potentially fund the buyout through:

  - Cash savings
  - A refinance
  - A home equity loan
  - Other marital assets
  - An agreed offset against other property

The financing method needs to be evaluated carefully because increasing the mortgage balance can raise the monthly payment and total interest cost.

## What If the Home Is Underwater?
The situation becomes more complicated if the mortgage balance exceeds the home's current market value.

Suppose:

  - Home value: $350,000
  - Mortgage balance: $375,000

The property has approximately **negative $25,000 in equity** before considering selling costs.

In that situation, selling the property may require additional funds to satisfy the mortgage unless the lender agrees to another arrangement.

Refinancing can also be difficult because the property's value may not support the required loan amount.

The CFPB has noted that an underwater property can make both selling and refinancing difficult following a separation.

## What Happens If the Home Is Sold?
Selling the property can provide a clean way to separate the spouses' financial obligations.

The process generally involves:

1.  Agreeing to sell the property.
2.  Establishing a listing price.
3.  Marketing the home.
4.  Accepting an offer.
5.  Paying the mortgage from the sale proceeds.
6.  Paying applicable transaction costs.
7.  Dividing remaining proceeds according to the divorce agreement and applicable law.

For example:

**Sale price:** $600,000
**Mortgage payoff:** $350,000
**Other selling costs:** $40,000
**Remaining proceeds:** $210,000

The final division depends on the divorce settlement and applicable property law.

Selling can also eliminate the problem of one former spouse remaining liable for a mortgage on a home they no longer own.

## How Divorce Can Affect Your Credit
Divorce itself does not automatically remove joint debts from your credit obligations.

If both spouses remain responsible for a mortgage and the payments are made late, the negative payment history can potentially affect both borrowers.

This is true even if the divorce agreement says that only one spouse is supposed to make the payments.

The CFPB explains that creditors generally continue to treat both borrowers as responsible when both names remain on the loan.

After divorce, monitor your credit reports and confirm that mortgage payments are being made on time until the loan is formally paid off, assumed with an appropriate release, or otherwise restructured.

## Can You Get a New Mortgage After Divorce?
Yes. A divorced borrower can generally qualify for a mortgage based on their own financial qualifications.

Mortgage lenders generally cannot deny credit simply because an applicant is divorced or unmarried. They can, however, consider financial obligations such as alimony or child support when applicable and permitted under lending rules.

The lender may evaluate:

  - Income
  - Credit history
  - Existing debts
  - Housing expenses
  - Down payment
  - Assets
  - Alimony obligations
  - Child support obligations
  - Other recurring liabilities

If your former spouse's income was previously used to qualify for the original mortgage, you may need to demonstrate that your own income can support the new loan.

## What Happens to the Mortgage if One Spouse Dies During the Process?
Divorce and mortgage issues can overlap with other life events, particularly when ownership or mortgage changes have not yet been completed.

The CFPB notes that certain federal rules and mortgage-program guidelines provide protections and processes for successor homeowners, including people who obtain ownership through divorce.

Because state property law and individual mortgage terms can affect the outcome, homeowners should keep copies of the divorce decree, deed, mortgage documents, and communications with the servicer.

## Should You Refinance After Divorce?
Refinancing can make sense when one spouse needs to remove the other from the mortgage and can qualify independently.

But refinancing is not always the only option.

Before refinancing, compare:

  - Current mortgage interest rate
  - New mortgage interest rate
  - Remaining loan balance
  - Closing costs
  - New monthly payment
  - Loan term
  - Cash-out amount
  - Equity settlement
  - Mortgage assumption availability

The CFPB has specifically cautioned that some homeowners following divorce have reported being pushed toward refinancing when an assumption or another way of managing the existing mortgage may have been available.

If your existing mortgage has a significantly lower rate, ask the servicer whether an assumption or release-of-liability process is available before automatically replacing the loan.

## What If You Are Not on the Mortgage but Are on the Title?
Being on the title and being on the mortgage are separate matters.

You can have an ownership interest without being personally obligated on the mortgage note.

Conversely, you can remain obligated on the mortgage after transferring your ownership interest.

This is why divorcing spouses should review both documents separately:

**Deed/title:** Who owns the property?

**Mortgage/note:** Who owes the lender?

Changing one does not necessarily change the other.

## A Practical Divorce Mortgage Checklist
Before finalizing the property settlement, determine:

1.  Who will own the home?
2.  Who will live there?
3.  What is the home's current market value?
4.  What is the current mortgage balance?
5.  How much equity exists?
6.  Will the home be sold or retained?
7.  If retained, can the remaining spouse qualify alone?
8.  Is mortgage assumption available?
9.  Does the departing spouse need a formal release from liability?
10. How will the equity buyout be funded?
11. Who will pay the mortgage until the transaction is completed?
12. Who will pay property taxes and homeowners insurance?
13. How will repairs and maintenance be handled?
14. What happens if refinancing or assumption is denied?
15. What happens if the property cannot be sold for enough to pay off the mortgage?

Putting these details into the divorce settlement can help avoid ambiguity later.

## Final Considerations
Divorce does not automatically change a mortgage.

The most important distinction is between **ownership of the property and responsibility for the mortgage debt**. A spouse can transfer their ownership interest while remaining liable for the loan, and a divorce decree alone generally does not release a borrower from a mortgage contract.

When one spouse wants to keep the home, the main possibilities can include refinancing, assuming the existing mortgage when permitted, or leaving both spouses on the loan temporarily. If the home cannot be comfortably financed by one spouse, selling the property may provide a cleaner way to separate the financial obligations.

Before signing a property settlement, determine the home's value, mortgage balance, equity, financing options, and requirements for removing a spouse from the loan. Because divorce and property laws vary by state, a qualified family-law attorney and mortgage professional can help coordinate the legal and financing sides of the transaction.
