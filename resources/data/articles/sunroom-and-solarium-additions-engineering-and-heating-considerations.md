A sunroom can add bright, flexible living space without the cost of building a conventional fully enclosed addition. A solarium takes the concept further, using extensive glass walls and often a glass roof to maximize daylight and outdoor views.

But the more glass a structure contains, the more carefully homeowners need to consider **structural loads, thermal performance, heating and cooling, moisture control, and solar heat gain**.

A three-season sunroom may be relatively simple because it is not intended to function as fully conditioned living space. A four-season sunroom or solarium is much closer to a conventional home addition and needs to be designed accordingly.

## Sunroom vs. Solarium: What's the Difference?
A **sunroom** is an enclosed addition designed around large windows or other glazed openings. It can be built as a three-season or four-season space.

A **solarium** generally has substantially more glass, often including a glass roof. It is sometimes also called a conservatory, although architectural definitions vary.

The distinction matters because increasing the glazed area changes the engineering and energy requirements.

The International Residential Code includes specific provisions for sunrooms and distinguishes between unconditioned, thermally isolated spaces and conditioned spaces that are connected to the home's living area.

## How Much Does a Sunroom Cost in 2026?
Current national estimates put the cost of a new sunroom at approximately **$22,000 to $75,000**, with an average around **$47,000**, according to Angi's August 2026 data. Custom projects with premium materials can cost substantially more.

Approximate costs by type include:

| Project Type | Typical 2026 Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Small prefabricated sunroom | 6,000–40,000 |
| Three-season room | 10,000–40,000 |
| Four-season room | 25,000–120,000 |
| Solarium | 30,000–75,000 |
| Large custom projects | Can exceed $100,000 |

HomeGuide places custom four-season sunrooms at roughly **$200 to $400 per square foot**, while glass solariums can range considerably higher depending on design and construction.

The difference between a basic three-season enclosure and a conditioned four-season addition is substantial because the latter requires insulation, appropriate glazing, heating and cooling, electrical work, and a more complete building envelope.

## The Foundation Comes First
One of the first engineering questions is whether the existing structure can support the addition.

A new sunroom may require:

  - Concrete footings
  - A slab foundation
  - Crawlspace construction
  - Frost-protected foundations
  - Structural connections to the existing house
  - Reinforcement of an existing deck

Angi's 2026 estimates place sunroom foundation work at approximately **$500 to $6,000**, although difficult sites and larger foundations can cost more.

An existing deck should never automatically be assumed to be adequate for conversion into a four-season room. A structural engineer may need to determine whether the footings, framing, connections, and foundation can support the additional dead and live loads.

Building-code requirements also address additions and their relationship to existing structural elements. The 2024 IRC states that additions and alterations must comply with applicable requirements and must not make an existing structure less compliant with the code.

## Glass Creates Structural Challenges
Large windows and glass roof panels are heavier and more structurally demanding than conventional wall assemblies.

The structure must account for loads such as:

  - Dead load from framing and glazing
  - Snow
  - Wind
  - Roof loads
  - Maintenance loads
  - Local environmental conditions

Sloped glass is particularly important in solariums. Building-code provisions specifically address glass used in sunrooms and sloped glazing because it must be designed for combinations of dead, snow, and wind loads.

The framing also needs sufficient stiffness. Excessive movement or deflection can damage glazing, seals, finishes, or connections.

This is why a large custom solarium should be designed as an engineered structure rather than treated as an oversized collection of windows.

## Choosing the Right Windows
Windows are arguably the most important component of a sunroom's thermal performance.

Two important specifications are:

**U-factor:** Measures how readily heat passes through the window. A lower U-factor generally means better insulating performance.

**Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC):** Measures how much solar radiation enters through the glazing. Lower SHGC values reduce solar heat gain, while higher values allow more solar energy into the space.

ENERGY STAR explains that the appropriate balance depends on climate. In colder climates, lower U-factors help reduce heat loss, while higher SHGC glazing can allow useful solar heat gain. In warmer climates, lower SHGC can help reduce unwanted heat entering the home.

For a sunroom, therefore, simply choosing the largest windows available is not necessarily the best strategy.

## Orientation Matters
The direction the sunroom faces can significantly affect comfort.

A south-facing room may receive substantial sunlight and passive solar heat, particularly in colder climates. West-facing glass can be especially challenging in hot weather because it receives strong afternoon sunlight.

ENERGY STAR recommends considering window orientation, shading, roof overhangs, and SHGC when designing additions. In colder climates, appropriately selected south-facing glazing can capture useful solar heat, while excessive west-facing glass can increase overheating.

External shading can include:

  - Roof overhangs
  - Exterior shades
  - Awnings
  - Pergolas
  - Deciduous landscaping

Interior blinds can also reduce glare and solar gain, although exterior shading generally addresses solar heat before it enters the building.

## Heating a Four-Season Sunroom
A four-season sunroom needs a deliberate heating and cooling strategy.

Angi's 2026 estimates put heating and air-conditioning installation for sunrooms at approximately **$2,300 to $20,500**, depending on the system and project. A ductless mini-split is generally less invasive than extending a home's existing ductwork, while larger HVAC modifications can cost substantially more.

Common options include:

### Ductless Mini-Split
A mini-split can provide both heating and cooling without requiring extensive new ductwork.

It can be particularly useful when the existing HVAC system does not have sufficient capacity or when routing ducts to the addition would be difficult.

### Extended Central HVAC
Connecting the sunroom to the home's existing ductwork can provide integrated temperature control.

However, the existing HVAC system needs to have adequate capacity. Simply adding another supply register does not guarantee that the system can efficiently heat and cool the additional space.

### Electric Baseboard or Other Supplemental Heat
Supplemental heating can provide relatively simple installation, but operating costs depend on electricity rates, insulation, climate, and how frequently the room is heated.

### Radiant Floor Heating
Radiant heating can be attractive in a tile-floored sunroom, especially in colder climates. It should be considered during initial floor and foundation design because retrofitting it later can be considerably more disruptive.

## Don't Rely on the Sun to Heat the Room
A common misconception is that extensive glass automatically makes a sunroom energy efficient because sunlight warms the room.

Solar gain can certainly provide useful heat, but it is inconsistent.

At night, the windows can become significant pathways for heat loss. During sunny summer afternoons, the same glazing that provides winter warmth can produce excessive indoor temperatures.

This is why the design needs to balance:

**Solar gain + insulation + air sealing + shading + mechanical heating/cooling.**

ENERGY STAR notes that certified windows can reduce heating and cooling costs compared with non-certified products, while window performance should be selected according to climate and orientation.

## Insulation Is Still Important
A four-season sunroom should not be treated as a lightly insulated porch.

Insulation may be required in:

  - Exterior walls
  - Roof or ceiling
  - Foundation or floor
  - Connections between the addition and existing house

Energy-code provisions can impose specific insulation and fenestration requirements on conditioned sunrooms. For example, current residential-code provisions include separate requirements for sunroom insulation and glazing depending on climate zone and whether the space is thermally isolated.

Air sealing is equally important. Gaps around windows, doors, framing connections, and utility penetrations can undermine otherwise high-performance insulation.

## Moisture and Condensation Control
Large amounts of glass create another potential problem: condensation.

Condensation occurs when a surface becomes cold enough for moisture in indoor air to reach its dew point.

A poorly designed sunroom can therefore experience moisture around:

  - Window frames
  - Glass roof panels
  - Exterior doors
  - Wall-to-roof connections
  - Floor-to-wall transitions

Proper flashing, drainage, sealants, ventilation, and window selection should be addressed during design.

Water management is especially important for glass roofs because even small failures around seams or flashing can allow water into the structure.

## Permits and Professional Design
A sunroom addition typically involves more than selecting a kit.

Angi's 2026 data estimates sunroom permits at approximately **$250 to $1,500**, although local requirements vary. Permit processing can take several weeks depending on the jurisdiction and project.

A professional project may involve:

  - Architect or designer
  - Structural engineer
  - General contractor
  - Electrician
  - HVAC contractor
  - Window/glazing installer
  - Roofing professional

The complexity increases substantially for a custom solarium with a glass roof, unusual geometry, large spans, or a foundation that differs from the existing house.

## Three-Season vs. Four-Season: Which Is More Practical?
A **three-season room** can make sense when the homeowner primarily wants a sheltered outdoor area for use during mild weather.

A **four-season room** is more appropriate when the objective is year-round usable living space.

The cost difference can be significant because four-season construction requires substantially more insulation, thermal performance, mechanical systems, and building-envelope detailing. Angi estimates three-season projects at approximately $10,000 to $40,000 compared with $25,000 to $120,000 for four-season designs.

For homeowners in a cold climate, spending more on insulation and high-performance glazing may make the four-season design more practical. In a mild climate, a three-season enclosure may satisfy the intended use without the cost of full conditioning.

## Final Considerations
Sunrooms and solariums can create attractive additional living space, but the large amount of glazing makes them more technically demanding than a conventional enclosed room.

A successful project begins with the **foundation and structural design**, followed by decisions about glazing, insulation, shading, heating, cooling, drainage, and ventilation.

For 2026, a typical sunroom addition costs roughly **$22,000 to $75,000**, while four-season and highly customized glass structures can reach or exceed $100,000.

The most important budgeting mistake to avoid is treating windows as the entire project. Foundation work, structural engineering, insulation, HVAC, electrical systems, waterproofing, permits, and interior finishes can represent a significant portion of the final price.

For a four-season addition, the goal should not simply be to maximize glass. The goal is to create a **well-engineered building envelope that controls solar gain, heat loss, moisture, and indoor temperature throughout the year**.
