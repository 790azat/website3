A reverse mortgage can allow older homeowners to access part of their home equity without selling the property or making traditional monthly mortgage payments. For eligible borrowers, the proceeds can be used for living expenses, home improvements, debt repayment, or other financial needs.

However, a reverse mortgage is still a loan. Interest and fees are added to the balance over time, which can reduce the equity available to the homeowner or their heirs. Borrowers must also continue paying property taxes and homeowners insurance and maintain the property.

The most common reverse mortgage is the **Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM)**, which is insured by the Federal Housing Administration (FHA). HECMs are available to homeowners age 62 and older who meet additional requirements.

## What Is a Reverse Mortgage?
A reverse mortgage allows a homeowner to borrow against home equity while continuing to hold title to the property.

Unlike a traditional mortgage, where the borrower makes monthly principal and interest payments and gradually reduces the balance, a reverse mortgage generally does not require monthly mortgage payments as long as the borrower satisfies the loan requirements.

Instead, interest and certain fees are added to the outstanding balance. As the balance increases, the homeowner's equity generally decreases. The loan typically becomes due when the borrower sells the home, permanently moves out, or dies, subject to specific rules and protections.

A reverse mortgage therefore converts some home equity into available funds without requiring the homeowner to sell the property immediately.

## Who Is Eligible for a Reverse Mortgage?
For an FHA-insured HECM, the borrower must meet several requirements.

Generally, the borrower must:

  - Be at least 62 years old
  - Own the home outright or have sufficient equity to pay off an existing mortgage at closing
  - Occupy the property as a principal residence
  - Meet the lender's financial assessment requirements
  - Continue paying property taxes and homeowners insurance
  - Maintain the home according to required property standards
  - Complete counseling with a HUD-approved reverse mortgage counseling agency

Existing liens generally need to be paid off when the reverse mortgage closes. If the homeowner has an existing mortgage, proceeds from the reverse mortgage can potentially be used to pay it off.

The property must also meet applicable FHA requirements. If repairs are necessary, the lender may require them to be completed before or as part of the reverse mortgage transaction.

## How Much Can You Borrow?
The amount available through an HECM depends on several factors, including the borrower's age, the home's value, the applicable interest rate, and the FHA's maximum claim amount.

For 2026, the nationwide HECM maximum claim amount is **$1,249,125**. This is not necessarily the amount every borrower can receive. The actual principal limit is determined using the property's value, the borrower's age, and other program factors.

Older borrowers may generally qualify for a larger percentage of their home's value because the loan is expected to remain outstanding for a shorter period.

An existing mortgage can also substantially reduce the amount of reverse mortgage proceeds available because it generally must be paid off at closing.

## How Do You Receive Reverse Mortgage Funds?
Depending on the loan structure, borrowers may have several ways to receive their proceeds.

Options can include:

  - A lump-sum payment
  - Monthly payments
  - A line of credit
  - A combination of payment methods

The available options can depend on whether the HECM has a fixed or adjustable interest rate and the applicable program rules.

Borrowers should consider how quickly they actually need the money. Taking more money than necessary can increase the loan balance and therefore the amount of interest and fees that accumulate over time.

## Pros of a Reverse Mortgage
### Access home equity without selling immediately
A reverse mortgage can provide liquidity while allowing an eligible homeowner to remain in the property.

This can be useful for someone who has substantial home equity but limited retirement income or liquid savings.

### No traditional monthly mortgage payment
HECM borrowers generally do not make monthly mortgage payments toward principal and interest while they remain in compliance with the loan requirements.

This can reduce monthly cash-flow pressure, although the homeowner remains responsible for property taxes, homeowners insurance, maintenance, and other required property expenses.

### Continue owning the home
The lender does not automatically become the owner of the property when a reverse mortgage is taken out. The homeowner retains title while the home serves as collateral for the loan.

### Potential financial flexibility
Depending on the borrower's circumstances, reverse mortgage proceeds can provide funds for household expenses, medical costs, home repairs, or other needs.

However, borrowers should understand how using home equity affects their longer-term finances and estate plans.

## Cons of a Reverse Mortgage
### The loan balance grows
This is one of the most important differences from a traditional mortgage.

Because interest and certain fees are added to the balance, the amount owed can increase over time. As a result, the homeowner's remaining equity can decline.

### Closing and ongoing costs
Reverse mortgages can involve origination charges, appraisal and title expenses, mortgage insurance premiums, and other closing costs.

Ongoing costs can include interest, servicing charges, mortgage insurance premiums, property taxes, and homeowners insurance. Some costs may be added to the loan balance rather than paid entirely upfront.

### You still have housing obligations
A reverse mortgage does not eliminate the financial responsibilities of owning a home.

You generally must continue paying:

  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Required maintenance and repairs
  - Any applicable homeowners association charges

Failure to satisfy these obligations can create a default and potentially lead to foreclosure.

### Less home equity may remain for heirs
Because the loan balance can grow, the amount of equity remaining when the home is eventually sold may be lower.

This can be particularly important for homeowners who intend to leave the property to children or other heirs.

## What Happens When the Homeowner Dies?
A reverse mortgage generally becomes due after the death of the last surviving borrower and, depending on the circumstances, after the death of an eligible non-borrowing spouse.

Heirs can generally choose to sell the property and use the proceeds to repay the loan, or pay off the balance and keep the home.

For an HECM, if the loan balance is greater than the home's value, heirs generally can satisfy the debt by paying at least **95% of the home's appraised value** when selling under the applicable rules. FHA mortgage insurance covers the remaining amount owed to the lender.

This means heirs generally are not personally responsible for paying an unlimited shortfall from other assets.

Estate planning is still important, particularly when family members want to retain the home.

## What Happens If You Move Out?
A reverse mortgage is intended for a principal residence.

If the borrower permanently moves out, sells the home, or otherwise stops meeting the occupancy requirements, the loan can become due.

Temporary absences can be treated differently under the program rules. For example, certain extended stays in a healthcare facility can affect the loan's status.

Anyone considering a reverse mortgage should therefore think about possible future moves before taking out the loan.

## Reverse Mortgage Alternatives
A reverse mortgage is only one way to access home equity.

Depending on income, credit, age, and financial circumstances, alternatives may include:

### Home equity loan
A home equity loan provides a lump sum secured by the property and generally requires monthly payments.

### HELOC
A home equity line of credit allows the homeowner to borrow as needed up to an approved limit. It generally requires repayment and can have a variable interest rate.

### Cash-out refinance
A cash-out refinance replaces the existing mortgage with a larger loan and provides the difference in cash. The borrower generally makes monthly mortgage payments.

### Selling and downsizing
Selling the existing property and purchasing a less expensive home can unlock equity without creating a reverse mortgage balance.

The CFPB specifically recommends considering alternatives such as home equity loans, HELOCs, refinancing, downsizing, and reducing expenses before taking out a reverse mortgage.

## Questions to Ask Before Applying
Before signing a reverse mortgage agreement, ask:

1.  How much can I receive based on my age and home value?
2.  How much will I owe after five, 10, or 15 years under different scenarios?
3.  What are the upfront closing costs?
4.  Which costs will be added to the loan balance?
5.  Is the interest rate fixed or adjustable?
6.  What happens if I move into assisted living or a nursing facility?
7.  What happens to my spouse if they are not a co-borrower?
8.  What options will my heirs have after my death?
9.  What property expenses must I continue paying?
10. How does the reverse mortgage compare with a HELOC, home equity loan, refinance, or downsizing?

HECM borrowers are required to complete counseling with a HUD-approved counseling agency. The counseling process is designed to address the financial implications of the loan and available alternatives before the mortgage is completed.

## Is a Reverse Mortgage Right for You?
A reverse mortgage can be a way for an older homeowner to access home equity without selling the property or making traditional monthly mortgage payments. But the tradeoff is that interest and fees can cause the loan balance to grow, potentially reducing the equity available later.

Eligibility depends on more than age. Homeownership, occupancy, existing mortgage debt, property condition, financial resources, and required counseling all matter.

Before proceeding, compare the reverse mortgage with other ways to access cash and calculate how the loan balance could affect your future finances and estate. Understanding the costs, repayment conditions, property obligations, and options for heirs can help you evaluate the loan based on your own retirement and housing plans.
