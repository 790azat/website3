Getting a mortgage when you are self-employed can require more documentation than applying as a traditional W-2 employee. Instead of relying primarily on recent pay stubs and W-2 forms, lenders may examine tax returns, business financial information, bank statements, ownership interests, and the stability of your income.

For many self-employed borrowers, the biggest issue is not how much money the business generates, but **how much qualifying income the lender can document**.

There are two common approaches to self-employed mortgage financing: conventional mortgages that analyze income using tax returns and other documentation, and **bank statement mortgage loans**, which are generally alternative or non-QM products that may use deposits shown in personal or business bank accounts to evaluate income.

Understanding the difference can help you prepare your application and determine which mortgage structure may fit your financial situation.

## Who Is Considered Self-Employed?
Mortgage underwriting rules can define self-employment differently from everyday usage.

Under Fannie Mae's conventional underwriting guidance, a borrower with a **25% or greater ownership interest in a business** is considered self-employed. The lender evaluates factors including the stability of the borrower's income, the nature of the business, its financial strength, and its ability to continue generating income.

This can include:

  - Sole proprietors
  - Independent contractors
  - Business owners
  - Members of LLCs
  - Partners in partnerships
  - S corporation owners
  - Certain corporation owners
  - Freelancers and consultants

A borrower can also have both self-employment and traditional employment income. In that situation, the lender may analyze each income source according to the applicable underwriting requirements.

## How Mortgage Lenders Calculate Self-Employed Income
One of the biggest differences between self-employed and W-2 borrowers is that **business revenue is not necessarily the same as qualifying income**.

A business might generate $200,000 in annual revenue, for example, but the owner's taxable income could be substantially lower after business expenses, depreciation, deductions, and other adjustments.

Conventional underwriting generally focuses on the income that can reasonably be relied upon to make the mortgage payments rather than simply using gross business revenue. Fannie Mae requires lenders to analyze the stability and continuance of self-employment income.

This is why a borrower with significant business revenue can sometimes qualify for a smaller mortgage than expected.

## Tax Returns for a Self-Employed Mortgage
Tax returns are among the most important documents for conventional self-employed mortgage underwriting.

Depending on the borrower's circumstances, the lender may request:

  - Personal federal tax returns
  - Business tax returns
  - Schedule C
  - Schedule E
  - Schedule K-1
  - Partnership returns
  - S corporation returns
  - Corporate returns
  - IRS tax transcripts
  - Year-to-date business financial information

Fannie Mae generally requires lenders to obtain two years of signed personal and, when applicable, business federal tax returns for self-employed borrowers. There are circumstances in which one year of returns may be sufficient, including certain borrowers whose businesses have been established for at least five years and who meet additional ownership and income requirements.

The exact documentation depends on the mortgage program and lender.

## Why Tax Deductions Can Affect Your Mortgage
Business deductions can reduce taxable income, which may be beneficial for tax purposes but can make mortgage qualification more complicated.

Suppose a business generates:

**$180,000 revenue**

and reports:

**$70,000 in deductible business expenses**

The resulting taxable business income may be significantly below the original revenue figure.

Mortgage underwriting can make certain adjustments to reported income depending on the type of expense and applicable guidelines. The lender's cash-flow analysis is therefore more complicated than simply taking the bottom-line number from a tax return.

Fannie Mae's guidance specifically calls for analysis of business income and expenses to determine the amount of stable income available to the borrower.

This is one reason self-employed borrowers should discuss their situation with lenders before assuming that business revenue will translate directly into mortgage qualification.

## How Long Do You Need to Be Self-Employed?
A longer business history can make income verification easier.

Fannie Mae generally looks for a two-year history of prior earnings when evaluating self-employment income. However, borrowers with less than two years of self-employment may qualify in certain circumstances when they have a full year of self-employment income documented on signed tax returns and qualifying prior experience in the same or a related field.

That means recently becoming self-employed does not necessarily make a mortgage impossible.

The lender may look at:

  - Previous employment history
  - Industry experience
  - Business history
  - Current income
  - Tax returns
  - Business liabilities
  - Income stability
  - Likelihood that the business will continue operating

## What Is a Bank Statement Mortgage?
A bank statement mortgage is an alternative mortgage product that can use deposits shown on bank statements as part of the income evaluation rather than relying exclusively on traditional tax-return income.

These loans are commonly marketed toward borrowers whose tax returns do not fully reflect the cash flow available to them because of substantial business deductions.

Potential borrowers can include:

  - Business owners
  - Contractors
  - Freelancers
  - Consultants
  - Real estate professionals
  - Commission-based entrepreneurs
  - Other borrowers with significant documented deposits

Unlike conventional mortgages sold to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, bank statement loans are generally offered through lenders specializing in alternative documentation or non-QM mortgage products.

Because there is no single universal bank statement underwriting standard, requirements can differ substantially between lenders.

## How Bank Statement Loans Work
Instead of relying primarily on taxable income, a lender may review a specified period of personal or business bank statements and calculate qualifying income from eligible deposits.

For example, a lender might examine 12 or 24 months of statements and identify recurring business deposits.

However, **not every deposit necessarily counts as income**.

Transfers between accounts, borrowed money, refunds, certain one-time deposits, and other transactions may need to be excluded or explained.

The lender may also apply an expense factor to business deposits to estimate the portion that represents usable income.

For example, if a business receives $20,000 in monthly deposits, a lender might not treat the entire $20,000 as personal qualifying income. The lender's specific calculation method and expense assumptions determine the amount used for underwriting.

## Personal vs. Business Bank Statements
One important distinction is whether the lender allows personal statements, business statements, or both.

Business bank statements can provide a clearer record of commercial revenue, while personal statements may show deposits after business expenses have already been paid.

Lenders can also require additional documentation to establish that deposits are genuinely related to the borrower's business.

Before applying, ask:

  - How many months of statements are required?
  - Are personal or business accounts acceptable?
  - What deposits are excluded?
  - How are business expenses calculated?
  - Are transfers between accounts excluded?
  - Is a CPA letter required?
  - Are tax returns still required?
  - What credit score is required?

These details can materially affect the qualifying income calculation.

## Bank Statement Loans vs. Conventional Mortgages
The two approaches serve different borrower profiles.

| Feature | Conventional Mortgage | Bank Statement Mortgage |
|---|---|---|
| Primary income documentation | Tax returns and other documentation | Bank statements and lender-specific documentation |
| Taxable deductions | Can reduce qualifying income | May have less direct impact |
| Underwriting standards | Standardized agency guidelines may apply | Varies by lender |
| Loan type | Often conforming | Generally alternative/non-QM |
| Rates and fees | Vary by borrower and market | May be higher depending on risk |
| Documentation | Detailed income analysis | Detailed deposit analysis |
| Availability | Broad lender availability | More specialized |

A bank statement mortgage is not automatically cheaper simply because it avoids traditional tax-return underwriting. Compare the interest rate, APR, points, closing costs, prepayment terms, reserve requirements, and other loan conditions.

## Documents Self-Employed Borrowers Should Prepare
Even if you are considering a bank statement loan, organizing your finances before applying can make the process easier.

Potential documents include:

  - Personal identification
  - Recent bank statements
  - Business bank statements
  - Personal tax returns
  - Business tax returns
  - 1099 forms
  - K-1 statements
  - Business license
  - Articles of incorporation or organization
  - CPA or accountant documentation
  - Profit-and-loss statements
  - Business debt information
  - Documentation for the down payment
  - Evidence supporting large deposits

The CFPB notes that self-employed and borrowers with irregular income may need more extensive income documentation, and lenders can request additional information depending on the borrower's circumstances.

## Large Deposits and Down Payment Funds
Self-employed borrowers should pay particular attention to unusual deposits.

Mortgage lenders generally need to verify the source of income and funds being used for the purchase. If a large deposit appears in a bank account, the borrower may need documentation explaining where the money came from.

This can become important when preparing a down payment shortly before applying.

Instead of moving large amounts of money without documentation, keep records showing the source and transfer history.

## How to Prepare for a Self-Employed Mortgage
Start several months before you expect to apply.

Keep business and personal finances organized, maintain consistent banking records, and avoid unexplained transfers whenever possible.

You should also:

1.  Review your credit reports.
2.  Calculate your current debt-to-income ratio.
3.  Estimate your qualifying income under conventional guidelines.
4.  Gather recent tax returns and bank statements.
5.  Document your down payment funds.
6.  Identify whether your business ownership exceeds 25%.
7.  Ask lenders how they treat business expenses and deductions.
8.  Compare conventional and bank statement mortgage options.

The CFPB recommends gathering financial documents in advance and requesting Loan Estimates from multiple lenders when shopping for a mortgage.

## Get Preapproved Before Choosing a Mortgage
A preapproval can reveal how a lender actually treats your self-employed income rather than relying on an online mortgage calculator.

When speaking with lenders, explain how your business operates and provide accurate information about ownership, income, expenses, and tax filings.

If you are considering a bank statement mortgage, ask for a written explanation of how qualifying income will be calculated.

Once you receive Loan Estimates, compare the interest rate, APR, loan amount, monthly payment, closing costs, and other terms. The CFPB notes that a Loan Estimate can be requested after providing six basic pieces of information, and borrowers can compare offers from multiple lenders.

## Final Considerations
Self-employed borrowers have multiple paths to mortgage financing, but income documentation is usually the central issue.

A conventional mortgage may work well when tax returns clearly demonstrate stable qualifying income. A bank statement mortgage may provide an alternative when business cash flow is strong but taxable income is reduced by legitimate deductions.

Neither approach should be evaluated solely on whether it produces a larger loan amount.

Compare the **interest rate, APR, fees, down payment, reserves, documentation requirements, prepayment terms, and total borrowing cost**. Most importantly, make sure the mortgage payment remains affordable after accounting for business expenses, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and the possibility that business income can fluctuate.

Preparing your financial records before applying can make it easier to compare mortgage options and give lenders the information they need to evaluate your application accurately.
