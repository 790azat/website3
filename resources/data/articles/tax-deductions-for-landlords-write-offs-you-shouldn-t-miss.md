Rental property can generate attractive income, but landlords also face a long list of expenses. Understanding which costs are deductible—and which must be capitalized and depreciated—can materially affect the property's taxable income.

The IRS generally allows landlords to deduct ordinary and necessary expenses associated with managing, maintaining, and conserving rental property. Common deductible expenses include mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, advertising, repairs, maintenance, utilities, and professional fees.

However, not every property expense is an immediate write-off. Major improvements generally must be capitalized and recovered through depreciation.

Here are some of the deductions landlords should review with their tax professional.

## 1. Mortgage Interest
Mortgage interest is one of the most significant potential rental-property deductions.

If a loan is used for an income-producing rental property, the interest portion of qualifying mortgage payments can generally be deducted as a rental expense.

The important distinction is **interest versus principal**.

For example, if a monthly mortgage payment is $2,400 and the payment consists of:

  - $1,800 principal
  - $600 interest

the $600 interest component may generally be deductible as a rental expense, while the $1,800 principal payment is not an immediate rental expense.

The IRS specifically notes that landlords cannot simply deduct mortgage principal payments as an expense.

Keep the lender's annual mortgage-interest statement and supporting loan records with the property's tax documentation.

## 2. Property Taxes
Real estate taxes associated with rental property can generally be deducted as a rental expense.

This can include recurring property taxes imposed by state or local governments.

However, landlords should distinguish ordinary property taxes from amounts that must instead be capitalized as part of the property's basis.

Maintain copies of property-tax bills and payment records so the deductible amounts can be reconciled with the property's accounting records.

## 3. Property Insurance
Insurance premiums can be another important rental-property deduction.

Depending on the property and policy, deductible insurance expenses may include coverage for:

  - Fire
  - Liability
  - Rental property
  - Certain casualty risks
  - Other qualifying property risks

The IRS lists insurance among the ordinary and necessary expenses that may be deductible for rental property.

For landlords with multiple properties, track premiums by property rather than combining all policies into a single expense category.

## 4. Repairs and Maintenance
Routine repairs can generally be deductible when they keep the property in ordinarily efficient operating condition rather than materially improving it.

Examples can include:

  - Fixing a leaking faucet
  - Repairing a damaged door
  - Patching drywall
  - Replacing a broken appliance component
  - Repairing plumbing
  - Routine HVAC work
  - Touch-up painting

The distinction becomes important when a project crosses into an improvement, restoration, or adaptation.

The IRS explains that costs for improvements generally cannot simply be deducted as current expenses; qualifying improvements are recovered through depreciation.

For example, repairing part of a roof is different from replacing an entire roof.

## 5. Depreciation
Depreciation is one of the most valuable—and frequently misunderstood—tax deductions available to rental-property owners.

Instead of deducting the entire cost of a qualifying building immediately, the owner generally recovers the property's depreciable basis over a prescribed recovery period.

For residential rental property under the general depreciation system, the IRS specifies a **27.5-year recovery period**, using the straight-line method and mid-month convention.

Land itself generally cannot be depreciated because it does not have a determinable useful life for this purpose.

For example, if a property's depreciable building basis were $275,000, a simplified annual depreciation calculation would be approximately:

**$275,000 ÷ 27.5 = $10,000 per year**

The actual deduction can depend on the property's placed-in-service date, basis calculations, conventions, and other tax rules.

## 6. Appliances, Furniture, and Equipment
Landlords may also have depreciable property separate from the building.

Examples include:

  - Refrigerators
  - Stoves
  - Washers and dryers
  - Furniture in furnished rentals
  - Certain equipment
  - Computers and office equipment

The IRS provides different recovery periods for different categories. For example, appliances and furniture used in rental activities are generally listed as five-year property under GDS, while office furniture and equipment are generally seven-year property.

The exact treatment depends on the asset and circumstances, so retain purchase invoices and placed-in-service dates.

## 7. Advertising and Marketing
Costs incurred to advertise a rental property can generally qualify as deductible rental expenses.

Examples include:

  - Rental listing fees
  - Online advertising
  - Property photographs
  - Signage
  - Promotional materials
  - Certain listing services

The IRS identifies advertising among the ordinary and necessary expenses that may be deductible for rental property.

Keep invoices and receipts, particularly when using multiple advertising platforms.

## 8. Property Management Fees
Professional property-management fees are another expense landlords should track.

These may include payments for:

  - Tenant placement
  - Rent collection
  - Property inspections
  - Maintenance coordination
  - Tenant communication
  - Lease administration
  - Ongoing management

If a property manager handles several properties, obtain statements that clearly identify which charges relate to each property.

This makes it easier to reconcile management expenses against rental income.

## 9. Legal and Professional Fees
Landlords may incur professional fees related to operating their rental businesses.

Potentially deductible expenses can include qualifying fees for:

  - Attorneys
  - Accountants
  - Tax professionals
  - Property-management consultants
  - Certain bookkeeping services

The key is the purpose of the expense. Personal legal or accounting expenses generally cannot simply be classified as rental expenses because the person also owns rental property.

Maintain invoices showing what professional service was actually provided.

## 10. Utilities Paid by the Landlord
If the landlord is responsible for utilities under the lease, qualifying utility expenses can generally be deducted.

Examples include:

  - Electricity
  - Natural gas
  - Water
  - Sewer
  - Trash collection

The IRS specifically includes utilities among expenses that can potentially reduce taxable rental income.

For multi-unit properties, allocate shared utility expenses using a reasonable and consistently applied method.

## 11. Cleaning and Turnover Costs
Landlords can also encounter cleaning and turnover expenses, particularly with furnished or short-term rental properties.

Potential examples include:

  - Move-out cleaning
  - Common-area cleaning
  - Carpet cleaning
  - Certain landscaping services
  - Turnover labor
  - Linen services for qualifying short-term rentals

The expense should be connected to the rental activity and properly documented.

Short-term and vacation rentals can have additional rules concerning personal use and allocation of expenses, so owners should not automatically apply the same calculation used for a purely rental property.

## 12. Travel and Local Transportation
Travel expenses connected with managing rental property can sometimes qualify for tax treatment, but this category requires particular care.

A trip may involve a mixture of rental-related and personal activities. The landlord therefore needs records showing:

  - Date
  - Destination
  - Business purpose
  - Mileage or transportation cost
  - Property visited
  - Supporting receipts

Do not assume that every trip to a rental property is automatically deductible.

When personal and rental activities are combined, the allocation of expenses becomes especially important.

## 13. Office and Administrative Expenses
Landlords can incur smaller operating expenses that are easy to overlook.

Depending on the circumstances, these can include qualifying costs associated with:

  - Office supplies
  - Postage
  - Printing
  - Property-management software
  - Tenant communication systems
  - Bank fees
  - Certain professional subscriptions

The IRS standard is generally whether an expense is ordinary and necessary for the rental activity.

Keep recurring software and subscription charges categorized rather than allowing them to disappear into a general credit-card expense.

## 14. Don't Confuse Repairs With Improvements
This is one of the most important distinctions in rental-property taxation.

A **repair** generally maintains the property.

An **improvement** generally involves a betterment, restoration, or adaptation to a new or different use and generally must be capitalized.

The IRS gives the example of replacing a furnace in a residential rental property: a major furnace replacement is generally treated as a capital improvement and depreciated rather than deducted entirely in the year of payment.

Similarly, replacing an entire roof, windows, or other major building components can result in capitalization and depreciation.

This distinction can significantly change the timing of deductions.

## 15. Track Capital Improvements Separately
Capital expenditures are not necessarily "lost" from a tax perspective. Instead, qualifying costs generally become part of the property's depreciable basis or are treated as separate depreciable assets.

Examples include:

  - New roof
  - Major HVAC replacement
  - Building addition
  - Major renovation
  - Significant electrical upgrade
  - New windows
  - Major structural work

The IRS explains that additions and improvements to residential rental property generally receive the same 27.5-year recovery period applicable to residential rental property under GDS.

Keep invoices, contracts, permits, and completion dates for every major project.

## 16. Don't Forget Casualty Losses
Rental properties can experience damage from events such as fires, storms, or other qualifying casualties.

The tax treatment depends on the circumstances, insurance reimbursement, and applicable rules. The IRS Publication 527 specifically addresses casualty and theft losses involving rental property.

Do not automatically deduct the full repair invoice as a casualty loss. Insurance payments, adjusted basis, the nature of the damage, and other factors can affect the calculation.

## 17. Keep Excellent Records
A deduction is much easier to support when the underlying documentation is organized.

Maintain:

**Income records**

  - Rent received
  - Application or other rental-related fees
  - Security deposits when they become income under applicable rules
  - Other rental income

**Expense records**

  - Mortgage interest
  - Property taxes
  - Insurance
  - Repairs
  - Utilities
  - Management
  - Advertising
  - Professional fees
  - Supplies

**Capital records**

  - Purchase documents
  - Closing statements
  - Improvement invoices
  - Major replacement costs
  - Asset placed-in-service dates

The IRS emphasizes maintaining records that substantiate rental income and expenses.

### Final Considerations
The biggest landlord tax mistake is often not failing to find an obscure deduction. It is **misclassifying an expense**.

A routine repair may potentially be deductible immediately, while a major improvement generally must be capitalized and recovered through depreciation. Mortgage principal is not the same as mortgage interest, and a security deposit is not automatically rental income simply because money was received.

For most individual landlords, rental real estate income and expenses are generally reported on **Schedule E**, although the appropriate tax treatment can change depending on the nature of the rental activity and services provided.

Landlords should also be aware that rental losses can be limited by passive-activity and at-risk rules, meaning that having deductible expenses does not necessarily mean every dollar of those expenses can immediately reduce other taxable income.

Because depreciation, personal use, passive-loss limitations, entity structure, and state taxes can materially change the result, landlords with significant portfolios should have their records and tax treatment reviewed by a qualified tax professional.
