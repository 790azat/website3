Buying a new home before selling your current property can create a financing gap. You may have substantial equity in your existing home, but that equity is not necessarily available as cash when you need to make a down payment on the next property.

A **bridge loan** is designed to help solve that timing problem. It is a short-term financing arrangement that can provide access to equity in an existing property while the homeowner completes the purchase of another home.

The basic idea is straightforward: borrow against the existing home, use the proceeds to help purchase the new home, then repay the bridge loan when the existing property sells. Federal mortgage rules specifically recognize a temporary bridge loan as financing used to purchase a new dwelling when the borrower expects to sell the current dwelling within a limited period.

Because bridge financing can involve additional interest, fees, and the possibility of carrying two homes at once, understanding the structure is important before using it.

## What Is a Bridge Loan?
A bridge loan is short-term financing intended to cover the period between two real estate transactions.

A typical situation looks like this:

1.  You own a home worth $500,000.
2.  Your existing mortgage balance is $250,000.
3.  You find a new home listed for $650,000.
4.  You want to buy the new home before your existing property sells.
5.  A lender provides temporary financing secured by your existing home, your new home, or both, depending on the loan structure.
6.  You purchase the new property.
7.  Your existing home is sold.
8.  Sale proceeds are used to repay the bridge loan and potentially other debts.
9.  You continue with the long-term mortgage on the new home.

The purpose is not normally to provide permanent financing. Instead, it creates liquidity while the homeowner transitions from one property to another.

CFPB regulations specifically describe a temporary bridge loan as an example of financing used to purchase a new dwelling when the borrower plans to sell the current dwelling within 12 months.

## Why Homebuyers Use Bridge Loans
The most common reason is timing.

Suppose your current home is worth $600,000 and you owe $280,000 on the mortgage. You may have substantial equity, but you cannot necessarily access the full amount before completing the sale.

At the same time, you find a $700,000 home that you want to purchase.

Without additional financing, you might need to:

  - Sell your current home first
  - Rent temporarily
  - Move twice
  - Store furniture and belongings
  - Delay making an offer on the new property
  - Make the purchase contingent on selling your current home

A bridge loan can potentially provide funds during this transition.

It can be particularly relevant in situations where a seller wants a non-contingent offer or where the buyer needs to close on the new home before the old home has sold.

## How Bridge Financing Works
Bridge loans are commonly structured around the equity in the existing property.

The lender evaluates factors such as:

  - Current property value
  - Existing mortgage balance
  - Available equity
  - New home's purchase price
  - Borrower's income
  - Credit profile
  - Existing monthly obligations
  - Expected sale of the current home
  - Loan-to-value requirements

The amount available depends on the lender and structure.

For example, assume:

**Existing home value:** $600,000
**Current mortgage:** $280,000
**Estimated equity:** $320,000

A lender will not necessarily allow you to borrow the entire $320,000. It may impose a maximum combined loan-to-value ratio, reserve requirement, or other underwriting restrictions.

If the lender allows sufficient borrowing against the property, some of the proceeds could be used toward the purchase of the replacement home.

## Bridge Loan Example
Consider a homeowner purchasing a $750,000 property.

The buyer has $150,000 available for a down payment but wants to preserve cash for closing costs, moving expenses, and repairs.

The existing home is worth $600,000 with a $250,000 mortgage balance.

If a bridge lender approves $150,000 of temporary financing, the buyer could potentially use those funds as part of the purchase financing for the new property.

After the existing home sells for $600,000, the sale proceeds would first be used to satisfy the existing mortgage and transaction costs. The remaining proceeds could then be used to repay the bridge loan.

The actual amount available would depend on the lender's valuation, maximum LTV, fees, and underwriting requirements.

## Do You Need to Make Two Mortgage Payments?
Potentially, yes.

This is one of the most important financial considerations.

Until the existing property sells, you could have:

  - The mortgage on your current home
  - The bridge loan payment
  - The mortgage on your new home
  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Maintenance expenses
  - Utilities and other ownership costs

Some bridge loans may be structured with interest-only payments or other temporary payment arrangements, but the specific terms depend on the lender.

The lender will also evaluate whether you can handle the financial obligations associated with the transaction.

A borrower should calculate the **maximum monthly carrying cost**, rather than assuming the old home will sell immediately.

## What Happens When the Existing Home Sells?
The sale of the old property is usually the event that allows the temporary financing to be repaid.

At closing, proceeds from the sale can be allocated toward:

1.  Paying off the existing mortgage
2.  Paying selling expenses
3.  Repaying the bridge loan
4.  Paying other liens or transaction-related obligations
5.  Delivering remaining proceeds to the homeowner

The exact order depends on the transaction and loan documents.

CFPB guidance describes bridge financing as temporary financing that can be paid off using proceeds from the sale of an existing home.

## Bridge Loans and Home Equity
Bridge financing is closely connected to home equity, but it is not the same thing as simply withdrawing equity.

A homeowner might have $300,000 of equity but still have limited access to that equity because lenders consider:

  - Property value
  - Existing liens
  - Maximum LTV
  - Income
  - Credit
  - Existing debt
  - Expected sale price
  - Loan term

The lender is primarily concerned with whether the temporary loan can be repaid and whether sufficient collateral supports the financing.

## Bridge Loan vs. HELOC
A **HELOC**, or home equity line of credit, is another way homeowners can access equity in an existing property.

A HELOC generally provides a revolving line of credit secured by the home. A bridge loan, by contrast, is specifically structured around the transition between properties.

A HELOC may make sense when you need flexible access to equity for renovations or other expenses.

A bridge loan can be more directly suited to a short window between buying a replacement home and selling the current home.

The costs, interest rates, repayment requirements, and availability can differ significantly.

CFPB describes HELOCs as a type of second mortgage secured by the home.

## Bridge Loan vs. Cash-Out Refinance
A cash-out refinance replaces an existing mortgage with a larger mortgage and provides the borrower with the difference in cash.

That can provide access to home equity, but refinancing an existing property can involve a longer-term loan structure and closing costs.

A bridge loan is generally intended to be temporary.

If you expect to sell the current home soon, taking out long-term financing against that property may not have the same purpose as temporary bridge financing.

## Bridge Loan vs. Home Equity Loan
A home equity loan provides a lump sum secured by the existing property.

Like a HELOC, it is generally considered a second mortgage when another mortgage remains on the home.

A bridge loan is more specifically connected to the transition between an existing residence and a replacement property.

The right structure depends on how quickly the current home is expected to sell, how much equity is available, and what the lender permits.

## Bridge Loan Costs
Bridge financing can cost more than a conventional long-term mortgage because it is temporary and can involve additional underwriting and collateral considerations.

Potential costs include:

  - Interest
  - Origination fees
  - Lender fees
  - Appraisal costs
  - Title and settlement charges
  - Closing costs
  - Other financing expenses

Historical CFPB regulatory materials describe bridge loans as potentially carrying higher rates, points, or fees than conventional mortgages, although actual pricing varies by lender and borrower.

Rather than looking only at the interest rate, calculate the **total dollar cost of the bridge financing** over the expected holding period.

## What If Your Home Does Not Sell Quickly?
This is one of the major risks.

A bridge loan depends on the homeowner's ability to transition from the existing property to the new one.

If the old property takes longer than expected to sell, you could face:

  - Additional interest charges
  - Extended bridge financing
  - Multiple housing payments
  - Reduced cash reserves
  - Pressure to reduce the asking price
  - Potential refinancing challenges

For this reason, homeowners should evaluate the existing home's realistic market value rather than assuming the highest possible listing price will become the final sale price.

The CFPB also recommends considering affordability and updating mortgage, down-payment, and closing-cost calculations as circumstances change during the home-buying process.

## Bridge Loan Qualification Requirements
Lenders can establish their own requirements, but common considerations include:

### Sufficient Home Equity
The existing property generally needs enough equity to support the requested financing.

### Strong Credit
A strong credit profile can improve the range of financing options available, although minimum requirements vary by lender.

### Stable Income
The lender may evaluate employment, income, and recurring obligations.

### Manageable Debt
The lender may need to determine whether you can handle the temporary increase in debt obligations.

### Saleability of the Existing Home
Some lenders may consider the condition, location, marketability, or listing status of the existing property.

### Cash Reserves
Because the sale can take longer than expected, a lender may require or favor borrowers with sufficient liquid reserves.

## Documents You May Need
A bridge-loan application can require documentation similar to other forms of mortgage financing.

Potential documents include:

  - Identification
  - Income documentation
  - Bank statements
  - Tax returns
  - Existing mortgage statement
  - Property insurance information
  - Property valuation
  - Purchase contract for the new home
  - Listing agreement for the existing home
  - Expected sale information
  - Details of other debts

The lender may also order an appraisal or other valuation of the property securing the financing.

## Can You Buy Before Selling Without a Bridge Loan?
Yes.

A bridge loan is only one strategy.

Depending on your financial situation, alternatives can include:

  - Making the purchase contingent on selling your current home
  - Selling the existing property first
  - Using available cash reserves
  - Using a HELOC
  - Using a home equity loan
  - Making a larger down payment after the sale
  - Negotiating a longer closing period
  - Using a rent-back agreement with the buyer of the existing home

Each option changes the timing, liquidity, and risk involved in the transaction.

## Questions to Ask a Bridge Loan Lender
Before accepting an offer, ask:

1.  What is the maximum loan amount?
2.  Which property secures the bridge loan?
3.  What is the interest rate?
4.  Is the rate fixed or variable?
5.  Are payments interest-only?
6.  How long is the loan term?
7.  Is there a prepayment penalty?
8.  What happens if my home does not sell within the loan term?
9.  Can the loan be extended?
10. What fees are charged?
11. What LTV or combined LTV limit applies?
12. How will the bridge loan affect qualification for my new mortgage?
13. What happens to the loan when my existing property sells?

These questions can reveal the actual cost and potential risks of the financing.

## Understanding the Closing Process
Buying and selling simultaneously means coordinating two separate real estate transactions.

The purchase of the new home typically involves mortgage underwriting, title work, insurance, closing documents, and the transfer of funds. CFPB explains that at a mortgage closing, the lender's funds are transferred through the settlement process and ownership is transferred once the transaction is finalized.

When two transactions occur close together, timing becomes especially important.

A delay in the sale of the old property does not necessarily stop the purchase of the new property if bridge financing is already in place, but it can increase the period during which the borrower carries multiple obligations.

## Is a Bridge Loan Right for Your Situation?
Bridge financing can be useful when a homeowner has substantial equity but needs access to that equity before selling the existing property.

It can help create flexibility when buying and selling transactions do not line up perfectly.

However, it is not simply a way to make a larger purchase. The borrower needs to consider the cost of temporary financing, the possibility of carrying two properties, the expected sale timeline, and what happens if the existing home sells for less than expected or takes longer to sell.

A bridge loan should therefore be evaluated alongside alternatives such as a HELOC, home equity loan, cash-out refinance, or a sale contingency.

The key calculation is not simply **"How much equity do I have?"**

It is:

**"How much temporary financing can I safely carry until my existing home sells?"**

Understanding that distinction can help homeowners structure a purchase-and-sale transition without relying entirely on an optimistic selling timeline.
