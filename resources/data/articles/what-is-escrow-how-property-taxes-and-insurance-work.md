When you make a mortgage payment, the amount you send to your mortgage servicer may cover more than principal and interest. Many homeowners also pay money into an **escrow account** that the servicer uses to pay property taxes, homeowners insurance, and certain other property-related expenses.

Escrow can make homeownership easier by spreading large annual or semiannual bills into smaller monthly payments. Instead of saving separately for a $4,800 property-tax bill, for example, a homeowner might contribute approximately $400 per month toward that expense through the mortgage payment.

Understanding how escrow works can help homeowners interpret their monthly mortgage payment, anticipate changes, and identify why their payment may increase even when their mortgage interest rate remains unchanged.

## What Is a Mortgage Escrow Account?
A mortgage escrow account is an account established by a mortgage lender or servicer to hold money collected from the borrower for certain property-related expenses.

The most common expenses paid from escrow are:

  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Flood insurance when required or included
  - Other insurance premiums required under the mortgage agreement

The mortgage servicer collects a portion of these expected expenses with each monthly mortgage payment and later uses the funds to pay the bills when they come due.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explains that many mortgage payments include money for property taxes and homeowners insurance in addition to principal and interest. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-account-en-138/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## How Escrow Changes Your Monthly Mortgage Payment
Consider a homeowner with the following annual expenses:

  - Property taxes: $4,800
  - Homeowners insurance: $1,200

The combined annual cost is:

**$4,800 + $1,200 = $6,000**

Dividing that amount across 12 months gives:

**$6,000 ÷ 12 = $500**

The homeowner could therefore contribute approximately $500 per month toward escrow, in addition to principal and interest.

If the mortgage's principal-and-interest payment is $2,000, the total monthly payment could be approximately:

**$2,000 + $500 = $2,500**

This is why two homeowners with identical mortgage balances and interest rates can have different total monthly payments if their property taxes or insurance costs differ.

## How Property Taxes Are Paid Through Escrow
Property taxes are generally assessed by a local government, such as a county, municipality, or other taxing authority.

When taxes are escrowed, the mortgage servicer collects money throughout the year and then pays the tax authority when the bill becomes due.

This arrangement can reduce the risk that a homeowner forgets to pay a tax bill.

Property taxes can vary substantially between properties because of differences in:

  - Assessed value
  - Local tax rates
  - Exemptions
  - Special assessments
  - Local government policies
  - Changes in property value

If your property tax bill increases, your escrow requirement may increase as well.

For example, suppose your annual property taxes rise from $4,800 to $5,400.

Your estimated monthly tax contribution would increase from:

**$4,800 ÷ 12 = $400**

to:

**$5,400 ÷ 12 = $450**

That $50 monthly difference can increase your total mortgage payment even if your interest rate and loan balance remain unchanged.

## How Homeowners Insurance Is Paid Through Escrow
Homeowners insurance can also be paid from an escrow account.

The servicer collects the expected annual premium throughout the year and then pays the insurance company when the policy premium is due.

This can make insurance costs more predictable from a budgeting perspective.

However, insurance premiums can change.

A homeowner's insurance premium may increase because of factors such as:

  - Higher rebuilding costs
  - Changes in local catastrophe risk
  - Property characteristics
  - Claims history
  - Changes in coverage
  - Inflation
  - Changes in the insurance market

If the annual insurance premium rises, the amount needed for future escrow payments can increase.

Homeowners should therefore review their insurance declarations page and escrow analysis rather than assuming that the insurance portion of the mortgage payment will remain constant.

## Why Your Mortgage Payment Can Increase
A common misconception is that a fixed-rate mortgage guarantees a completely fixed monthly payment.

A **fixed interest rate** generally keeps the principal-and-interest portion of the mortgage stable, but the total monthly payment can change when escrow expenses change.

For example:

**Principal and interest:** $2,000
**Property taxes:** $400
**Homeowners insurance:** $100

Total:

**$2,500 per month**

If property taxes increase by $600 per year, the monthly tax allocation increases by approximately $50.

The total payment could therefore rise to approximately:

**$2,550 per month**

The mortgage interest rate did not change. The escrow requirement did.

The CFPB notes that property taxes and homeowners insurance are among the costs that can cause the total mortgage payment to change. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-can-my-monthly-mortgage-payment-change-en-1943/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## What Is an Escrow Shortage?
An **escrow shortage** occurs when the amount currently in the escrow account is less than the amount needed to cover expected future bills and the required minimum balance.

This can happen when property taxes or insurance premiums increase unexpectedly.

For example, suppose a servicer expected a homeowner's annual insurance premium to be $1,200 but the renewal premium increases to $1,600.

The escrow account may not have enough money to cover the difference while also maintaining the required balance.

The servicer conducts an escrow analysis and may determine that the account has a shortage.

Depending on the circumstances and applicable rules, the homeowner may be able to repay the shortage over time through increased monthly payments or may have other payment options.

## What Is an Escrow Surplus?
The opposite situation is an **escrow surplus**.

If the account contains more money than required after the servicer's analysis, the homeowner may be entitled to a refund depending on the applicable rules and the size of the surplus.

Federal rules governing escrow accounts establish requirements for how servicers calculate and handle shortages and surpluses. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/17/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The servicer generally provides an annual escrow statement explaining the account's activity and projected expenses.

## What Is an Escrow Analysis?
An escrow analysis compares the amount currently held in the account with the amount expected to be needed for property taxes, insurance, and other escrowed expenses.

Mortgage servicers generally perform an escrow analysis annually.

The analysis considers:

  - Current escrow balance
  - Expected property-tax bills
  - Insurance premiums
  - Required minimum balance
  - Previous shortages or surpluses
  - Projected monthly contributions

The result can affect your future monthly mortgage payment.

If taxes and insurance remain relatively stable, the change may be small. If either expense increases significantly, the payment can change substantially.

## Why Does an Escrow Cushion Exist?
Mortgage servicers generally maintain a reserve, or **cushion**, in the escrow account to help cover unexpected increases in bills or timing differences.

Federal regulations generally allow a servicer to maintain a cushion up to one-sixth of the estimated annual escrow disbursements, or approximately two months of estimated escrow payments, subject to applicable rules and loan documents. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/17/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The purpose is to reduce the risk that the account becomes underfunded when a tax or insurance bill is higher than expected.

The cushion is not an additional fee charged by the servicer. It is money associated with the homeowner's escrow account.

## Can You Choose Not to Have an Escrow Account?
Whether escrow is required depends on the mortgage, lender, loan type, property, down payment, applicable law, and other factors.

Some borrowers may be allowed to waive escrow under certain circumstances, while other loans or transactions can require an escrow account.

For example, federal rules impose escrow requirements for certain higher-priced mortgage loans secured by first liens on a principal dwelling, subject to specified exceptions. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1026/35/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Even when escrow is optional, homeowners should consider whether they are comfortable managing large tax and insurance bills themselves.

Without escrow, the homeowner is responsible for setting aside enough money to make the payments when they become due.

## Escrow and Homeowners Insurance Changes
Homeowners should notify their mortgage servicer if they change insurance companies.

If the servicer does not receive evidence of adequate coverage, it may purchase insurance on the property under certain circumstances. This is commonly called **force-placed insurance**.

Force-placed coverage can be significantly more expensive and may provide less protection than a homeowner's regular policy. The CFPB advises borrowers to maintain required homeowners insurance and provide updated policy information to their servicer when necessary. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-force-placed-insurance-en-181/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

When switching insurers, coordinate the effective dates carefully so there is no lapse in coverage.

## Escrow and Home Improvements
Major home improvements can indirectly affect escrow costs.

A renovation itself does not normally create an escrow payment, but improvements can affect:

  - Property value
  - Property taxes
  - Insurance replacement costs
  - Homeowners insurance premiums

For example, a major addition could increase the home's assessed value in some jurisdictions. A significant renovation may also increase the amount it would cost to rebuild the property, potentially affecting insurance coverage requirements or premiums.

After completing major work, homeowners should review both their property-tax assessment and homeowners insurance coverage.

## What Happens to Escrow When You Sell or Refinance?
When you sell a home and the existing mortgage is paid off, the remaining escrow balance is generally handled according to applicable servicing and closing procedures.

If you refinance with the same or a different lender, the existing escrow account may be closed and a new escrow account may be established for the replacement mortgage.

The treatment of the old escrow balance depends on the transaction and servicer. Homeowners should review the closing documents and confirm how the existing balance will be returned or applied.

A refinance can also require an initial escrow deposit for future taxes and insurance.

## How to Read an Escrow Statement
Your annual escrow statement should help explain why your mortgage payment changed.

Look for:

**Beginning balance****
**The amount in the account at the start of the analysis period.

**Deposits****
**The amount contributed through your monthly mortgage payments.

**Disbursements****
**Payments made to the tax authority or insurance company.

**Projected expenses****
**The servicer's estimate of future property-tax and insurance bills.

**Shortage or surplus****
**The difference between the projected account balance and the amount the servicer determines should be available.

If an amount appears incorrect, compare the statement with your actual property-tax and insurance bills and contact the servicer if necessary.

## How to Keep Your Escrow Costs Under Control
You cannot directly control many local property-tax changes, but there are steps you can take to manage escrow-related expenses.

### Review Your Homeowners Insurance
Insurance is often one of the largest components of escrow after property taxes. Compare coverage and premiums periodically, especially after major home improvements.

### Check Your Property-Tax Assessment
Review your local assessment and tax bill for accuracy. If your jurisdiction provides a formal appeal process and you believe the assessed value is incorrect, investigate the applicable procedure.

### Monitor Insurance Renewals
Do not wait for your mortgage payment to increase before checking your insurance premium. Reviewing the renewal notice can help explain a projected escrow change.

### Keep Records of Improvements
Maintain permits, invoices, contractor documentation, and other records related to major improvements. These documents can be useful when updating insurance coverage or addressing property assessments.

## Escrow vs. Paying Taxes and Insurance Yourself
Escrow offers convenience because the mortgage servicer handles major recurring bills.

Without escrow, homeowners retain more control over their cash and can potentially earn interest on money set aside for future bills, depending on their financial strategy and applicable account options.

However, they must also budget for large payments themselves.

|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| \*\*Feature\*\* | \*\*With Escrow\*\* | \*\*Without Escrow\*\* |
| Property taxes | Paid by servicer | Paid by homeowner |
| Insurance | Often paid by servicer | Paid by homeowner |
| Monthly budgeting | More predictable | Requires personal planning |
| Large annual bills | Spread into monthly payments | Homeowner must save separately |
| Control of funds | Servicer holds funds | Homeowner retains funds |
| Risk of missing payment | Lower if account is properly funded | Homeowner responsible |

Neither structure is automatically appropriate for every borrower.

## Final Considerations
An escrow account is essentially a budgeting and payment mechanism connected to a mortgage. Instead of paying property taxes and homeowners insurance directly when the bills arrive, many homeowners contribute toward those expenses every month and the mortgage servicer makes the payments from the escrow account. ([consumerfinance.gov](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-an-escrow-account-en-138/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The most important point to remember is that a fixed-rate mortgage does not necessarily mean a fixed **total** monthly payment. Property taxes and insurance premiums can change, causing the escrow portion—and therefore the total mortgage payment—to increase or decrease.

Reviewing your annual escrow statement, checking insurance renewals, monitoring property-tax assessments, and maintaining accurate records of major home improvements can make these changes easier to understand.

For prospective homebuyers, escrow should also be included when estimating the true monthly cost of a property. A mortgage payment that looks affordable based only on principal and interest can be substantially higher once property taxes, homeowners insurance, and other required housing costs are included.
