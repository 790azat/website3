The final stage of a remodeling or home improvement project can be surprisingly complicated. A contractor may consider the work substantially complete while the homeowner notices unfinished trim, damaged surfaces, missing hardware, poor paint touch-ups, or systems that have not been properly tested.

That is where a **punch list** comes in. A punch list is a documented list of incomplete, defective, or corrective work that must be addressed before the project is considered fully complete. Construction-industry guidance treats punch-list preparation and closeout as distinct steps in the completion process.

A well-managed punch list can help prevent small defects from becoming disputes and gives both the homeowner and contractor a clear path to final completion.

## What Is a Punch List?
A punch list identifies work that remains unfinished or does not conform to the agreed project requirements.

Typical items include:

  - Paint touch-ups
  - Scratched flooring or cabinets
  - Missing trim or hardware
  - Doors that do not close correctly
  - Uneven tile or grout
  - Leaking faucets or plumbing connections
  - Incorrect electrical fixtures
  - Damaged countertops
  - Missing caulk or sealant
  - Incomplete landscaping
  - Debris or construction materials left on-site
  - Appliances or equipment that have not been tested

The list should focus on **specific, observable issues**, rather than vague comments such as "finish the bathroom."

A better entry would be: "Replace cracked grout along the lower-right shower wall and reseal the corner joint."

AIA guidance similarly describes punch lists as lists of items requiring completion or correction and notes that additional deficiencies can still be identified during later inspections.

## Start With the Original Contract and Scope of Work
Before walking through the property, review the signed contract, plans, specifications, approved change orders, and selections.

The purpose is to determine what the contractor actually agreed to provide.

For example, if the original scope specified:

Install six recessed LED fixtures in the kitchen.

and only five were installed, that is different from asking the contractor to add another fixture that was never part of the agreement.

Check:

1.  Original scope of work
2.  Architectural drawings, if applicable
3.  Material and finish selections
4.  Change orders
5.  Allowances
6.  Approved substitutions
7.  Warranty requirements
8.  Permit and inspection requirements

This comparison prevents the punch list from becoming a collection of new requests that were never included in the contract.

## Do a Complete Final Walkthrough
Schedule enough time for a systematic walkthrough rather than inspecting the house casually.

Start outside and work inward. Move room by room and examine surfaces, fixtures, doors, windows, cabinets, flooring, plumbing, electrical components, and mechanical equipment.

A useful sequence is:

| Area | What to Check |
|---|---|
| Exterior | Siding, roofing, drainage, doors, trim, grading |
| Entryways | Doors, locks, thresholds, weatherstripping |
| Walls/Ceilings | Paint, drywall, cracks, patches |
| Floors | Scratches, transitions, grout, level changes |
| Windows | Operation, locks, screens, seals |
| Cabinets | Alignment, doors, drawers, hardware |
| Plumbing | Leaks, drainage, hot/cold operation |
| Electrical | Switches, outlets, fixtures, GFCI protection where required |
| HVAC | Thermostats, airflow, heating/cooling operation |
| Appliances | Installation, connections, basic operation |
| Exterior work | Decks, patios, fences, landscaping and drainage |

For larger projects, a floor plan or room-by-room checklist can make the process easier. AIA construction guidance identifies narrative lists, floor-plan-based punch lists, and room-specific documentation as common approaches.

## Test Systems Instead of Only Looking at Them
Some defects are impossible to identify by visual inspection alone.

Turn on faucets and check underneath cabinets. Run appliances. Open and close windows. Test doors and locks. Operate thermostats and verify that heating or cooling responds appropriately.

For larger construction projects, professional closeout procedures can include testing, balancing, commissioning, operating instructions, and owner training before completion.

For a major renovation, ask the contractor which systems require formal testing or inspection before final acceptance.

Do not assume that a system works simply because it turns on.

## Photograph Every Punch-List Item
Take clear photographs of each issue and attach them to the written list.

For example:

**Item \#12 — Master Bathroom**
"Missing silicone sealant at tub-to-wall transition."

Attach a photograph and identify the exact location.

This is especially useful when a contractor has multiple trades working simultaneously. Numbering the items creates a simple system for tracking responsibility and completion.

A spreadsheet can include:

| Item | Location | Required Correction | Responsible Trade | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | Kitchen | Repair cabinet scratch | Cabinet installer | Open |
| 02 | Hallway | Touch up wall paint | Painter | Open |
| 03 | Bathroom | Correct faucet leak | Plumber | Complete |

## Separate Cosmetic Issues From Functional Problems
Not every punch-list item carries the same importance.

Prioritize problems involving:

### 1. Safety
Examples include exposed wiring, missing required guards, unsafe stairs, or other potentially hazardous conditions.

### 2. Water intrusion
Leaks, failed waterproofing, missing flashing, and drainage problems deserve immediate attention because concealed damage can become much more expensive later.

### 3. Building systems
Electrical, plumbing, HVAC, gas, and other major systems should be functioning properly before final acceptance.

### 4. Contract compliance
Missing materials or work that differs from the approved plans should be documented.

### 5. Cosmetic corrections
Paint imperfections, minor trim issues, scratches, and similar items can generally be addressed after more critical problems have been resolved.

AIA risk-management guidance specifically recommends identifying life-safety and other nonconforming items that remain unresolved during project closeout.

## Give the Contractor a Clear Completion Process
Once the list is prepared, send the contractor one consolidated version.

Avoid sending dozens of scattered text messages over several days. A centralized list makes it easier to determine what has been corrected and what remains open.

For each item, specify:

  - Location
  - Problem
  - Required correction
  - Photo
  - Priority, if appropriate
  - Date identified
  - Completion date
  - Verification status

The contractor should then schedule the necessary trades to return and correct the items.

For larger projects, formal closeout procedures may distinguish between substantial completion and final completion, with the outstanding punch-list items documented as part of that process.

## Don't Automatically Approve Final Payment
Final payment should be handled according to the contract—not simply because the contractor says the project is finished.

Review:

  - Remaining contract balance
  - Approved change orders
  - Credits
  - Uncompleted work
  - Retainage, if applicable
  - Required releases or documentation
  - Warranty information
  - Final inspection approvals
  - Permit closeout
  - Other contractual closeout requirements

For larger construction contracts, AIA payment documents specifically account for the contract sum, previous payments, retainage, change orders, and the amount requested for the current payment.

Homeowners should also check their own contract and applicable state or local law before withholding payment. Payment, retainage, lien rights, and final acceptance rules can vary substantially.

## Collect the Closeout Package
The project is not necessarily finished just because the workers have left.

Before closing the project, request documentation applicable to your renovation, such as:

  - Product warranties
  - Contractor warranties
  - Appliance manuals
  - HVAC documentation
  - Paint or finish information
  - Maintenance instructions
  - Final invoices
  - Approved change orders
  - Permit records
  - Inspection approvals
  - As-built information where applicable
  - Spare materials, keys, remotes, and access devices

AIA closeout guidance identifies items such as record drawings, warranties, operation and maintenance information, test reports, and other project records as part of organized project closeout.

Keep digital copies in a dedicated home-improvement folder.

## Do a Second Walkthrough
After the contractor reports that the punch list is complete, walk through the property again.

Do not simply assume that every item has been corrected.

Use the original numbered list:

  - Item 1 — completed and verified
  - Item 2 — completed and verified
  - Item 3 — still open
  - Item 4 — completed but requires another test

For functional items, test them again. For repaired surfaces, inspect the repair under normal lighting. For plumbing, look for leaks after the system has operated for a reasonable period.

Only close an item after verifying the correction.

## Watch for "Punch-List Creep"
A common problem is adding new work after the original list has been established.

If you decide you want a different faucet, an additional outlet, upgraded trim, or another remodeling change, that may be a **new scope item**, not a punch-list correction.

Keep the distinction clear:

**Punch-list item:** The contractor failed to complete or correctly perform agreed work.

**Change request:** The homeowner wants something different from what was originally agreed.

This distinction can prevent disagreements over additional charges.

## Final Punch List Checklist
Before considering the project closed, confirm:

  - Contract scope has been reviewed
  - Approved change orders have been incorporated
  - Every room has been inspected
  - Exterior work has been checked
  - Plumbing has been tested
  - Electrical components have been checked
  - HVAC has been tested
  - Doors and windows operate correctly
  - Cabinets, flooring, tile, and finishes have been inspected
  - Defects have been photographed
  - Punch-list items are numbered
  - Contractor has completed corrections
  - Corrections have been independently verified
  - Required permits/inspections are closed
  - Warranties and manuals have been collected
  - Keys, remotes, spare materials, and access information have been received
  - Final payment requirements have been reviewed
  - A final copy of the project records has been saved

## Final Considerations
A punch list should not be treated as a last-minute argument with a contractor. It is a **structured quality-control process** that gives both parties a written record of what remains before completion.

The most effective approach is simple: compare the finished project against the contract, document every deficiency clearly, prioritize safety and functional problems, give the contractor a consolidated list, verify every correction, and collect the necessary closeout documents before declaring the project complete.

For major renovations, the contract should ultimately control the closeout process. Industry guidance also emphasizes that the exact sequence of substantial completion, punch-list correction, inspections, documentation, and final payment depends on the contract and the requirements of the applicable authority having jurisdiction.
