When a home no longer fits your household, there are usually two paths: expand the property you already own or sell and move to a larger home. Both can require substantial money, planning, and patience—but the financial calculation is very different.

A room addition gives you more usable space without giving up your current property. Moving gives you the opportunity to solve several problems at once, such as an inconvenient layout, undesirable location, or limited lot size. The right comparison is therefore not simply "addition cost vs. new home price." Homeowners should also consider financing, closing costs, moving expenses, construction disruption, and the long-term value of the property.

## How Much Does a Room Addition Cost in 2026?
Current national estimates show that room additions can vary considerably depending on size, structure, location, and finishes.

Angi's 2026 data places typical room addition costs at approximately **$20,900 to $72,600**, while broader home additions average around **$51,000**. Building outward generally costs less per square foot than adding a second story because an upper-level addition can require significant structural reinforcement.

For example, Angi estimates that a 12-by-12-foot room addition averages about $18,000 when built outward, while the same amount of second-story space can average roughly $57,600.

Typical expenses can include:

  - Architectural or design services
  - Engineering and structural calculations
  - Building permits
  - Foundation and excavation work
  - Framing and roofing
  - Windows and exterior doors
  - Electrical and HVAC work
  - Plumbing, if applicable
  - Insulation and drywall
  - Flooring and finishes
  - Painting and final cleanup

Permits alone can add hundreds or thousands of dollars depending on the project and jurisdiction. Angi reports permit costs for additions ranging from approximately $150 to $2,000 for many room-addition projects, although larger or more complicated projects can cost more.

### What Can Make an Addition Much More Expensive?
A simple bedroom is usually less complicated than adding a kitchen or bathroom. Plumbing, additional electrical capacity, ventilation, waterproofing, and specialized finishes can substantially increase the budget.

Building upward can also be expensive because the existing structure may need reinforcement. Angi's September 2026 data puts a full second-story addition at roughly **$100,000 to $250,000**, with an average around $175,000.

## How Long Does a Room Addition Take?
Construction time is another major consideration.

A straightforward conversion can take only a few months, while a complex structural addition can take substantially longer. Current Angi estimates put straightforward additions at roughly **three to four months**, while extensive bump-outs and second-story projects can approach **a year**.

A typical project may involve:

1.  Initial planning and feasibility review
2.  Architectural drawings
3.  Contractor estimates
4.  Engineering
5.  Permit applications
6.  Site preparation
7.  Foundation and framing
8.  Roofing and exterior work
9.  Electrical, plumbing, and HVAC
10. Drywall, flooring, cabinets, and finishes
11. Inspections
12. Final completion

The construction period is only part of the schedule. Design changes, permit delays, material availability, weather, contractor scheduling, and unexpected structural problems can extend the project.

For homeowners considering an addition, getting detailed plans and contractor estimates before committing to financing can help reduce the risk of major cost overruns.

## What Does Moving Cost?
Moving to another home introduces a different collection of expenses.

The most obvious cost is the purchase price of the new property, but homeowners also need to account for the costs of selling the existing home and buying another one.

The CFPB states that **closing costs on a home purchase typically range from 2% to 5% of the purchase price**, excluding the down payment. Actual costs vary by loan, property, location, lender, and other factors.

For a $400,000 purchase, that 2%–5% range represents approximately **$8,000 to $20,000** in buyer closing costs alone.

Other potential expenses include:

  - Professional moving services
  - Packing materials or professional packing
  - Temporary storage
  - Temporary housing
  - Home inspection
  - Appraisal
  - Loan origination and other lender charges
  - Repairs required before selling the current home
  - Improvements made to prepare the property for sale
  - New furniture or appliances
  - Utility transfers and installation fees

Moving costs themselves vary significantly based on distance, household size, services, and timing. One Forbes Home survey-based estimate put the average move at $1,035, but homeowners should treat such figures as broad benchmarks rather than quotes for an individual move.

## Moving Can Take Longer Than It Appears
Buying a different house is not necessarily faster than building an addition.

NerdWallet's June 2026 analysis estimates that buying a home generally takes **three to six months once the buyer is financially ready**, with the mortgage closing itself commonly taking 30–60 days.

Selling your current home adds another variable. You may need to prepare the property, list it, negotiate with buyers, complete inspections and appraisal requirements, and coordinate the closing date with your next purchase.

That creates a timing problem that does not exist in the same way with an addition: you may need to coordinate **two real-estate transactions instead of one construction project**.

## Room Addition vs. Moving: Cost Comparison
| Factor | Room Addition | Moving |
|---|---|---|
| Major expense | Construction | New property purchase |
| Typical project range | \\~20,900–72,600 for many room additions | Depends primarily on new home's price |
| Closing costs | Usually limited to financing/project-related costs | Buyer closing costs commonly 2%–5% of purchase price |
| Moving expenses | Minimal if staying in the home | Professional movers, storage, packing |
| Timeline | About 3–12+ months depending on complexity | Often several months when buying and selling |
| Financing | Cash, HELOC, home equity loan, construction financing | New mortgage or cash |
| Main disruption | Construction inside/around existing home | Selling, buying, packing, and relocating |
| Location change | None | Yes |
| Ability to customize | High | Depends on the property purchased |
| Structural risk | Possible unexpected construction costs | Inspection can reveal property problems |

The important point is that an addition can look expensive in isolation while still being cheaper than moving into a substantially more expensive home.

## Financing an Addition
Homeowners with sufficient equity may have several financing options.

A **home equity loan** provides a lump sum secured by the home's equity. A **HELOC** provides a revolving credit line that allows the homeowner to draw funds as needed. The CFPB notes that HELOCs generally have variable interest rates, while home equity loans often have fixed rates.

For a project with uncertain expenses, a HELOC can provide flexibility because funds can be drawn progressively. A home equity loan may be easier to budget when the project has a clearly defined cost.

However, both use the home as collateral. Borrowers should compare interest, fees, repayment terms, and total borrowing costs rather than choosing based solely on the monthly payment.

## When Moving May Solve More Than an Addition
An addition makes more sense when the **house itself works but the space does not**.

For example, a family may love its neighborhood, schools, commute, lot, and existing mortgage but simply need another bedroom and bathroom.

Moving can be more appropriate when the problem extends beyond square footage. Examples include:

  - The lot cannot accommodate the desired addition
  - Local zoning restricts expansion
  - The existing foundation or structure makes expansion unusually expensive
  - The neighborhood no longer meets the household's needs
  - The commute is too long
  - The homeowner wants a substantially different floor plan
  - The property requires several major repairs in addition to the space problem

In these cases, spending $100,000 or more on an addition may not solve the underlying problem.

## A Simple Break-Even Calculation
Before choosing, calculate the **all-in cost**, rather than comparing a contractor's quote with the listing price of another home.

For an addition:

**Total Addition Cost = Construction + Design + Permits + Financing + Temporary Housing + Contingency**

For moving:

**Total Moving Cost = Purchase Costs + Selling Costs + Closing Costs + Moving + Repairs + Financing + Temporary Housing**

Then compare the two totals over the period you expect to own the property.

For example, suppose an addition costs $75,000 but allows you to remain in a neighborhood you expect to stay in for another decade. That may have a very different financial outcome from purchasing a $150,000-more-expensive home and paying the associated transaction and financing costs.

The calculation should also include the value of the resulting property—not simply the amount spent.

## Final Considerations
The decision between adding a room and moving is ultimately a **property, financing, and lifestyle decision**, not simply a construction-cost comparison.

A room addition can preserve your location and allow you to customize the exact space you need, but construction can take months and may expose you to permitting, structural, and cost-overrun risks. Moving can provide a larger or fundamentally different home, but it introduces transaction costs, financing requirements, moving expenses, and the uncertainty of selling and buying on compatible schedules.

Before committing, obtain detailed contractor estimates, investigate local permitting requirements, calculate your available home equity, and compare the full cost of moving against the full cost of expanding.

For homeowners who otherwise like their property, the key question is often straightforward: **Is the problem the house—or simply the amount of space inside it?**
