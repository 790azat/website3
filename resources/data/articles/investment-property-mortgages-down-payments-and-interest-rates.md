Buying an investment property requires a different financing strategy from purchasing a primary residence. Lenders generally view rental properties as higher-risk transactions because the borrower does not occupy the property and repayment can depend partly on rental income.

As a result, investment property mortgages typically require a larger down payment, stronger financial reserves, and different pricing from owner-occupied mortgages.

For conventional financing, Freddie Mac currently allows purchase mortgages on one-unit investment properties up to 85% loan-to-value (LTV), while two- to four-unit investment properties can generally reach 75% LTV under its standard requirements. That translates to minimum down payments of approximately 15% and 25%, respectively, before considering lender-specific requirements.

Interest rates are also generally higher for investment properties than for primary residences. The actual rate depends on credit, LTV, property type, loan amount, market conditions, and the lender's pricing.

## What Is an Investment Property Mortgage?
An investment property mortgage is a loan used to purchase or refinance a property that the borrower does not intend to occupy as their primary residence.

Common examples include:

  - Single-family rental homes
  - Duplexes purchased strictly as rentals
  - Triplexes and fourplexes
  - Rental condominiums
  - Certain townhomes
  - Other eligible one- to four-unit residential properties

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both have conventional mortgage programs for one- to four-unit investment properties, although specific underwriting and eligibility requirements differ.

An investment property is different from a **second home**. A second home is generally intended for the borrower's personal use, while an investment property is acquired primarily to generate rental income or investment returns.

That distinction affects mortgage eligibility, pricing, down payment requirements, and underwriting.

## How Much Down Payment Is Required?
The down payment depends on the property type and mortgage program.

For conventional Freddie Mac financing, current maximum purchase LTV ratios are:

| Property | Maximum LTV | Approximate Minimum Down Payment |
|---|---|---|
| 1-unit investment property | 85% | 15% |
| 2- to 4-unit investment property | 75% | 25% |

These are program-level maximums rather than guarantees that every borrower will receive that financing. A lender can require a larger down payment based on credit, reserves, property characteristics, loan amount, or its own underwriting policies.

For example, a 1-unit rental property priced at $400,000 could potentially be financed with a $340,000 mortgage and a $60,000 down payment under an 85% LTV structure.

A $400,000 fourplex, however, could require approximately $100,000 down under a 75% LTV structure.

Closing costs and required reserves would be additional.

## Why Investment Property Loans Require More Cash
A larger down payment gives the lender more protection if the property must be sold after default.

It also means the borrower has more equity invested in the property.

From the lender's perspective, an investment property introduces additional risks:

  - The property may remain vacant.
  - Tenants may pay late.
  - Repairs can be unexpected.
  - Rental income can decline.
  - Property taxes and insurance can increase.
  - The property could require substantial maintenance.
  - The borrower still has to make the mortgage payment when rent is unavailable.

Because of these risks, investment-property financing generally receives different underwriting and pricing treatment than owner-occupied financing.

Freddie Mac specifically applies additional requirements to investment-property mortgages involving reserves, rental income, housing expenses, and borrowers with multiple financed properties.

## How Investment Property Mortgage Rates Work
Investment-property mortgage rates are usually higher than rates for comparable primary-residence mortgages.

There is no single investment-property rate that applies to every borrower.

Your rate can depend on:

  - Credit score
  - LTV ratio
  - Loan amount
  - Property type
  - Number of units
  - Fixed-rate or adjustable-rate structure
  - Loan term
  - Occupancy classification
  - Reserves
  - Rental income
  - Overall borrower profile
  - Current mortgage-market conditions

Because mortgage rates change continuously, an advertised rate should be treated as a point-in-time quote rather than a permanent market rate.

The difference between a primary-residence mortgage and an investment-property mortgage can also vary by lender.

For that reason, comparing the **annual percentage rate (APR), points, lender fees, and total closing costs** can be more informative than comparing the note rate alone.

## How Credit Affects Investment Property Financing
Credit history is an important part of investment-property underwriting.

A lender may evaluate:

  - Credit score
  - Payment history
  - Existing mortgage obligations
  - Revolving debt
  - Installment loans
  - Previous foreclosures or bankruptcies
  - Length and stability of credit history

A stronger credit profile can help a borrower qualify for more favorable pricing, although the exact impact depends on the lender and loan program.

Investment-property financing can become more difficult when combined with high LTV, limited reserves, significant existing debt, or multiple financed properties.

Improving credit before applying can therefore be useful, particularly when the borrower expects to finance several properties over time.

## Reserve Requirements
Down payment money is not the only cash that may be required.

Lenders can also require **financial reserves** after closing.

Reserves are liquid or near-liquid assets available to cover mortgage payments and other financial obligations.

Under Fannie Mae's current DU guidelines, an investment-property transaction generally requires six months of reserves for the subject property's qualifying payment amount. Additional reserves can apply when the borrower has multiple financed properties.

For borrowers with multiple financed properties, Fannie Mae currently calculates additional reserves based on the aggregate unpaid principal balances of other financed properties:

  - 2% for one to four other financed properties
  - 4% for five to six
  - 6% for seven to ten under DU

The specific calculation depends on the borrower's circumstances and applicable underwriting rules.

This means an investor should not plan to use every available dollar for the down payment.

## Can Rental Income Help You Qualify?
Potentially.

One of the major differences between an investment-property mortgage and a standard owner-occupied mortgage is that rental income can play a role in qualifying.

Lenders generally need documentation demonstrating that the expected rental income is reasonable and likely to continue.

For eligible Fannie Mae transactions involving a purchased one- to four-unit investment property, the lender can use rental income based on applicable documentation and calculation requirements.

For example, Fannie Mae's current guidelines generally calculate adjusted net rental income for a subject investment property by taking **75% of monthly gross rent** and subtracting the property's principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and applicable association dues (PITIA). Different rules can apply depending on the borrower's rental history and circumstances.

Consider a hypothetical rental property with:

**Monthly market rent:** $3,000
**75% of gross rent:** $2,250
**Monthly PITIA:** $2,000

The resulting adjusted net rental income would be approximately $250 per month under that simplified calculation.

The lender would then apply the applicable underwriting rules to determine how that amount affects qualification.

## Rental Income Is Not Always Counted in Full
A common mistake is assuming that the lender will simply take the advertised monthly rent and add 100% of it to income.

That is generally not how conventional underwriting works.

Vacancy, maintenance, operating costs, and other factors mean that lenders can apply adjustments to gross rental income.

Fannie Mae's current guidelines use a 75% calculation for certain investment-property rental-income scenarios. Other circumstances, including documented rental history and tax-return information, can change the calculation.

The lender may also require:

  - Appraisal rent schedules
  - Executed leases
  - Tax returns
  - Schedule E
  - Evidence of rental receipts
  - Documentation of the borrower's housing payment

The applicable documentation depends on the property and transaction.

## What If You Are a New Real Estate Investor?
You do not necessarily need years of rental-property experience to purchase an investment property.

However, rental-property experience can affect how rental income is treated for qualifying purposes.

Fannie Mae's current guidelines distinguish between borrowers with at least 12 months of rental-property management experience and those with less experience in certain subject-property transactions.

For borrowers with sufficient experience, eligible rental income can potentially have a broader effect on qualifying income.

For borrowers without that history, the amount of rental income that can be used may be more restricted.

This is one reason new investors should have the lender evaluate the property's expected rent before making assumptions about qualification.

## Investment Property Loan Terms
Investment-property mortgages can be structured with different terms.

Common options include:

  - 30-year fixed-rate mortgages
  - 15-year fixed-rate mortgages
  - Adjustable-rate mortgages
  - Conventional conforming financing
  - Portfolio loans
  - Other investor-oriented financing

Freddie Mac currently lists fixed-rate mortgages, most ARMs, and certain super conforming mortgages among eligible investment-property mortgage products, subject to applicable requirements.

A fixed-rate mortgage provides a predictable principal-and-interest payment.

An adjustable-rate mortgage may offer a different initial rate structure but can introduce payment changes later.

Investors should evaluate the financing based on the property's expected holding period and projected cash flow rather than choosing a loan solely according to the initial payment.

## Investment Property Mortgages and Cash Flow
The mortgage payment is only one part of the property's financial picture.

A rental-property budget should account for:

  - Mortgage principal and interest
  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance
  - HOA dues
  - Repairs
  - Maintenance
  - Vacancy
  - Property management
  - Utilities paid by the owner
  - Leasing costs
  - Capital expenditures

For example, a property generating $3,000 in monthly rent might initially appear attractive if the mortgage payment is $2,000.

But after property taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancy, management, and other costs are included, the actual cash flow could be considerably lower.

A lender's qualifying-income calculation is also not necessarily the same as an investor's full economic cash-flow analysis.

## One-Unit vs. Multi-Unit Investment Properties
The number of units can affect financing.

A one-unit investment property may qualify for a higher maximum LTV than a two- to four-unit investment property under conventional Freddie Mac purchase guidelines.

Multi-unit properties can potentially produce more rental income, but they can also involve:

  - Higher purchase prices
  - Larger down payments
  - More maintenance
  - Multiple tenants
  - Greater management responsibilities
  - Different appraisal considerations

A duplex or fourplex can therefore have a different financing profile from a single-family rental even when both are intended strictly as investments.

## Can You Finance Multiple Investment Properties?
Yes, subject to the applicable loan program and underwriting requirements.

Fannie Mae currently permits up to 10 financed properties for certain borrowers with a second-home or investment-property subject transaction when the loans are underwritten through DU. Additional reserve requirements apply as the number of financed properties increases.

This can be relevant to investors who intend to build a rental portfolio rather than purchase only one property.

However, every additional mortgage increases the borrower's financial obligations and can affect reserves, debt-to-income calculations, and future borrowing capacity.

## Investment Property Mortgage Closing Costs
Investment-property financing can involve many of the same closing costs associated with other mortgages.

Potential costs include:

  - Origination charges
  - Appraisal
  - Credit report
  - Title services
  - Recording fees
  - Prepaid taxes
  - Insurance
  - Discount points
  - Lender-specific fees

Investment properties can also have additional pricing adjustments or fees.

Freddie Mac explicitly notes that credit fees apply to investment-property mortgages.

When comparing lenders, request a **Loan Estimate** and evaluate the complete financing package rather than focusing only on the quoted interest rate.

## Can You Use Gift Money for an Investment Property Down Payment?
Do not assume that gift funds available for a primary residence can be used in the same way for an investment property.

The rules for acceptable sources of funds can differ substantially by loan program.

For example, Freddie Mac's current investment-property requirements state that borrower funds for down payment and closing costs must not include gifts as defined under its applicable guidelines.

Investors should therefore confirm the source-of-funds rules with the lender before relying on gifted money.

## Investment Property vs. Primary Residence Mortgage
The difference can be summarized as follows:

| Feature | Primary Residence | Investment Property |
|---|---|---|
| Intended occupancy | Owner lives there | Primarily rented/investment use |
| Down payment | Often lower | Generally higher |
| Interest rate | Typically lower | Typically higher |
| Reserves | Often lower | Often higher |
| Rental income | Limited circumstances | Can be important |
| Property risk | Lower from lender perspective | Higher |
| Financing options | Broad | More restricted |

These are general distinctions. Specific mortgage products can have different requirements.

## Questions to Ask an Investment Property Lender
Before applying, ask:

1.  What is the minimum down payment for this property?
2.  What interest rate and APR would apply?
3.  Are there investment-property pricing adjustments?
4.  How many months of reserves are required?
5.  Can projected rental income be used to qualify?
6.  How will rental income be calculated?
7.  What documentation is required for the rent?
8.  Are there restrictions on the property type?
9.  Are there limits on the number of financed properties I can own?
10. Can I use gifts or other outside funds?
11. What credit-score requirements apply?
12. What closing costs should I expect?
13. Are there prepayment penalties?
14. Is the mortgage fixed-rate or adjustable?
15. What happens if the property remains vacant?

Getting these answers before making an offer can make it easier to calculate the property's actual financing requirements.

## How to Compare Investment Property Mortgage Offers
The lowest advertised interest rate is not necessarily the lowest-cost mortgage.

Compare:

**Interest rate + APR + points + lender fees + required reserves + down payment + monthly payment**

For example, one lender might offer a slightly lower rate but charge more points. Another might offer a higher rate with fewer upfront costs.

The appropriate comparison depends partly on how long you expect to hold the mortgage.

An investor planning to sell or refinance within a few years may evaluate upfront costs differently from someone planning to hold the property for decades.

## Final Considerations
Investment property mortgages can provide leverage for purchasing rental real estate, but they generally require more cash and stronger financial preparation than financing a primary residence.

For conventional financing, current Freddie Mac guidelines allow maximum purchase LTV ratios of 85% for one-unit investment properties and 75% for two- to four-unit investment properties, although individual lenders and borrower circumstances can result in stricter requirements.

Rental income can help with qualification, but lenders do not necessarily count every dollar of expected rent. Fannie Mae's current guidelines use specific documentation and rental-income calculations, including a 75% gross-rent calculation in certain subject-property scenarios.

Investors should also plan for reserves. Under current Fannie Mae DU guidance, investment-property transactions generally require six months of reserves for the subject property's qualifying payment, with additional reserves potentially required when the borrower owns multiple financed properties.

Before purchasing, calculate the property's expected cash flow separately from the lender's qualification calculation. Include the mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancy, management, and other operating expenses.

The financing decision should ultimately be based on the **complete cost of borrowing and the property's projected financial performance**, not just the advertised down payment or interest rate.
