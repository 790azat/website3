Buying down a mortgage interest rate means paying an upfront cost to the lender in exchange for a lower interest rate. The most common way to do this is through **discount points**, which are paid at closing and reduce the interest rate on the mortgage.

For example, one discount point equals 1% of the mortgage amount. On a $400,000 loan, one point would cost $4,000. However, there is no universal rule that says one point will reduce the rate by a specific percentage. The reduction depends on the lender, loan type, market conditions, and other pricing factors.

The important question is not simply whether a lower mortgage rate sounds attractive. You need to compare the **upfront cost of the points with the monthly savings they create** and determine how long you expect to keep the mortgage.

## What Are Mortgage Discount Points?
Discount points are an upfront fee paid to a mortgage lender in exchange for a lower interest rate.

One point equals **1% of the loan amount**:

  - $200,000 mortgage → 1 point = $2,000
  - $300,000 mortgage → 1 point = $3,000
  - $400,000 mortgage → 1 point = $4,000
  - $500,000 mortgage → 1 point = $5,000

You can also purchase fractions of a point. For example, 0.5 points on a $400,000 mortgage would cost $2,000.

The amount by which the points reduce your interest rate is not fixed. One lender might offer a particular rate reduction for one point while another lender offers a different reduction. The CFPB specifically recommends asking lenders to clarify exactly how the points affect the rate being offered.

## How Buying Down Your Rate Works
Consider a hypothetical $400,000 30-year fixed-rate mortgage.

Suppose your lender offers:

| Option | Interest Rate | Points | Upfront Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| No points | 6.50% | 0 | $0 |
| Buy points | 6.25% | 1 | $4,000 |
| Buy more points | 6.00% | 2 | $8,000 |

These numbers are illustrative. Actual pricing varies by lender and market.

The lower-rate options require more cash at closing, but they reduce the monthly principal-and-interest payment.

The tradeoff is straightforward:

**Pay more now → pay less each month later.**

Whether that tradeoff makes financial sense depends primarily on how long you keep the mortgage.

## How Much Does One Point Lower Your Interest Rate?
There is no standard rate reduction for one discount point.

A commonly used example is one point reducing a mortgage rate by around 0.25 percentage points, but this is only an example—not a guaranteed pricing formula. Freddie Mac gives an example in which a $300,000 mortgage at 6.25% could potentially fall to 6.00% after purchasing one $3,000 point, depending on the lender.

The CFPB similarly emphasizes that the interest-rate reduction associated with points depends on the lender, loan type, and market conditions.

That means you should never assume:

**1 point = exactly 0.25% lower rate.**

Instead, ask the lender for the actual rate available at zero points, one point, and potentially two points.

## Calculate Your Break-Even Point
The most useful calculation when evaluating discount points is the **break-even period**.

The basic formula is:

**Break-even period = Cost of points ÷ Monthly payment savings**

Suppose you pay $4,000 for discount points and reduce your mortgage payment by $80 per month.

**$4,000 ÷ $80 = 50 months**

Your break-even point would be approximately **50 months**, or just over four years.

If you keep the mortgage longer than that, the cumulative payment savings could exceed the upfront cost of the points.

If you sell the home or refinance before reaching the break-even point, you may not recover the money spent on the points.

The CFPB recommends comparing points under multiple potential timeframes because your future plans can materially affect the economics.

## Points vs. a Lower Monthly Payment
The monthly payment reduction can be meaningful, particularly on a large mortgage.

For example, imagine two 30-year fixed mortgages:

  - $400,000 at 6.50%
  - $400,000 at 6.25%

The principal-and-interest payment at 6.50% is approximately $2,528 per month, while the payment at 6.25% is approximately $2,463.

That is a difference of roughly **$65 per month**.

If the lower rate costs $4,000 in discount points, the simple break-even calculation would be:

**$4,000 ÷ $65 ≈ 62 months**

That is about five years and two months.

Taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance, changes in the loan balance, and other costs are not included in this simplified comparison.

## When Paying Points Can Make Sense
Paying discount points may fit a borrower who expects to keep the mortgage for a sufficiently long period and has enough cash available for closing and reserves.

For example, points may deserve consideration if:

  - You expect to remain in the home for many years.
  - You do not anticipate refinancing soon.
  - You have sufficient savings after closing.
  - The rate reduction is substantial relative to the cost.
  - You want a lower recurring mortgage payment.
  - The points do not interfere with your down payment or emergency fund.

The CFPB notes that points can be useful for borrowers who plan to keep their mortgage for a long time, while borrowers expecting to refinance or move relatively soon may have less opportunity to recover the upfront expense.

## When Paying Points May Not Work
A lower rate is not automatically a lower-cost mortgage.

Points may be less attractive when you expect to sell or refinance relatively soon.

For example, if your break-even period is five years but you expect to refinance after two years, you would have paid the upfront fee without receiving enough monthly savings to recover it.

Points can also be less attractive if paying them would leave you with little money for:

  - Emergency savings
  - Closing costs
  - Moving expenses
  - Home repairs
  - Furniture and appliances
  - Property taxes
  - Homeowners insurance

A lower mortgage payment does not compensate for having insufficient cash reserves after purchasing the property.

## Discount Points vs. Origination Fees
The word **"points" can be confusing** because lenders sometimes use it differently.

Discount points specifically refer to upfront charges connected to obtaining a lower interest rate. The CFPB explains that points appearing on the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure should be connected to a discounted interest rate.

Origination charges, on the other hand, compensate the lender for originating or processing the mortgage and are not necessarily the same thing as discount points.

When a lender quotes "one point," ask:

**"Is this a discount point that lowers my interest rate, or is it an origination charge?"**

Getting the answer in writing can make mortgage offers easier to compare.

## How to Compare a Zero-Point Loan With a Buydown
Ask the same lender to provide multiple versions of the mortgage.

For example:

### Option A: Zero points
  - Interest rate: 6.50%
  - Points: $0
  - Monthly principal and interest: $2,528

### Option B: One point
  - Interest rate: 6.25%
  - Points: $4,000
  - Monthly principal and interest: $2,463

### Option C: Two points
  - Interest rate: 6.00%
  - Points: $8,000
  - Monthly principal and interest: $2,398

Then calculate the cumulative savings over different periods.

| Holding Period | Zero Points | 1 Point Savings | 2 Point Savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 years | — | Compare against $4,000 cost | Compare against $8,000 cost |
| 5 years | — | Compare against $4,000 cost | Compare against $8,000 cost |
| 10 years | — | Compare against $4,000 cost | Compare against $8,000 cost |

Do not compare the interest rates alone. The CFPB recommends requesting comparable Loan Estimates and examining the overall cost structure.

## What About Lender Credits?
Lender credits work in the opposite direction.

Instead of paying more at closing to receive a lower interest rate, you can sometimes accept a higher rate in exchange for credits that reduce certain upfront closing costs.

In simplified terms:

**Discount points:** pay more now → lower rate.

**Lender credits:** pay less now → higher rate.

The CFPB describes these as tradeoffs between upfront costs and ongoing mortgage costs.

This can be useful when comparing mortgage offers because the lowest advertised interest rate may involve substantial points, while another offer may have a slightly higher rate but significantly lower upfront costs.

## Can You Finance Discount Points?
In some circumstances, borrowers may be able to finance discount points rather than paying the entire cost separately at closing.

For example, Freddie Mac currently permits certain eligible borrowers to finance permanent discount points into the mortgage amount, subject to specific requirements and limits. Its current program allows up to three discount points for eligible fixed-rate mortgages and certain ARMs.

However, financing the points means borrowing more money. You should compare the additional loan balance and interest costs with the benefit of the lower rate.

Availability also depends on the mortgage program and lender.

## Can the Seller Pay for a Rate Buydown?
In some transactions, the seller or another permitted third party may contribute toward eligible closing costs, which can potentially include costs associated with a mortgage rate reduction subject to applicable loan-program rules.

This can sometimes be negotiated as part of the purchase agreement.

However, contribution limits and eligible uses vary by mortgage type, occupancy, loan-to-value ratio, and other factors. Your lender should confirm what is permitted for your specific mortgage.

A seller contribution can be particularly relevant when comparing a higher purchase price with a negotiated seller credit, because the financial effects are different.

## Permanent vs. Temporary Rate Buydowns
Not every mortgage rate buydown works the same way.

A **permanent buydown** reduces the mortgage's interest rate for the applicable term of the loan.

A **temporary buydown** generally provides a lower payment for an initial period, after which the payment returns to the underlying mortgage terms.

For example, a temporary 2-1 buydown could reduce the effective payment during the first two years before reaching the full note-rate payment.

This is different from purchasing permanent discount points. With a temporary buydown, borrowers need to understand what the payment becomes when the subsidy ends.

## Check Your Loan Estimate
Discount points appear on the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure.

The CFPB recommends using the Loan Estimate to compare mortgage offers and verify that the loan terms and costs match what you discussed with the lender.

Look specifically at:

  - Interest rate
  - Annual percentage rate (APR)
  - Points
  - Origination charges
  - Lender credits
  - Loan amount
  - Monthly principal and interest
  - Cash to close
  - Total interest and other projected costs

If a lender advertises a particularly low mortgage rate, check whether the quoted rate assumes that you are paying discount points.

The CFPB has warned that comparing mortgage offers solely by their advertised interest rate can be misleading when different lenders attach different amounts of discount points to those rates.

## How to Buy Down Your Mortgage Rate
The process is relatively straightforward:

1.  **Get mortgage quotes from multiple lenders.**
2.  **Request a zero-point option from each lender.**
3.  **Ask for pricing with one or more discount points.**
4.  **Record the rate reduction and upfront cost for each option.**
5.  **Calculate the break-even period.**
6.  **Consider how long you expect to keep the mortgage.**
7.  **Check whether paying points would reduce your emergency reserves.**
8.  **Compare the Loan Estimates, including APR and total costs.**
9.  **Confirm whether seller or lender credits are available.**
10. **Review the final Closing Disclosure before closing.**

This gives you an apples-to-apples comparison rather than choosing a mortgage based solely on the headline interest rate.

## Final Considerations
Buying mortgage discount points is essentially a decision to pay more upfront in exchange for a lower interest rate.

The calculation is simple, but the decision depends on your expected holding period, available cash, mortgage amount, rate reduction, and likelihood of refinancing.

Start by comparing the **zero-point rate** with the rate offered after purchasing points. Then calculate the break-even period and consider whether you are likely to keep the mortgage long enough to recover the upfront cost.

A lower interest rate can reduce monthly payments, but the lowest rate is not necessarily the lowest-cost mortgage. Compare points, lender credits, APR, closing costs, and the overall loan terms before deciding how much, if any, of your cash should go toward buying down the rate.
