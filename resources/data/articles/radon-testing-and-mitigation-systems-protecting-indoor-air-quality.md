Radon is an invisible, radioactive gas that can enter homes from the soil beneath and around a foundation. Because it has no reliable smell, color, or taste, homeowners cannot determine whether their indoor air contains elevated radon simply by looking for signs of a problem.

**Testing is the only way to know the radon concentration inside a home.** The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) recommends that homeowners take action when indoor radon reaches **4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L)** or higher and consider reducing exposure even when levels are between 2 and 4 pCi/L.

For homeowners, the process is straightforward:

**Test → understand the result → install mitigation if necessary → retest → maintain the system.**

## What Is Radon and Where Does It Come From?
Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas produced as uranium in soil and rock breaks down. It can move through soil and enter buildings through openings in foundations and floors.

Potential entry points include:

  - Cracks in concrete slabs
  - Gaps around pipes and utility penetrations
  - Construction joints
  - Floor drains
  - Sump pits
  - Crawl spaces
  - Gaps around foundations
  - Other openings between the building and surrounding soil

Radon can accumulate indoors because buildings can trap soil gases inside. Levels can vary substantially between homes, even when the properties are next door to one another.

The EPA notes that radon has been found at elevated levels throughout the United States and recommends testing homes regardless of location rather than relying solely on regional radon maps.

## Why Radon Testing Matters
You cannot reliably determine a home's radon level without measuring it.

A home may have:

  - No visible foundation problems but elevated radon
  - A finished basement with elevated radon
  - Low radon one season and higher readings during another
  - Different radon concentrations between floors

This is why radon testing is an important part of both routine home maintenance and real estate transactions.

The EPA specifically recommends testing when buying or selling a home.

## How Radon Testing Works
Homeowners generally encounter two broad testing approaches: **short-term testing** and **long-term testing**.

### Short-Term Radon Tests
Short-term tests measure radon over a relatively brief period. They can be useful when a homeowner wants an initial measurement or needs results during a real estate transaction.

Depending on the device and testing protocol, the test may run from a few days to several weeks.

Short-term tests are convenient, but a single short measurement may not represent a home's long-term average exposure because radon levels fluctuate.

### Long-Term Radon Tests
Long-term testing measures radon for more than 90 days.

Because radon concentrations can change over time, a longer testing period can provide a more representative picture of the home's average exposure.

Homeowners should follow the specific instructions supplied with the test device. Improper placement, interference with the device, or failure to maintain required testing conditions can compromise results.

## Where Should You Test for Radon?
Testing should generally focus on the **lowest level of the home that could be regularly occupied**.

For example, if a basement is finished and used as a living space, that area may be an important testing location.

The EPA notes that radon concentrations are often highest in the lowest part of a building, making basements and ground floors important places to test.

The testing device should be positioned according to the applicable testing protocol rather than simply placed wherever convenient.

Avoid placing it:

  - Directly beside windows
  - Near exterior doors
  - In direct sunlight
  - Near heating or ventilation equipment
  - In bathrooms or other unusually humid locations
  - Where it can be disturbed

## What Do Radon Test Results Mean?
Radon results are commonly reported in **picocuries per liter (pCi/L)**.

| Radon Level | General EPA Guidance |
|---|---|
| Below 2 pCi/L | Lower measured level, but not considered zero risk |
| 2–4 pCi/L | Consider reducing exposure |
| 4 pCi/L or higher | EPA recommends taking action to reduce radon |
| Very high levels | Prompt mitigation should be prioritized |

The EPA emphasizes that there is no known completely risk-free level of radon exposure. Its action level is **4 pCi/L**, equivalent to approximately **150 Bq/m³**.

The 4 pCi/L figure should therefore not be interpreted as a point at which radon suddenly becomes dangerous and anything below it is automatically safe. It is an action level used to determine when mitigation is recommended.

## What Is a Radon Mitigation System?
A radon mitigation system is designed to reduce the amount of radon entering or accumulating inside a building.

For many homes, the primary approach is **soil suction or sub-slab depressurization**.

The system typically uses:

1.  A collection point beneath or through the foundation
2.  A vertical or otherwise appropriately routed vent pipe
3.  A fan that creates negative pressure beneath the building
4.  An exterior discharge point where the radon is released safely outdoors

Instead of allowing soil gas to migrate into the living space, the system draws it from beneath the building and exhausts it outside.

The EPA generally recommends techniques that prevent radon from entering the home, such as soil suction, rather than relying exclusively on sealing cracks.

## Why Sealing Cracks Alone Usually Isn't Enough
It may seem logical to solve a radon problem by sealing every visible foundation crack.

Sealing cracks and openings is often an important component of mitigation, but **EPA does not recommend sealing alone as the primary solution** because it has not been shown to reduce radon concentrations significantly or consistently by itself.

A mitigation system may therefore combine:

  - Foundation sealing
  - Soil suction
  - Sub-slab depressurization
  - Crawl-space mitigation
  - Proper venting
  - Sump-pit modifications
  - Other building-specific techniques

The appropriate configuration depends on the home's foundation and construction.

## Radon Mitigation for Crawl Spaces
Homes with crawl spaces may require a different approach from homes with full concrete basements.

A mitigation contractor may install a gas-permeable membrane over the crawl-space floor and use a suction system beneath the membrane to collect soil gas.

The objective is the same: reduce the movement of radon from the soil into occupied indoor areas.

Because crawl-space configurations vary significantly, homeowners should avoid assuming that a mitigation system designed for a basement will work identically in a crawl space.

## How Effective Are Radon Mitigation Systems?
Properly designed and installed mitigation systems can substantially reduce indoor radon.

The EPA states that some radon reduction systems can reduce radon concentrations by **up to 99%**, although actual results depend on the home, initial radon concentration, system design, and installation.

A contractor should not simply install a fan and declare the problem solved.

**Post-mitigation testing is essential.**

The home should be tested again after the system has been installed to determine whether radon concentrations have actually been reduced.

## What Does a Radon Mitigation System Cost?
The cost varies according to the home's size, foundation type, construction, radon concentration, and mitigation method.

The EPA does not give one universal installation price because the appropriate system differs from property to property. Its guidance recommends obtaining estimates from qualified mitigation contractors.

Factors that can increase project costs include:

  - Multiple foundation levels
  - Difficult access beneath slabs
  - Crawl-space construction
  - Multiple suction points
  - Extensive foundation sealing
  - Electrical work
  - Complex vent routing
  - Difficult exterior installation
  - Additional testing

When comparing estimates, homeowners should evaluate the **complete mitigation system**, not simply the quoted price of the fan.

## How to Choose a Radon Mitigation Contractor
Ask potential contractors:

  - Are you certified, licensed, or registered where required?
  - What mitigation method do you recommend?
  - Why is that method appropriate for this house?
  - Where will the suction point be located?
  - Where will the exhaust pipe terminate?
  - Is electrical work included?
  - Is post-installation testing included?
  - What warranty is provided?
  - What maintenance will the system require?
  - How will system operation be monitored?

The EPA recommends checking with the relevant state radon program and using qualified radon professionals.

A good contractor should be able to explain the proposed system in terms a homeowner can understand rather than simply providing a price.

## How to Maintain a Radon Mitigation System
Installing the system is not the end of the process.

Homeowners should periodically check the system's warning or monitoring device to make sure the fan and system are operating correctly.

EPA guidance notes that mitigation fans may last five years or longer and may eventually need replacement. It also recommends retesting the home at least every **two years** to confirm that radon levels remain low.

A basic maintenance routine includes:

  - Check the system indicator regularly.
  - Listen for unusual fan noises.
  - Inspect visible vent components.
  - Keep the exhaust outlet unobstructed.
  - Watch for changes after major foundation or remodeling work.
  - Retest indoor radon periodically.
  - Replace failed fans or components promptly.

## Radon and Home Buying
Radon testing is particularly important during a home purchase because mitigation can become a negotiation and budgeting issue.

If testing reveals elevated radon, buyers and sellers may need to determine:

  - Who will hire the mitigation contractor
  - Who will pay for the work
  - Whether installation occurs before closing
  - Whether post-mitigation testing is required
  - What documentation should be provided to the buyer

The EPA's home buyer and seller guidance recommends testing and notes that the cost of mitigation depends on how the home was constructed and the reduction method required.

## Radon-Resistant Construction for New Homes
New construction can incorporate radon-resistant features before the house is completed.

Depending on local requirements and building design, these may include:

  - Gas-permeable material beneath the slab
  - Plastic sheeting or a gas-retarder membrane
  - Sealed foundation openings
  - A dedicated radon vent pathway
  - Provisions for installing an active fan later

EPA guidance explains that passive radon-resistant construction can make future mitigation easier and less expensive, but the completed home should still be tested after occupancy.

## Radon Testing and Mitigation Checklist
| Step | Homeowner Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Test the lowest regularly occupied level |
| 2 | Follow the test device's placement instructions |
| 3 | Review the result in pCi/L |
| 4 | Consider mitigation at 2–4 pCi/L |
| 5 | Take action at 4 pCi/L or higher |
| 6 | Obtain estimates from qualified mitigators |
| 7 | Choose a system suited to the home's foundation |
| 8 | Retest after installation |
| 9 | Monitor the mitigation system |
| 10 | Retest periodically |

## The Bottom Line
Radon is one of the easier indoor-air problems to overlook because there is usually no visible warning. A home can look clean, dry, and well maintained while still having elevated radon concentrations.

The solution begins with **testing rather than guessing**. If levels are elevated, a properly designed mitigation system—often based on soil suction and active venting—can substantially reduce indoor radon. EPA data indicates that some systems can achieve reductions of up to 99%.

For homeowners, the most important steps are straightforward: test the property, understand the result, use a qualified mitigation contractor when needed, verify the system with follow-up testing, and continue monitoring it over time.
