Buying a home involves more than transferring money and signing a deed. Before ownership changes hands, the property's title must be examined for potential problems that could affect the buyer's or lender's legal interests.

**Title insurance** provides protection against certain covered title defects and claims that existed before the policy was issued. In a typical financed home purchase, there are two distinct policies to understand: **lender's title insurance** and **owner's title insurance**.

The difference is important because the two policies protect different parties.

A lender's policy generally protects the mortgage lender's interest in the property and is usually required as a condition of obtaining a mortgage. An owner's policy protects the homeowner's financial interest and is generally optional.

Understanding what each policy covers, who pays for it, and what protection remains after the mortgage is paid off can help buyers evaluate their closing costs more effectively.

## What Is Title Insurance?
Title insurance protects against certain financial losses or legal problems arising from covered defects in a property's title.

A property's title represents the legal rights associated with ownership. Before closing, a title company or other title professional generally searches public records and other available information to identify potential issues.

Possible title problems can include:

  - Unpaid property taxes
  - Previously recorded liens
  - Errors in public records
  - Undisclosed ownership interests
  - Claims by previous owners
  - Forged documents
  - Recording mistakes
  - Certain undisclosed easements
  - Contractor claims involving unpaid work
  - Fraud or impersonation affecting prior transactions

The exact risks covered depend on the policy, exclusions, exceptions, and applicable state law.

Title insurance is different from homeowners insurance. Homeowners insurance generally protects against covered future property-related risks such as certain damage to the home, while title insurance addresses certain covered title problems originating from before the policy date.

## What Is Lender's Title Insurance?
**Lender's title insurance** protects the mortgage lender against covered title problems that could affect its security interest in the property.

For example, suppose you purchase a home with a $400,000 mortgage. After closing, someone makes a legitimate claim that they have a previously undisclosed legal interest in the property.

If the claim is covered under the lender's title policy, the policy protects the lender's insured interest according to the policy terms.

The important point is that the lender's policy protects **the lender**, not the homeowner's equity.

The CFPB specifically states that lender's title insurance protects the lender against title problems and does not protect the homeowner's investment in the property.

## Is Lender's Title Insurance Required?
When you finance a home with a mortgage, the lender will generally require a lender's title insurance policy.

The purpose is to protect the lender's collateral.

If a title problem threatens the lender's lien or ownership-related rights, the policy can provide protection according to its terms.

The cost of the lender's policy is generally included among the title-related closing costs associated with obtaining the mortgage.

The exact requirements and closing practices can vary by state and lender.

## What Is Owner's Title Insurance?
**Owner's title insurance** protects the homeowner's financial interest in the property against covered title claims.

Suppose you purchase a home for $500,000 and make a $100,000 down payment.

Your mortgage lender may have a lender's title policy protecting its loan interest. But that policy does not insure your $100,000 equity or other financial interest in the property.

An owner's policy can provide protection for the homeowner's covered interest under the policy's terms.

The CFPB explains that owner's title insurance can protect the homeowner if someone later claims an interest in the home based on circumstances that existed before the homeowner purchased it.

## Is Owner's Title Insurance Required?
Owner's title insurance is generally optional from the mortgage lender's perspective.

The lender can require its own lender's policy while leaving the decision to purchase an owner's policy to the buyer.

That distinction can sometimes create confusion at closing.

A buyer may see a title insurance charge on the closing documents and assume that the entire amount is required by the lender. In reality, the closing statement can contain separate charges for lender's and owner's policies.

The CFPB identifies owner's title insurance as an optional product for consumers, while lender's title insurance is generally required for a mortgage.

State rules and transaction-specific arrangements can affect how the costs are presented and who typically pays them.

## Owner's vs. Lender's Title Insurance
The simplest distinction is **who is protected**.

|  |  |  |
| :-: | :-: | :-: |
| **Feature** | **Owner's Policy** | **Lender's Policy** |
| Protected party | Homeowner | Mortgage lender |
| Usually required for mortgage? | Generally no | Generally yes |
| Protects homeowner's equity | Yes, subject to policy terms | No |
| Protects lender's loan | No | Yes |
| Premium | Typically paid once at closing | Typically paid once at closing |
| Coverage | Depends on policy | Depends on policy |
| Remains relevant after mortgage payoff | Generally yes while policy conditions apply | No lender interest remains after payoff |

The two policies can be issued by the same title company, but they serve different purposes.

## What Does Owner's Title Insurance Cover?
Coverage varies by policy and state, but an owner's policy can address certain covered claims involving the property's prior ownership history.

Examples can include:

  - Forgery in an earlier deed
  - Undisclosed heirs claiming ownership
  - Certain errors in public records
  - Certain unpaid taxes or liens
  - Undisclosed claims against the property
  - Certain fraud involving previous transfers
  - Certain defects in a previous owner's title

The exact coverage depends on the policy language.

A buyer should therefore read the policy's covered risks, exclusions, exceptions, and conditions rather than assuming that every possible title problem is insured.

## What Does Lender's Title Insurance Cover?
The lender's policy focuses on the lender's security interest.

If a covered title defect threatens the lender's lien or other insured interest, the policy can provide protection according to its terms.

The amount of lender's coverage is generally tied to the mortgage loan rather than the homeowner's full investment in the property.

As the mortgage is paid down, the lender's outstanding interest changes.

This illustrates why lender's and owner's policies are fundamentally different: one protects the financing interest, while the other protects the homeowner's property interest.

## Why Does a Title Search Matter?
Title insurance is generally issued after a title search or examination designed to identify potential problems.

The title professional may examine records relating to:

  - Prior deeds
  - Mortgages
  - Liens
  - Property taxes
  - Judgments
  - Easements
  - Ownership transfers
  - Other recorded interests

The goal is to identify and resolve title problems before closing whenever possible.

Title professionals may also perform other work to clear or "cure" defects before the transaction closes.

Title insurance therefore does not mean that the title company simply ignores potential problems and relies on insurance after closing. The title process is also intended to identify and resolve issues before ownership transfers.

## What Is a Title Defect?
A **title defect** is a problem that can affect the legal ownership or transferability of property.

For example, imagine that a previous owner transferred the property but the deed contained an error.

Or suppose an earlier owner failed to satisfy a lien that remains attached to the property.

Another possibility is that someone claims an ownership interest that was not discovered during the purchase process.

The legal consequences depend on the specific circumstances.

A title insurance policy can provide protection against certain covered defects, but exclusions and exceptions can limit coverage.

## What Are Exceptions to Title Insurance?
A title insurance policy does not necessarily cover every issue associated with a property.

The policy will identify **exceptions** and **exclusions**.

An exception is a specific matter that the policy does not insure against.

For example, a recorded easement may appear in the title commitment and be excluded from coverage because it was identified before the policy was issued.

Other exclusions can apply based on the policy form and circumstances.

This is why reviewing the title commitment and final policy matters.

## How Much Does Title Insurance Cost?
Title insurance costs vary based on factors such as:

  - Property purchase price
  - Loan amount
  - State regulations
  - Title company
  - Policy type
  - Coverage level
  - Optional endorsements
  - Local pricing practices

Unlike many other insurance products, title insurance generally involves a **one-time premium paid in connection with the transaction**, rather than a recurring monthly premium.

The CFPB notes that title services are part of mortgage closing costs and can include the title search, lender's title insurance premium, and other services associated with issuing the policy.

Because state laws and local practices differ, buyers should compare the total title-related charges rather than assuming there is one nationwide price.

## Who Pays for Title Insurance?
Who pays for owner's and lender's title insurance can vary by location and contract.

Depending on the state and transaction, the buyer, seller, or another party may pay some or all title-related costs.

The CFPB notes that title insurance and other closing services can vary by state, and buyers may be able to shop for certain providers.

Your purchase contract should specify which party is responsible for particular closing costs.

If the contract is unclear, ask the real estate agent, closing agent, or attorney to explain the allocation before signing.

## Can You Shop for Title Insurance?
In many transactions, yes.

The CFPB explains that title services are among the closing services consumers can often shop for. Lenders generally provide a list of available providers, although the exact services that can be shopped for vary by lender and location.

When comparing providers, ask for a complete quote that identifies:

  - Owner's policy premium
  - Lender's policy premium
  - Title search
  - Settlement or closing fee
  - Recording-related charges
  - Endorsements
  - Other title-related services

Comparing only the insurance premium can be misleading if another provider has substantially different settlement or title-service charges.

## Can One Company Issue Both Policies?
Yes.

A title company can often issue both the owner's and lender's policies for the same transaction.

The CFPB notes that when a buyer purchases owner's title insurance, using the same provider for both policies can result in a lower combined cost than purchasing the policies separately.

This does not mean buyers should automatically choose the first provider recommended by a lender or real estate agent.

Compare the total cost and services available from the providers you are permitted to select.

## What Happens to the Owner's Policy When You Sell?
An owner's title insurance policy is generally associated with the insured owner's interest in that particular property.

If you sell the home, your ownership interest ends.

The policy's terms determine the duration and scope of coverage, but owner's policies can generally provide protection for the homeowner for as long as the insured ownership interest continues, subject to the policy terms.

A new buyer would normally obtain their own title insurance coverage as part of their transaction.

## What Happens to Lender's Title Insurance When You Pay Off the Mortgage?
The lender's policy protects the lender's interest in its mortgage loan.

When that mortgage is fully paid off, the lender no longer has the same insured loan interest in the property.

If you later refinance with a new lender, the new lender will generally have its own title requirements and may require a new lender's policy or another acceptable form of title protection.

Your existing owner's policy, however, can remain relevant to your ownership interest subject to its terms.

## Is Owner's Title Insurance Worth Considering?
The decision depends on the buyer's circumstances, risk tolerance, transaction costs, and the coverage offered.

The key distinction is straightforward:

**Lender's title insurance protects the lender. Owner's title insurance protects the homeowner's covered financial interest.**

A buyer who declines an owner's policy is not simply declining another form of lender protection. They are choosing not to purchase that particular form of protection for their own property interest.

The CFPB describes owner's title insurance as a way for consumers to protect their financial investment in the home from covered title issues.

## Questions to Ask Before Closing
Before purchasing a home, ask the title or closing professional:

1.  Is lender's title insurance required for my mortgage?
2.  Is owner's title insurance optional in this transaction?
3.  Who is paying each policy premium?
4.  What does the owner's policy cover?
5.  What exclusions and exceptions apply?
6.  Are enhanced coverage options available?
7.  Can I choose my own title insurance provider?
8.  What is the total cost of title services?
9.  Are there discounts for purchasing both policies together?
10. Are there state-specific requirements affecting title insurance?
11. What title issues were discovered during the title search?
12. Which issues have been resolved before closing?

Getting these questions answered before signing can make the title-related portion of your closing costs easier to understand.

## Title Insurance and the Mortgage Closing
Title insurance is only one part of the closing process.

A mortgage closing can also involve:

  - Loan origination charges
  - Appraisal
  - Credit report
  - Homeowners insurance
  - Property taxes
  - Recording fees
  - Prepaid interest
  - Escrow deposits
  - Settlement services

The CFPB recommends reviewing your Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure and identifying which closing services you can shop for.

Your Closing Disclosure should be reviewed carefully before closing so you understand the final amounts being charged.

## Final Considerations
Owner's and lender's title insurance are often discussed together, but they protect different interests.

**Lender's title insurance** generally protects the mortgage lender against covered title problems affecting its loan and is usually required when obtaining a mortgage. **Owner's title insurance** protects the homeowner's covered financial interest in the property and is generally optional from the lender's perspective.

Both policies are generally purchased in connection with the closing rather than through ongoing monthly premiums. The cost and responsibility for paying the policies can vary by state, transaction, and purchase contract.

When comparing title services, look at the complete cost rather than only the insurance premium. The CFPB notes that consumers can often shop for title services and may be able to save money by comparing providers.

Most importantly, do not assume that a lender's title policy protects your personal equity. It is designed to protect the lender's interest. If you want protection for your own financial interest in the property, an owner's policy is the separate form of title insurance to evaluate.
