A major storm can turn a small roof defect into an active leak within hours. High winds can lift shingles, hail can puncture roofing materials, falling branches can damage the roof deck, and heavy rain can expose weak flashing or existing deterioration.

The first priority after storm damage is **safety and temporary protection—not immediately attempting a permanent repair**. Roofing work involves significant fall hazards, and a storm-damaged roof may have compromised decking or unstable materials. OSHA specifically warns that workers repairing roofs need appropriate fall protection and that damaged roof structures should be evaluated before work begins.

For landlords and homeowners, the most effective process is to document the damage, prevent additional water intrusion, arrange a professional inspection, and then complete the permanent repair using materials appropriate for the roofing system.

## 1. Inspect the Property From the Ground First
Do not immediately climb onto the roof after a major storm.

Start from the ground and look for:

  - Missing shingles
  - Lifted or curled shingles
  - Exposed underlayment
  - Damaged flashing
  - Missing ridge caps
  - Dented metal roofing
  - Broken roof tiles
  - Fallen branches
  - Sagging roof sections
  - Damaged gutters
  - Visible holes
  - Water entering the attic or living space

Also inspect the attic if it can be accessed safely.

Look for:

  - Wet insulation
  - Water stains
  - Damp roof decking
  - Dripping water
  - Mold or discoloration
  - Daylight visible through the roof
  - Damaged rafters or framing

A visible ceiling stain does not necessarily indicate that the roof penetration is directly above it. Water can travel along decking, insulation, framing, and other building components before appearing inside.

## 2. Treat Structural Damage as an Emergency
Some storm damage requires more than a roofing contractor.

If the roof is sagging, the decking appears unstable, structural members are damaged, or a large section of the roof has collapsed, keep occupants away from the affected area and arrange an appropriate professional assessment.

OSHA's roof-repair guidance specifically emphasizes evaluating structural integrity before workers begin repairs.

Do not walk across a roof simply because the surface appears intact. Water-damaged decking can lose strength, while loose shingles, wet surfaces, and debris can create serious fall hazards.

## 3. Document the Damage Before Making Repairs
If the property is insured, photograph and document the damage before removing damaged materials whenever doing so is safe.

Take photographs of:

  - Entire roof elevations
  - Individual damaged areas
  - Missing or displaced shingles
  - Damaged flashing
  - Gutters and downspouts
  - Fallen trees or branches
  - Interior water damage
  - Attic damage
  - Damaged personal property
  - Temporary repairs

Take wide photographs that establish where the damage occurred as well as close-up photographs showing the specific problem.

Keep copies of contractor estimates, invoices, receipts, inspection reports, and communications with the insurer.

The Insurance Information Institute recommends contacting the insurer promptly, determining whether the damage is covered, checking applicable deadlines, and keeping receipts for reasonable temporary repairs.

## 4. Stop Additional Water From Entering
Once it is safe to do so, the immediate objective is to prevent additional water damage.

Temporary measures can include:

  - Covering exposed areas with an appropriate tarp
  - Securing temporary protective materials
  - Placing containers beneath active interior leaks
  - Moving vulnerable belongings away from water
  - Protecting damaged ceilings and floors
  - Arranging emergency water extraction when necessary

A tarp is a **temporary weather barrier**, not a permanent roofing solution.

FEMA's guidance on its temporary roof program similarly describes plastic coverings and tarps as short-term protection intended to prevent additional damage until permanent repairs can be made.

## 5. Do Not Tarp a Roof Yourself if the Conditions Are Unsafe
A common mistake after storms is sending an inexperienced person onto the roof with a tarp.

Wet roofing materials can be extremely slippery. High winds can make large sheets of plastic difficult to control, and damaged decking may not safely support a person's weight.

OSHA's roofing guidance says that adverse weather conditions such as high winds, rain, snow, or sleet can create hazardous conditions requiring roofing operations to be suspended.

If the roof cannot be safely accessed, have a qualified roofing or emergency-restoration contractor install temporary protection.

## 6. Contact the Insurance Company Promptly
If the damage may be covered by insurance, notify the insurer or agent as soon as practical.

Ask:

  - Is storm damage covered?
  - What is the deductible?
  - Is emergency mitigation covered?
  - Does the policy require specific documentation?
  - Is a roof inspection required?
  - Can repairs begin before an adjuster visits?
  - Are there deadlines for submitting the claim?
  - Are temporary repairs reimbursable?

The Insurance Information Institute recommends contacting the insurer immediately after a disaster and keeping receipts for reasonable temporary repairs.

Do not assume that every roof problem after a storm is covered. Coverage depends on the policy, cause of loss, exclusions, deductibles, and the specific circumstances.

## 7. Have the Roof Professionally Inspected
Once immediate water intrusion is controlled, arrange a detailed inspection.

The contractor should evaluate:

### Roofing material
Check for:

  - Missing shingles
  - Cracked shingles
  - Granule loss
  - Wind uplift
  - Hail impacts
  - Broken tiles
  - Damaged metal panels

### Flashing
Inspect areas around:

  - Chimneys
  - Skylights
  - Roof valleys
  - Vents
  - Plumbing penetrations
  - Wall intersections

### Underlayment
The underlayment can be damaged even when the visible roof covering looks relatively intact.

### Roof deck
Look for:

  - Soft areas
  - Rot
  - Holes
  - Water damage
  - Structural deterioration

### Drainage
Check:

  - Gutters
  - Downspouts
  - Valley drainage
  - Roof drains
  - Flashing around drainage components

A professional inspection should determine whether the problem can be repaired locally or whether a larger section requires replacement.

## 8. Repair Small Areas When the Roof Is Otherwise Sound
A localized repair may be appropriate when storm damage is limited and the surrounding roofing system remains serviceable.

For asphalt shingles, a contractor may replace:

  - Individual missing shingles
  - Damaged shingles
  - Ridge caps
  - Certain flashing components

The repair should restore the roof's weather-resistant system rather than simply covering the visible hole.

For example, applying roofing cement over a damaged shingle may provide a short-term patch but does not necessarily replace the underlying damaged material.

The repair should address the actual failure point.

## 9. Replace Damaged Flashing and Seal Penetrations
Flashing is designed to direct water away from vulnerable roof transitions and penetrations.

Common problem areas include:

  - Chimneys
  - Skylights
  - Plumbing vents
  - Roof-to-wall intersections
  - Valleys
  - Dormers

After a storm, inspect flashing for displacement, cracks, corrosion, or separation.

Sealants can be useful as part of a properly designed repair, but **sealant should not be treated as a universal substitute for damaged flashing**.

A roof that relies on large quantities of caulk to remain watertight may have an underlying installation or material problem.

## 10. Address Roof-Deck Damage Before Installing New Roofing
If water entered through a damaged section and the roof deck is compromised, the underlying material may need to be repaired or replaced.

Installing new shingles over structurally damaged decking can conceal the problem rather than solve it.

OSHA's roofing guidance specifically calls for damaged portions of roof decking to be repaired and unsafe openings to be covered or guarded during roofing work.

For significant structural damage, a qualified contractor should determine the appropriate repair method.

## 11. Know When a Roof Needs Replacement
Storm damage does not automatically mean the entire roof needs to be replaced.

Replacement may become more appropriate when:

  - Damage is widespread
  - Large portions of the roof covering are missing
  - The roof deck is extensively damaged
  - The underlying system has reached the end of its service life
  - Repeated repairs are no longer economical
  - Storm damage exposes substantial pre-existing deterioration
  - The roofing system cannot be reliably restored

A professional should distinguish storm damage from ordinary aging and deferred maintenance.

This distinction can also matter for insurance claims.

## 12. Be Careful When Hiring Storm-Repair Contractors
Major storms can produce a sudden influx of contractors and repair companies.

Before signing a contract, verify:

  - Business identity
  - Licensing requirements
  - Insurance
  - References
  - Written estimate
  - Scope of work
  - Materials
  - Warranty terms
  - Payment schedule
  - Change-order procedures

Be cautious about contractors demanding a large upfront payment or pressuring you to sign immediately.

The Insurance Information Institute specifically warns consumers to be cautious about contractors who request large upfront payments and recommends retaining documentation related to temporary and permanent repairs.

For a large insurance claim, do not sign an assignment or other document transferring significant rights without understanding exactly what it does.

## 13. Protect Tenants During Roof Repairs
For rental properties, roof repairs create an additional responsibility: keeping tenants safe and minimizing property damage.

Before work begins:

  - Notify occupants as required
  - Establish contractor access
  - Keep occupants away from active work areas
  - Protect interior areas from falling debris
  - Coordinate emergency access
  - Address leaks promptly
  - Document tenant communications

OSHA's roof-repair guidance recommends controlling access and keeping occupants away from areas where roofing work is occurring overhead.

If a unit becomes temporarily uninhabitable, landlords should follow applicable state and local landlord-tenant requirements and review their insurance coverage.

## 14. Prevent the Next Storm Leak
Once the roof has been repaired, address vulnerabilities that could cause another failure.

Consider:

  - Replacing deteriorated flashing
  - Securing loose roofing components
  - Cleaning gutters
  - Improving drainage
  - Trimming hazardous branches
  - Inspecting roof penetrations
  - Replacing deteriorated sealants
  - Checking attic ventilation
  - Inspecting the roof annually

Preventive maintenance can reduce the probability that a minor roofing defect becomes a major interior water-loss event.

**Immediately after the storm:**

☐ Keep people away from unsafe areas
☐ Inspect the property from the ground
☐ Check the attic if safely accessible
☐ Photograph and document damage
☐ Contact the insurer if appropriate
☐ Prevent additional water intrusion
☐ Arrange professional temporary protection when needed

**Before permanent repairs:**

☐ Obtain a professional roof inspection
☐ Check the roof covering
☐ Inspect flashing and penetrations

☐ Verify contractor credentials and insurance

**After repairs:**

☐ Document completed work
☐ Keep invoices and warranties
☐ Confirm interior damage is addressed
☐ Inspect gutters and drainage

### Final Considerations
After a major storm, the priority should be **safety, documentation, temporary protection, and professional assessment**—in that order.

A tarp or emergency patch can prevent additional water damage, but it should be viewed as temporary protection rather than a permanent repair. FEMA describes temporary roof coverings as short-term measures intended to protect structures until permanent repairs can be completed.

Roof work also carries serious fall hazards. OSHA recommends appropriate fall-protection measures for residential roofing work and emphasizes evaluating roof integrity and suspending operations when weather creates unsafe conditions.

For landlords, storm response should also include tenant communication, insurance documentation, contractor management, and a record of the property's condition before and after the repair.

The goal is not simply to stop today's leak. A properly completed storm repair should restore the roof's weather resistance, address underlying damage, and reduce the likelihood that the next major storm produces another expensive failure.
