An emergency generator is only useful if it starts when the power goes out. A generator that has been sitting unused for months can develop problems with its battery, fuel system, oil, air filter, electrical connections, or starting system.

Regular maintenance is therefore more than routine upkeep. It is a way to reduce the chance of discovering a mechanical problem during an actual outage.

Generator maintenance requirements vary by model, so the owner's manual should always take priority over a generic schedule. CPSC also recommends regularly checking and maintaining generators and following the manufacturer's instructions and warnings.

## Portable vs. Standby Generator Maintenance
The first step is identifying what type of generator you have.

**Portable generators** are typically moved into position when electricity is unavailable. They commonly run on gasoline, although propane and dual-fuel models are also available.

**Standby generators** are permanently installed and connected to the home's electrical system through dedicated equipment. Many are designed to start automatically when utility power fails.

Both types require engine and electrical maintenance, but standby systems can also involve an automatic transfer switch, permanently installed fuel connections, battery charging equipment, and scheduled automatic exercise cycles.

## Generator Maintenance Checklist
A basic maintenance program should cover:

  - Engine oil
  - Oil filter where applicable
  - Air filter
  - Spark plug on applicable engines
  - Fuel system
  - Battery and terminals
  - Electrical connections
  - Cooling and ventilation
  - Exhaust system
  - Control panel
  - Generator enclosure
  - Transfer switch on standby systems
  - Output voltage and frequency
  - Automatic-start function
  - Carbon-monoxide safety features where equipped

The exact service intervals depend on the manufacturer, engine, fuel type, and operating hours.

## 1. Check the Engine Oil
Oil is one of the most important routine maintenance items on an engine-driven generator.

Before starting the generator, check the oil level according to the manufacturer's procedure. Running an engine with insufficient oil can cause severe internal damage.

Oil should also be replaced at the interval specified in the owner's manual. Heavy use during a prolonged outage may require more frequent service than a generator that only operates during scheduled tests.

Do not assume that unused oil remains suitable indefinitely. Follow the manufacturer's recommended oil type, viscosity, capacity, and replacement interval.

If the oil level repeatedly drops between services, investigate the cause rather than simply adding oil.

Possible explanations include:

  - An external oil leak
  - Excessive consumption
  - Incorrect oil level
  - Engine wear
  - A damaged seal or gasket

A significant leak should be evaluated before the generator is relied upon for emergency operation.

## 2. Inspect the Battery on Standby Generators
A standby generator can have a perfectly healthy engine and still fail to start because its starting battery is weak.

Inspect the battery and terminals for:

  - Corrosion
  - Loose connections
  - Physical damage
  - Swelling
  - Leakage
  - Poor cable connections

The battery should be maintained according to the generator manufacturer's instructions.

Some standby generators continuously maintain the starting battery with a charger. A battery can nevertheless deteriorate with age, particularly if the generator is exposed to extreme temperatures or has experienced charging problems.

If the generator cranks slowly or repeatedly fails to start, have the battery and charging system tested rather than assuming the engine itself is defective.

## 3. Check the Fuel System
Fuel-related problems are particularly common in generators that sit unused for long periods.

For gasoline-powered portable generators, stale fuel can contribute to starting problems and deposits in the fuel system.

Before storing a portable generator, follow the manufacturer's fuel-storage instructions. Depending on the model, this may involve using fresh fuel, an approved fuel stabilizer, draining fuel, or another manufacturer-specified procedure.

Inspect for:

  - Fuel leaks
  - Cracked fuel lines
  - Damaged fuel caps
  - Deteriorated hoses
  - Corrosion
  - Unusual fuel odors

Never operate a generator if fuel is leaking.

For natural-gas or propane standby generators, fuel-system servicing should be performed by appropriately qualified professionals when the work involves gas piping, regulators, or other components requiring specialized knowledge.

## 4. Inspect the Air Filter
A dirty air filter can restrict airflow and affect engine performance.

Check the filter according to the manufacturer's maintenance schedule. Some filters can be cleaned; others are designed to be replaced.

Do not automatically wash or blow out a filter unless the manufacturer specifically permits that procedure.

A generator that starts but runs poorly, loses power under load, or produces abnormal exhaust may have an air-intake problem, but those symptoms can also have other causes.

## 5. Inspect the Spark Plug
Gasoline-powered engines use spark plugs that gradually wear or become contaminated.

A maintenance inspection may include checking:

  - Electrode condition
  - Carbon deposits
  - Cracks
  - Correct gap
  - Installation condition

Replace the spark plug according to the manufacturer's service schedule and specification.

If a generator repeatedly fails to start despite having fuel and a charged battery, ignition components may need professional diagnosis.

## 6. Inspect Electrical Cords and Connections
Before using a portable generator, inspect the generator itself and all cords being used with it.

Look for:

  - Cut or damaged insulation
  - Bent plugs
  - Burn marks
  - Loose connections
  - Damaged receptacles
  - Overheated connectors

OSHA recommends visually inspecting portable generators and removing defective equipment from service. It also emphasizes proper grounding and bonding and warns against unsafe connections to structures.

Never use a damaged extension cord simply because the generator is needed during an emergency.

## 7. Never Backfeed a House
One of the most important electrical safety rules is **never connect a portable generator directly to a home's electrical system through an improvised connection**.

A properly installed transfer switch or other approved transfer equipment is required when connecting a generator to a building electrical system.

OSHA specifically warns against connecting a portable generator directly to a structure unless a properly installed transfer switch is used.

Backfeeding can energize electrical lines that utility workers believe are de-energized, creating a potentially fatal shock hazard.

If a portable generator is intended to power selected household circuits, have the transfer equipment installed by a qualified electrician.

## 8. Test the Generator Under Load
Starting the engine is only part of a generator test.

A generator can start normally but fail when appliances or building circuits are connected.

During scheduled maintenance, check whether the generator can deliver the expected electrical output without:

  - Excessive voltage fluctuation
  - Abnormal noises
  - Overheating
  - Excessive smoke
  - Repeated overload trips
  - Unexpected shutdowns

For standby systems, the automatic transfer function should also be tested according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Some homeowners should leave transfer-switch testing to the installer or generator service company because the procedure involves the home's electrical system.

## 9. Exercise a Standby Generator
Automatic standby generators are generally designed to run periodic exercise cycles.

These scheduled tests allow the engine and control system to operate even when there is no utility outage.

Exercise schedules differ by manufacturer and model. Some systems allow weekly or other recurring test schedules, while others offer different operating modes.

The purpose is not simply to keep the engine running. It can also help reveal problems with:

  - Battery charging
  - Starting
  - Fuel delivery
  - Engine operation
  - Control electronics
  - Automatic transfer equipment

If an exercise cycle repeatedly stops early or generates an error code, investigate the problem rather than repeatedly resetting the system.

## 10. Keep the Generator and Exhaust Area Clear
A generator needs adequate airflow and unobstructed exhaust.

Do not allow leaves, vegetation, snow, boxes, construction materials, or other objects to block ventilation or exhaust pathways.

For portable generators, CPSC currently recommends operating them **outside and at least 20 feet from the home**, with exhaust directed away from the house, windows, doors, and vents.

A garage, basement, shed, crawlspace, porch, or other partially enclosed location is not a safe substitute for outdoor operation.

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless and can become lethal rapidly.

## 11. Check Carbon Monoxide Alarms
Generator safety does not end with maintaining the machine.

CPSC recommends working carbon-monoxide alarms on each level of the home and outside sleeping areas, with battery backup where appropriate.

Test alarms according to the manufacturer's instructions and replace batteries or the alarm itself when required.

A CO alarm is an additional safety measure—not permission to operate a generator closer to the house or in an enclosed area.

## 12. Generator Maintenance After a Long Outage
A generator that operates continuously during a prolonged power outage may need maintenance sooner than normal.

Extended operation can increase wear on:

  - Engine oil
  - Air filters
  - Spark plugs
  - Belts
  - Cooling components
  - Battery systems
  - Fuel systems

Follow the manufacturer's operating-hour service intervals.

After a long outage, inspect the generator once it has cooled and check for:

  - Oil leaks
  - Fuel leaks
  - Overheating
  - Damaged wiring
  - Unusual noises
  - Excessive exhaust smoke
  - Loose components

Do not perform maintenance while the engine is hot unless the manufacturer specifically permits it.

## Common Generator Problems and What They May Mean
| Problem | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Won't start | Battery, fuel, ignition, controls, or engine problem |
| Starts and stops | Fuel delivery, overload, sensor, or control issue |
| Low electrical output | Engine speed, alternator, wiring, or control problem |
| Runs roughly | Fuel, air, ignition, or engine issue |
| Battery keeps dying | Battery, charger, wiring, or standby-system problem |
| Excessive smoke | Oil, fuel, engine, or load-related problem |
| Overheating | Blocked airflow, cooling problem, excessive load |
| Breaker trips | Overload, short circuit, or connected-equipment problem |
| Transfer switch fails | Control, wiring, utility, or transfer-equipment issue |

These symptoms are diagnostic starting points rather than definitive diagnoses.

## When to Call a Generator Repair Professional
Professional service is appropriate when the problem involves:

  - Persistent failure to start
  - Fuel leaks
  - Gas-system problems
  - Repeated overheating
  - Major oil leaks
  - Electrical faults
  - Alternator problems
  - Automatic transfer-switch issues
  - Repeated fault codes
  - Damaged wiring
  - Internal engine problems
  - Abnormal voltage or frequency
  - Generator connection to the home's electrical system

Do not repeatedly attempt to restart a generator that is producing unusual noises, smoke, fuel odors, or electrical faults.

Fuel-burning equipment should be serviced according to the manufacturer's instructions, and CPSC advises against servicing fuel-burning appliances without the appropriate knowledge, skill, and tools.

## Emergency Generator Maintenance Schedule
A practical schedule can look like this, subject to the owner's manual:

### Before storm season
  - Inspect the generator.
  - Check oil.
  - Inspect fuel.
  - Test the starting system.
  - Inspect cords and connections.
  - Check the battery on standby units.
  - Confirm CO alarms work.
  - Review operating instructions.
  - Verify safe generator placement.
  - Confirm transfer equipment is functioning where applicable.

### Monthly
  - Inspect the unit for damage or leaks.
  - Check battery condition on standby systems.
  - Verify the automatic exercise system where applicable.
  - Inspect ventilation and exhaust areas.
  - Check fuel-system components.

### At manufacturer-specified service intervals
  - Change engine oil.
  - Replace oil filters where applicable.
  - Inspect or replace air filters.
  - Inspect or replace spark plugs where applicable.
  - Inspect belts and hoses.
  - Check electrical output.
  - Service cooling components.
  - Perform professional inspection when required.

### After extended operation
  - Check oil level and condition.
  - Inspect for leaks.
  - Check for overheating or abnormal operation.
  - Inspect electrical connections.
  - Complete any required operating-hour maintenance.

## Final Considerations
Generator maintenance should happen **before the power goes out**, not when the household is already in darkness.

The most important routine tasks are checking oil, fuel, batteries, air filters, electrical connections, cooling and exhaust pathways, and the manufacturer's scheduled service requirements. Standby generators should also have their exercise cycles and automatic-transfer systems checked according to the manufacturer's procedures.

Safety is equally important. CPSC currently recommends operating portable generators outdoors at least **20 feet from the home**, with exhaust directed away from windows, doors, and vents. Never operate one inside a house, garage, basement, crawlspace, shed, or other enclosed space—even if doors or windows are open.

For generators connected to a home's electrical system, use properly installed transfer equipment rather than improvised connections. OSHA specifically warns against directly connecting a portable generator to a structure without an appropriate transfer switch.

A generator that starts during a maintenance test is not necessarily ready for a prolonged emergency. The goal is a complete system that can **start, run safely, produce the required power, transfer correctly where applicable, and continue operating for the duration of an outage**.
