Subleasing can be a legitimate way for a tenant to transfer occupancy rights to another person, but short-term rental arbitrage creates a substantially different risk profile.

In a traditional sublease, a tenant rents the property to another occupant while remaining responsible to the original landlord under the primary lease. In **Airbnb arbitrage**, a tenant typically leases a property from a landlord and then attempts to generate profit by offering the property to short-term guests for more than the tenant's monthly rent.

For example:

**Monthly lease:** $2,000
**Average short-term rental revenue:** $3,500
**Potential gross spread:** $1,500

That $1,500 is not profit. Cleaning, platform fees, utilities, supplies, taxes, insurance, maintenance, vacancies, and regulatory costs can substantially reduce the difference.

More importantly for landlords, the arrangement can introduce risks that were never contemplated when the original residential lease was signed.

## What Is Airbnb Arbitrage?
Airbnb arbitrage generally involves:

1.  A tenant signs a conventional residential lease.
2.  The tenant obtains permission—or sometimes fails to obtain permission—to sublease or host guests.
3.  The tenant furnishes the property.
4.  The tenant lists it on Airbnb or another short-term-rental platform.
5.  Guests pay nightly or weekly rates.
6.  The tenant keeps the difference after expenses.

The landlord continues to own the property, while the tenant effectively operates a short-term accommodation business from it.

Airbnb itself warns hosts that leases, contracts, building rules, mortgages, and local laws may restrict or prohibit subletting or short-term hosting.

## 1. Start With the Lease
The first question for a landlord is whether the existing lease permits the proposed activity.

Review provisions concerning:

  - Subleasing
  - Assignment
  - Guests
  - Occupancy
  - Business activities
  - Short-term rentals
  - Property use
  - Noise
  - Parking
  - Alterations
  - Insurance
  - HOA or condominium rules

A lease that permits an occasional guest does not necessarily authorize the tenant to operate a short-term rental business.

If the landlord is willing to permit Airbnb activity, the safest approach is usually to document the arrangement in a specific written addendum rather than relying on an informal conversation.

Airbnb itself recommends reviewing the lease and obtaining the necessary permissions before hosting, and notes that a lease or contract can be amended to establish responsibilities and liabilities.

## 2. Require Written Landlord Approval
If a tenant wants to use the property for short-term rentals, require written approval before allowing the activity.

The agreement should identify:

  - Whether short-term rentals are permitted
  - Approved platforms
  - Maximum occupancy
  - Minimum stay
  - Maximum number of guests
  - Permitted hosting dates
  - Noise and nuisance standards
  - Cleaning requirements
  - Parking rules
  - Guest screening requirements
  - Damage responsibility
  - Insurance requirements
  - Regulatory responsibilities
  - Tax responsibilities
  - Termination rights

Do not rely on a vague provision such as "subletting is allowed."

Short-term guests can create a completely different operational environment from a single long-term occupant.

## 3. Verify Local Short-Term-Rental Regulations
Landlord approval does not automatically make Airbnb activity legal.

Cities and counties can regulate short-term rentals through:

  - Zoning
  - Registration
  - Business licenses
  - Short-term-rental permits
  - Occupancy limits
  - Safety requirements
  - Primary-residence requirements
  - Local taxes
  - Parking restrictions
  - Noise regulations
  - Building-specific restrictions

Airbnb's current terms specifically state that hosts are responsible for understanding applicable laws and that some jurisdictions restrict or prohibit short-term rentals or require registration, permits, or licenses.

Before approving arbitrage, the landlord should verify the property's actual eligibility with the relevant local authority.

## 4. Check HOA and Condominium Rules
A landlord may own the unit but still be restricted by an HOA, condominium association, cooperative, or other governing documents.

These rules can address:

  - Minimum lease periods
  - Guest registration
  - Short-term rentals
  - Building access
  - Noise
  - Parking
  - Number of occupants
  - Commercial activity

A tenant cannot generally grant themselves rights that the property owner does not possess.

If the building prohibits short-term rentals, a private agreement between landlord and tenant may not solve the problem.

Airbnb specifically advises hosts to check HOA, co-op, condominium, building, and community rules before hosting.

## 5. Review the Mortgage and Insurance
The landlord should also check financing and insurance restrictions.

Some loan documents can restrict certain uses of a property, while insurance policies can distinguish between ordinary residential tenancy and short-term lodging activity.

Airbnb advises hosts to check mortgage restrictions and obtain appropriate insurance because standard arrangements may not necessarily cover every hosting activity or associated risk.

Do not assume that platform-provided protection replaces the landlord's own insurance.

Ask the insurer specifically whether the policy covers:

  - Short-term guests
  - Guest-caused damage
  - Liability claims
  - Theft
  - Water damage
  - Commercial activity
  - Loss of rental income
  - Vacant periods

The answer should be documented before approving the arrangement.

## 6. Understand Who Is Responsible for Guests
One of the biggest concerns with arbitrage is that the landlord may have no direct relationship with the people staying in the property.

A traditional tenant may have passed the landlord's screening process. Their Airbnb guests generally have not been screened by the landlord in the same way.

The landlord's agreement with the tenant should therefore establish who is responsible for:

  - Guest conduct
  - Property damage
  - Noise complaints
  - Unauthorized occupants
  - Emergency calls
  - Cleaning
  - Fines
  - Neighbor complaints
  - Regulatory violations

The tenant should remain responsible for complying with the primary lease and any additional written conditions.

## 7. Establish Occupancy and Guest Rules
A landlord who permits arbitrage should set clear limits.

For example:

**Maximum overnight occupancy:** 4
**Maximum stay:** 14 nights
**Parties/events:** prohibited
**Smoking:** prohibited
**Unauthorized guests:** prohibited
**Parking:** two designated spaces

These rules should align with applicable law and building regulations.

Avoid creating rules based on protected characteristics. The objective should be to regulate legitimate property-use issues such as occupancy, noise, safety, and damage.

## 8. Decide How the Landlord Will Monitor the Arrangement
Monitoring does not necessarily mean interfering with every guest.

Instead, establish measurable requirements.

The tenant could be required to provide:

  - Proof of required permits
  - Proof of insurance
  - Platform listing information
  - Emergency contact information
  - Periodic compliance certifications
  - Copies of regulatory notices
  - Evidence of tax registration where applicable

The lease addendum can also give the landlord appropriate inspection rights consistent with applicable landlord-tenant law.

The goal is to identify violations early rather than discovering months later that the property has effectively become an unauthorized hotel.

## 9. Understand the Economics
Landlords should evaluate whether allowing arbitrage actually benefits the property.

Suppose the tenant pays:

**$2,000 monthly rent**

and generates:

**$4,000 gross short-term bookings**

The tenant's expenses might include:

|  |  |
| :-: | :-: |
| \*\*Expense\*\* | \*\*Monthly Cost\*\* |
| Rent | $2,000 |
| Platform/payment fees | $400 |
| Cleaning | $400 |
| Utilities | $250 |
| Supplies | $100 |
| Insurance | $100 |
| Maintenance | $150 |
| \*\*Total\*\* | \*\*$3,400\*\* |

The tenant's estimated operating spread would be:

**$4,000 − $3,400 = $600**

And that is before accounting for taxes, vacancy, unusual repairs, furnishings, or the tenant's own labor.

This illustrates why landlords should not assume that a tenant earning higher gross revenue automatically means the landlord should charge substantially more rent.

## 10. Consider a Revenue-Sharing Arrangement
If the landlord is willing to permit short-term rentals, there are alternatives to simply charging fixed monthly rent.

Possible structures include:

### Fixed rent
The tenant pays an agreed monthly amount regardless of short-term rental revenue.

**Advantage:** predictable landlord income.

### Fixed rent plus additional fee
The tenant pays base rent plus an agreed fee for the permitted short-term-rental use.

**Advantage:** gives the landlord additional compensation for increased risk and wear.

### Revenue sharing
The landlord receives an agreed percentage of qualifying short-term-rental revenue.

**Advantage:** landlord participates in upside.

Each structure creates different accounting, contractual, and tax considerations. The agreement should define exactly how revenue is calculated and what expenses, if any, can be deducted.

## 11. Address Cleaning and Property Condition
Short-term guests can increase turnover dramatically.

The agreement should specify who handles:

  - Cleaning
  - Linen replacement
  - Trash removal
  - Restocking
  - Minor repairs
  - Damage claims
  - Preventive maintenance

The landlord should also consider whether the property's condition is suitable for frequent guest turnover.

A furnished short-term rental may experience substantially more wear than a conventional long-term tenancy.

## 12. Handle Fees and Guest Pricing Transparently
Short-term rental operators should be particularly careful about pricing disclosures.

The FTC's rule governing covered short-term lodging requires mandatory fees to be disclosed upfront in the total price, subject to the rule's specific provisions and exceptions. The FTC explicitly identifies vacation rentals and Airbnb-type lodging as examples of covered short-term lodging.

This is distinct from the broader rental-housing fee issues the FTC is currently examining through its 2026 proposed rulemaking.

Landlords permitting arbitrage should therefore understand which party is responsible for compliant pricing and disclosures.

## 13. Protect Against Unauthorized Airbnb Listings
If the lease prohibits subleasing or short-term rentals, landlords can establish a monitoring process.

Possible indicators include:

  - Neighbors reporting frequent unfamiliar guests
  - Repeated short-term occupancy
  - Key or access-code activity inconsistent with the tenant's occupancy
  - Online listings showing the property
  - Increased cleaning activity
  - Frequent luggage or guest turnover

However, landlords should follow lawful inspection and privacy procedures rather than entering the property without proper notice or authorization.

If unauthorized hosting is discovered, document the evidence and follow the lease and applicable landlord-tenant law.

## 14. Include a Clear Exit Strategy
A short-term-rental addendum should explain what happens if the tenant loses permission to host.

Potential triggers could include:

  - Permit cancellation
  - Insurance cancellation
  - HOA prohibition
  - Regulatory violation
  - Repeated guest complaints
  - Property damage
  - Unauthorized listings
  - Material lease violations

The agreement should explain whether hosting privileges can be suspended or terminated and how that interacts with the underlying lease.

Do not assume that terminating Airbnb permission automatically terminates the entire tenancy. The legal consequences depend on the lease and applicable law.

### Final Considerations
Subleasing and Airbnb arbitrage are not necessarily the same thing from a landlord's risk perspective.

A conventional sublease generally involves another occupant taking over some or all of the tenant's lease rights. Airbnb arbitrage can transform the property into a short-term lodging operation with frequent guest turnover, additional wear, new insurance considerations, and potentially significant regulatory requirements.

For landlords, the safest approach is to make the decision **before hosting begins**. Review the lease, confirm local regulations, check building and mortgage restrictions, verify insurance, and document any permission in a detailed written agreement.

Airbnb itself emphasizes that hosts are responsible for complying with applicable contracts, laws, regulations, and insurance requirements, and that some jurisdictions prohibit or restrict short-term hosting altogether.

If arbitrage is permitted, treat it as a separate operating model rather than simply allowing "subletting." Establish clear limits, assign responsibility for guests and damage, monitor compliance appropriately, and create a process for addressing violations.

For properties where local law, HOA rules, financing documents, or insurance coverage are unclear, obtaining legal and insurance advice before approving short-term rental activity can prevent a potentially profitable arrangement from becoming a costly liability.
